# FROZEN SCORING RUBRIC (pre-registered before any Stage 1 review)

Scores are integers 1-10 on each of the FOUR official criteria. Every score carries:
rationale, evidence_surfaces, confidence (0-1). Reviewers use the full scale and these
band anchors verbatim. Category neutrality is absolute: a game, art piece, music tool,
CRM, weird toy, and developer tool are all eligible for 10/10 on every criterion if they
satisfy that criterion exceptionally well FOR WHAT THEY ARE TRYING TO BE.
No aesthetic penalties. Signs of hurried AI work matter only as actual product-quality
problems (incoherence, broken flow, placeholder content), never as style.

## WebMCP Leverage — what does WebMCP change for this product?
Judge the delta WebMCP makes to reliability, precision, structured state access,
shared human-agent state, repeatability, and capability. Tool COUNT is not leverage.
- 9-10 TRANSFORMATIVE: the intended experience is impractical without the structured
  agent surface; human and agent share live product state; the collaboration could not
  be replicated comparably well by a general agent driving the UI.
- 7-8 MAJOR DELTA: without WebMCP a general agent is markedly less reliable/precise
  here; the agent is central to the product, not a bolt-on.
- 5-6 MEANINGFUL DELTA: existing capability packaged into a genuinely better product
  experience via structured tools; same outcome otherwise possible with real friction.
- 3-4 MINOR DELTA: generic browser automation achieves comparable outcomes; WebMCP is
  a convenience layer.
- 1-2 COSMETIC: exposes an action a UI button already offers; no meaningful change.

## Execution — is the actual product coherent, intentional, complete for its scope?
Stage 1 scores EXECUTION_CLAIMED: coherence/intentionality/completeness as evidenced by
the packet (description + packaging + video frames/transcript). Thin evidence caps the
score (end-to-end credit requires end-to-end evidence) and lowers confidence.
Stage 2 may rescore as EXECUTION_OBSERVED; latest evidence-backed score replaces, never
sums.
- 9-10: all surfaces agree; central workflow demonstrably completes; states handled;
  feels finished for its stated scope.
- 7-8: strong coherence and intentionality; central workflow appears functional; minor
  unfinished edges.
- 5-6: plausibly real with visible gaps; scope unclear or core not demonstrated
  end-to-end.
- 3-4: thin prototype; contradictions between surfaces; placeholder content.
- 1-2: non-functional or incoherent for its claimed scope.

## Creativity & Ambition — novelty of concept and interaction model
Judge ONLY: novelty of the concept, novelty of the interaction model, originality
relative to existing concepts, ambition, memorable/surprising use of the medium, and
the depth with which the concept is pursued. WebMCP indispensability belongs under
WebMCP Leverage, NOT here: a project can be extremely creative even if another agent
API could theoretically have enabled something similar.
- 9-10: genuinely novel concept or interaction model; ambitious scope pursued deeply
  and coherently; memorable.
- 7-8: fresh synthesis or notably original interaction; ambitious for a hackathon.
- 5-6: competent take on a known category with a real new angle.
- 3-4: derivative; novelty mostly surface details.
- 1-2: clone/template with trivial changes.
"That the category exists" is never a penalty; judge the submitted concept.

## Potential Impact — credible value for the intended audience
Judge: is there a real audience; is there a real problem, desire, or use case for that
audience; does the demonstrated solution genuinely address it. NO commercial adoption,
monetization, market pull, or startup viability is required. Entertainment, play, art,
creative expression, learning, niche hobby use, experimentation, and community value
are legitimate forms of impact. A tiny audience fully justifies a top score.
- 9-10: specific real audience with a real problem/desire; solution demonstrably
  addresses it; credible value, appeal, usefulness, or meaning for that audience.
- 7-8: clear audience and problem, good fit, credible value.
- 5-6: identifiable audience, looser fit or partial address.
- 3-4: speculative audience or strained fit; impact mostly aspirational.
- 1-2: no coherent audience/problem pairing.
Audience size is NOT a multiplier.

## Aggregate & ranking
aggregate = leverage + execution + impact + creativity (4-40). Nothing else enters.
Tie-breaks: Leverage, then Execution, then Impact, then Creativity, then higher
combined evidence confidence, then slug alphabetical. See PROTOCOL.md Ranking section.

How we built it colorwake is a static React 19 and TypeScript SPA built with Vite. It has no app backend or accounts; artwork and state persist in localStorage. Characters are rigged SVGs across five rig families, animated with the Web Animations API. Motion parameters are clamped to safe ranges for both whole-character and part-targeted moves. The WebMCP layer registers 10 tools through document.modelContext.registerTool , typed with webmcp-types and validated with Zod: four read tools and six write tools. Three decisions mattered most: get_guide — an in-page playbook, delivered like a system prompt. A read-only tool can return more than app state. It can carry guidance: how the studio works, what the child can do in each state, and the house rules. An agent that reads it starts with a clear role as a playmate. A loop designed around WebMCP. On-screen button presses are not announced to the agent. Successful write-tool results provide current, action-relevant context, while get_studio_state lets the agent resync after manual interactions. The experience works best when the family continues through the protocol. Parity, with one boundary the agent cannot cross. UI-equivalent tools call the same actions as the on-screen controls, while motion tools drive the same play engine. The 20-picture and 8-step caps return actionable errors; the three-spot stage predictably swaps out the friend that has been there longest. There is deliberately no painting tool — coloring belongs to the child. Challenges I ran into This was my first hackathon, and I came to it with a product background rather than a traditional engineering one. The hardest part was translation : turning WebMCP’s technical properties into moments a family could actually feel. get_guide emerged from asking what happens when young children cannot type commands and turning that constraint into a feature. AI coding agents were my hands, but direction, taste, and QA were mine: round after round of on-device review, dozens of UI/UX fixes, and a 148-test verification chain. One technical lesson stood out: tool descriptions are UX writing. Agents behave only as well as the schemas and errors guiding them. Accomplishments that I'm proud of The promise works end to end: a picture colored a minute ago can walk across the stage beneath the weather the child chose. The page teaches the agent: get_guide gives it a playbook for becoming a good playmate. I shipped it solo for my first hackathon, with a local-first privacy model designed for families. What I learned Read-only tools can carry guidance — context, norms, and a role — not just app state. Designing for agents is UX design made of words: schemas, descriptions, and error messages are the interface. What's next for colorwake More sketches and motion families, sound, saved and replayable shows, and more languages — so more families can color together and more compatible agents can direct. From creative needs to the right plan, tools, and actions - powered by WebMCP Like Comment Story Updates Creative Community Orchestrator From the right plan to the right creative action — powered by WebMCP. Inspiration Adobe's community is full of creators asking practical questions: Which Creative Cloud plan is right for me? Will Premiere Pro run on my machine? Which app or feature should I use for this? How do I solve this creative problem? Creators often know what they want to accomplish , but not which plan, product, capability, or workflow will get them there. Creative software is particularly challenging for agents because the right answer often depends on structured context that is easy to miss when interpreting webpages: region, student eligibility, plan contents, product-specific system requirements, checkout destinations, and capabilities inside individual applications . An agent might return US pricing to a student in India, retrieve generic Creative Cloud requirements when asked about Premiere Pro, or suggest manual editing techniques without discovering that Photoshop has a specific capability — Harmonize — designed for the problem. The agent understands what the creator wants, but it cannot always reliably discover what the creative ecosystem can offer. That inspired Creative Community Orchestrator : use WebMCP to make the creative ecosystem structured and discoverable to agents, helping creators move from intent to the right action faster. What it does Creative Community Orchestrator connects a creator's intent and context to the right plan, product, capability, workflow, and action. A design student in India can ask: "I need Photoshop, Illustrator and occasional video editing. Which plan should I get?" The agent can use her needs, region, and eligibility to find a relevant plan and regional offer, check product-specific compatibility, and guide her to the appropriate checkout path. The same approach extends to creation: "Clean up these jewelry photos, give them a premium campaign look, and create an Instagram post and launch flyer." The agent can discover capabilities the creator may not know exist and connect them into a workflow: Firefly → Photoshop + Harmonize → Adobe Express The creator describes the outcome. The agent discovers the path. This reduces searching, conflicting information, and dead ends — creating a more direct path from intent → confidence → action . How we built it We built Creative Community Orchestrator as a human-browsable website for exploring creative goals, workflows, capabilities, products, and plans. Using WebMCP , we expose key capabilities as structured semantic tools for external agents, including: Plan recommendation and regional pricing Product-specific compatibility Checkout navigation Creative capability discovery Cross-product workflow composition The tools accept structured context such as region, eligibility, product needs, device context, and creative goals , and return structured results agents can use in subsequent steps. This lets an external agent move from: intent → tool discovery → verified information → capability discovery → workflow → action without relying on brittle DOM interpretation or requiring creators to understand the structure of the creative ecosystem. The website remains fully usable by people through its normal interface, while WebMCP gives agents a structured interface to the same ecosystem. Creators use the UI. Agents use WebMCP. Both access the same creative ecosystem. Challenges we ran into One of the biggest challenges was turning a fragmented creative journey into structured context an agent could reliably use . Plan recommendations depend on region, eligibility, required apps, pricing, and compatibility, while creative questions require mapping natural-language intent to the right capability. Capability discovery was especially interesting. Users rarely ask for features by name. Someone might say, "the lighting on this object doesn't match the scene," and the agent needs to connect that problem to Photoshop Harmonize . Another challenge was deciding what should become a WebMCP tool . Instead of exposing every button or UI interaction, we focused on meaningful creator intents: recommend a plan, check compatibility, discover a capability, find the correct purchase path, and compose a workflow. We also wanted the website to remain useful to humans while becoming understandable to agents , without turning the experience into another chatbot. Accomplishments that we're proud of We're proud that the project demonstrates WebMCP across a connected creator journey: Plan discovery → Regional pricing → Compatibility → Checkout → Capability discovery → Creative workflow The agent can move beyond recommending broad products and discover specific capabilities such as Harmonize based on the creator's actual problem. For a creative workflow, the collaboration can look like: Firefly → create the campaign treatment Photoshop + Harmonize → refine the composition and resolve the lighting mismatch Adobe Express → create the Instagram post and launch flyer Meera doesn't need to know beforehand which plan fits her needs, that Harmonize exists, which application contains it, or how multiple creative products can work together. At the same time, the experience remains fully human-browsable while exposing the same ecosystem to external agents. What we learned Our biggest learning was that the agentic web isn't only about helping agents perform actions — it's also about helping them discover what actions are possible . Creators think in outcomes: "Which plan fits me?" , "Will this run on my machine?" , "Make this composition look natural," or "Turn this into a campaign." They shouldn't need to know the exact plan, product, feature name, or navigation path first. Previously, creators had to translate their needs into the product ecosystem themselves: research plans → compare prices → check requirements → discover products → find features → assemble a workflow With WebMCP, that responsibility can be shared: Creators provide intent, context, and creative judgment. Agents discover, verify, recommend, and orchestrate. WebMCP becomes the bridge between creator intent and product capability . What's next for Creative Community Orchestrator Next, we want to expand Creative Community Orchestrator into an agent-accessible support layer across the creator journey . That includes troubleshooting : a creator could say, "My Premiere Pro export looks washed out," and the agent could discover relevant product-specific guidance instead of returning generic results. We also want to connect feature discovery directly to feature access . If the agent discovers that Harmonize is the right capability, it should take the creator to the exact feature, product experience, or relevant page where they can learn more or start using it. Our vision expands the journey to: Choose → Verify → Discover → Troubleshoot → Navigate → Create So creators can stay focused on what they want to accomplish , while agents reliably discover how the creative ecosystem can help them get there. People describe what they want to accomplish. Agents discover what's possible and help them act on it. From creative intent to creative action — with WebMCP.
