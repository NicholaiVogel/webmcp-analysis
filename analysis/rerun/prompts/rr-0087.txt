# FROZEN SCORING RUBRIC (pre-registered before any Stage 1 review)

Scores are integers 1-10 on each of the FOUR official criteria. Every score carries:
rationale, evidence_surfaces, confidence (0-1). Reviewers use the full scale and these
band anchors verbatim. Category neutrality is absolute: a game, art piece, music tool,
CRM, weird toy, and developer tool are all eligible for 10/10 on every criterion if they
satisfy that criterion exceptionally well FOR WHAT THEY ARE TRYING TO BE.
No aesthetic penalties. Signs of hurried AI work matter only as actual product-quality
problems (incoherence, broken flow, placeholder content), never as style.

## WebMCP Leverage — what does WebMCP change for this product?
Judge the delta WebMCP makes to reliability, precision, structured state access,
shared human-agent state, repeatability, and capability. Tool COUNT is not leverage.
- 9-10 TRANSFORMATIVE: the intended experience is impractical without the structured
  agent surface; human and agent share live product state; the collaboration could not
  be replicated comparably well by a general agent driving the UI.
- 7-8 MAJOR DELTA: without WebMCP a general agent is markedly less reliable/precise
  here; the agent is central to the product, not a bolt-on.
- 5-6 MEANINGFUL DELTA: existing capability packaged into a genuinely better product
  experience via structured tools; same outcome otherwise possible with real friction.
- 3-4 MINOR DELTA: generic browser automation achieves comparable outcomes; WebMCP is
  a convenience layer.
- 1-2 COSMETIC: exposes an action a UI button already offers; no meaningful change.

## Execution — is the actual product coherent, intentional, complete for its scope?
Stage 1 scores EXECUTION_CLAIMED: coherence/intentionality/completeness as evidenced by
the packet (description + packaging + video frames/transcript). Thin evidence caps the
score (end-to-end credit requires end-to-end evidence) and lowers confidence.
Stage 2 may rescore as EXECUTION_OBSERVED; latest evidence-backed score replaces, never
sums.
- 9-10: all surfaces agree; central workflow demonstrably completes; states handled;
  feels finished for its stated scope.
- 7-8: strong coherence and intentionality; central workflow appears functional; minor
  unfinished edges.
- 5-6: plausibly real with visible gaps; scope unclear or core not demonstrated
  end-to-end.
- 3-4: thin prototype; contradictions between surfaces; placeholder content.
- 1-2: non-functional or incoherent for its claimed scope.

## Creativity & Ambition — novelty of concept and interaction model
Judge ONLY: novelty of the concept, novelty of the interaction model, originality
relative to existing concepts, ambition, memorable/surprising use of the medium, and
the depth with which the concept is pursued. WebMCP indispensability belongs under
WebMCP Leverage, NOT here: a project can be extremely creative even if another agent
API could theoretically have enabled something similar.
- 9-10: genuinely novel concept or interaction model; ambitious scope pursued deeply
  and coherently; memorable.
- 7-8: fresh synthesis or notably original interaction; ambitious for a hackathon.
- 5-6: competent take on a known category with a real new angle.
- 3-4: derivative; novelty mostly surface details.
- 1-2: clone/template with trivial changes.
"That the category exists" is never a penalty; judge the submitted concept.

## Potential Impact — credible value for the intended audience
Judge: is there a real audience; is there a real problem, desire, or use case for that
audience; does the demonstrated solution genuinely address it. NO commercial adoption,
monetization, market pull, or startup viability is required. Entertainment, play, art,
creative expression, learning, niche hobby use, experimentation, and community value
are legitimate forms of impact. A tiny audience fully justifies a top score.
- 9-10: specific real audience with a real problem/desire; solution demonstrably
  addresses it; credible value, appeal, usefulness, or meaning for that audience.
- 7-8: clear audience and problem, good fit, credible value.
- 5-6: identifiable audience, looser fit or partial address.
- 3-4: speculative audience or strained fit; impact mostly aspirational.
- 1-2: no coherent audience/problem pairing.
Audience size is NOT a multiplier.

## Aggregate & ranking
aggregate = leverage + execution + impact + creativity (4-40). Nothing else enters.
Tie-breaks: Leverage, then Execution, then Impact, then Creativity, then higher
combined evidence confidence, then slug alphabetical. See PROTOCOL.md Ranking section.

Algoosh's WebMCP tools neither request nor return delivery plaintext or wallet credentials. After payment, the agent can retrieve a privacy-safe order summary, the payment state, the selected fulfillment option, the final total, and links to the verified payment and application transactions. How we built it We extended the existing Algoosh platform with four page-local WebMCP tools: get_current_algoosh_catalogue get_current_algoosh_product prepare_algoosh_order get_algoosh_order_status The tools are registered through document.modelContext.registerTool() only after the current catalogue has loaded. Their lifecycle is tied to an AbortSignal , so stale registrations are removed when the catalogue changes or the page unloads. No separate MCP server installation is required for this flow; the loaded catalogue page exposes the tools directly. The ordering system uses a two-stage process. First, the WebMCP tool creates a durable but non-payable preliminary intent. The agent supplies only product IDs, quantities, and an idempotency key. It cannot set authoritative prices, seller addresses, payment destinations, delivery details, or wallet information in the order request. Next, the buyer opens the approval handoff. The Algoosh application collects fulfillment information, encrypts the private delivery payload in the browser, and asks the x402 service to finalize the order. Before issuing a payment requirement, the service independently reloads the seller's catalogue and verifies: Product identities and quantities. Current prices and payment assets. Seller and payment addresses. The selected fulfillment option and its current terms and cost. The target Algorand network and application. The service then binds the buyer identity and encrypted-order commitments into the signed quote. If any important commercial term changed, the order stops before payment and the buyer must create and review a new order. Because a token transfer alone is not sufficient evidence of an Algoosh order, we implemented a custom x402 v2 scheme named algoosh-order . Settlement validation checks both the payment and the adjacent createOrderPaid smart-contract call against the signed quote. The finalized order uses an x402 PAYMENT-REQUIRED response containing the signed quote. The buyer application verifies that quote and constructs an atomic Algorand transaction group containing the payment and the Algoosh smart-contract order call. Only the buyer's wallet can approve and submit that group. Redis stores the durable state used for atomic intent creation, idempotency, settlement recovery, and duplicate-order protection. Algoosh Sync validates the resulting public on-chain evidence and correlates it with the prepared order. The approval URL is a short-lived opaque capability link. It contains no personal information, is served with Cache-Control: no-store , and cannot be redirected to an untrusted destination. Challenges we ran into The biggest challenge was preserving human wallet control while still creating a genuine agentic shopping experience. Many agent-payment flows assume that the agent or facilitator can sign and submit the payment. That model did not fit Algoosh. We designed the preliminary-intent and browser-approval stages so the agent can make useful progress without ever receiving payment authority. Another challenge was maintaining trust across several independent surfaces: Promo catalogue -> WebMCP agent -> x402 service -> Buyer application -> Wallet -> Algorand -> Sync The trust-critical components needed to preserve consistent bindings across the catalogue, products, mount, buyer, seller, fulfillment selection, order seed, and transaction, while exposing only the information appropriate to each surface. Accomplishments that we're proud of We are especially proud that Algoosh Agentic Shopping is more than a catalogue-reading demo. It connects WebMCP product discovery to a real, human-approved commerce flow. Key accomplishments include: Four functional WebMCP tools verified in a production browser context. A cross-device approval handoff with both a link and generated QR code. A complete low-value Algorand TestNet purchase with explicit buyer approval. x402 payment requirements carrying a signed quote backed by authoritative catalogue data. A custom x402 settlement scheme that validates the complete Algoosh atomic group rather than only a token transfer. Redis-backed recovery that survives service restarts. Protection against duplicate payments during uncertain confirmation. Recovery from temporary blockchain indexer delays. Detection of changed prices or selected fulfillment terms before payment. Privacy-safe agent status responses containing no delivery details. Exact display amounts, ISO timestamps, consistent network metadata, repeated order summaries, and transaction explorer links for agents. Browser-side encryption of the buyer's delivery information. Strict separation between AI assistance and wallet authority. Most importantly, we extended the real Algoosh shopping and payment architecture instead of building a disconnected hackathon simulation. What we learned We learned that agentic commerce does not have to mean autonomous spending. The most useful experience is collaborative: the agent handles discovery, comparison, and preparation, while the person remains responsible for private information and consequential decisions. Fill the form. Keep the rest. Like 1 Comment Story Updates Inspiration Most forms ask for more personal information than they actually need. I wanted to test a simple idea: could an agent help finish a form without getting a free pass to read an entire profile? That became 5 of 23. The name is literal. The demo profile has 23 fields, but the sample application only needs five of them and an over-18 check. The point is not to make form filling faster at any cost. It is to make the boundary visible: what is needed, why it is needed, and who gets to release it. What it does The agent can inspect the application requirements and see what is missing without receiving the raw profile. For the age requirement, it calls an allow-listed is_adult check and gets a boolean result instead of a date of birth. It then requests the five fields required by the form. The request appears in the page, but it does not grant access. I approve or reject each field in the interface. Once the approved values have been applied, the form reaches 6/6 requirements and the temporary write capability disappears. There is still no submit tool. Final review remains a separate UI action. If that review is approved, the page exposes a local packet marked submitted: false . Revoking city removes the value, clears the reviewed copy and makes the old tool handle fail because it belongs to an earlier consent version. Everything in the demo is synthetic. It does not send an application anywhere. Why WebMCP This flow depends on the agent and the person sharing the live state of the same page: requirements, missing answers, approved scope, consent version and review status. WebMCP gives the agent a semantic tool surface instead of making it scrape buttons and labels. More importantly, the page can register a capability only while that capability is valid. The available tools change with consent, and the callbacks check the consent version again when they run. That is why this is not a chat box added to a form. The agent works out what the task needs and carries out the approved part. The person controls disclosure and review. The page enforces the boundary. How I built it I built the project in React and TypeScript with Vinext and Vite. The page registers its tools at the top level with document.modelContext.registerTool(...) so a compatible browser can discover them directly. A deterministic in-browser state engine owns the synthetic profile, form progress, disclosure requests, approvals, consent versions, receipts and reviewed packet. The normal interface and the WebMCP callbacks use that same store. The schemas use narrow enums, reject extra properties and bind dynamic writes to the current consent version. The prototype has no application API, database, login, analytics, payment flow or external submission handler. That kept the build focused on the consent model rather than infrastructure. The source code is public under the MIT License . Challenges I ran into The hard part was not the happy path. It was keeping six states separate and understandable: request, approval, application, review, revocation and stale authority. Dynamic tools also needed two layers of protection. A tool has to disappear when its authority disappears, but its callback must still reject an old handle that was fetched before the change. I also had to keep the proof honest: a mocked test is useful, but it is not the same thing as a real browser discovering and invoking the final page. What I am proud of In the local end-to-end browser run, the available tool count moved 8 → 9 → 8 → 9 → 8 as consent and review changed. The minimum path completed 6/6 requirements using 5 of 23 fields and one boolean age check. The other 18 fields stayed sealed in the tool/UI flow. Revoking city returned the form to 5/6 , left 4 of 23 fields disclosed, removed the reviewed-packet tool and rejected its old handle. The deterministic suite passed 14/14 tests , along with typecheck, lint, the production build and the recorded npm audit. The demo data lives in the public client bundle, so “sealed” describes the exercised interface and tool behavior, not source-code secrecy. This is a synthetic prototype, not a claim of production-grade security or measured user impact. What I learned Tool discovery is only the start of an agent-native product. The more interesting question is when a capability should exist, how small its scope can be, and what should happen to authority the moment consent changes. I also learned that privacy needs visible negative evidence. A good demo should show not only what the agent can do, but what it cannot read, cannot approve and can no longer use after revocation. What's next I would add property-based tests around redaction and stale versions, a small policy language for purpose and expiry, and a proper accessibility review of the consent controls. The next product question is whether people clearly understand the difference between a request, a grant and a reviewed local packet.
