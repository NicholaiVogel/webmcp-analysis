Like Comment Story Updates Product Picture apply edit Inspiration My girlfriend is a medical student. I'm training as an alternative practitioner, the regulated German profession called Heilpraktiker . We spend a lot of our time on opposite sides of the same questions, and we kept ending up in long kitchen-table conversations about what the evidence actually says, where it's thin, and where people are being handed something more confident than anyone can honestly claim. At some point we figured those conversations would make a decent podcast. Then we looked at our calendars. We both work full time and we're both still studying. Finding two hours to record was doable. The rest of it was the problem. An hour of the two of us talking turns into a long evening of cleanup: the false starts, the "hold on, let me say that again", the twenty seconds where one of us is looking up a paper. Every podcast we know that quietly stopped after four episodes stopped for that reason. So the podcast went on hold and we built the tool first. A slightly ridiculous order of operations, but here we are. What it does Werkstern records each person's voice locally and uncompressed. While you're talking, you tag things with a single keypress: retake, error, good point, chapter, source. Those tags are the whole idea. The tedious part of editing gets done while you're still in the conversation, by the person who just heard what went wrong. Afterwards, ChatGPT reads the finished take through WebMCP and suggests specific regions to remove, in seconds. The app lists them for you, you keep or drop each one, and only then does anything get cut. You get an audible preview before you commit and an undo that puts everything back. The original recordings stay untouched, which we verify byte for byte in the test suite. Why we used MCP We started from a constraint. We're recording medical conversations, including half-formed thoughts and things one of us says and then takes back. That audio stays on the machine, so any design that began with uploading the file was ruled out early. The second thing that shaped it was accuracy. In a conversation about medicine the qualifier is often the load-bearing part of the sentence. "This helps in a narrow group of patients" and "this helps" are two different claims, and only one of them is true. We needed to see every proposed cut before it happened. WebMCP fit both of those. The model never receives audio. It receives a deliberately thin description of the take: markers, director signals and pause windows, all in seconds. No names, no device labels, no file paths, no tokens. It reasons over that and hands back numbers, and the app does the actual cutting locally with FFmpeg, sample-accurately. That division of labour is what made the project feel workable. The model handles the part it's good at, which is reading a conversation and having an opinion about what to trim. The app handles the part it's good at, which is being exact and reversible. The approval step in between is where most of our care went. How we built it The recorder came first and took most of the time. Capture. An AudioWorklet per station at 48 kHz, 24-bit mono, with echo cancellation, noise suppression and automatic gain deliberately switched off. Two-second chunks, each carrying a CRC32, buffered in IndexedDB so a Wi-Fi dropout costs you nothing. Node, Fastify and ws on the host machine; the browser side is plain ES modules with no framework. Clock drift. Two laptops have two crystals, and over an hour they disagree. This was the hardest audio problem here. Werkstern writes a 1 kHz stripe digitally into every raw track just after the start and again just before the stop, then locates both afterwards by cross-correlation. For each guest track the correction factor is $$f = \frac{n_{\text{end}} - n_{\text{start}}}{N_{\text{end}} - N_{\text{start}}}$$ where $n$ are the guest's stripe positions and $N$ the host's, in samples. We report it as $(f - 1) \times 10^{6}$ ppm and correct it with asetrate plus SOXR resampling at 28-bit precision. A take is accepted only when the residual offset stays under 240 samples, which at 48 kHz works out to $$\frac{240}{48\,000} = 5\ \text{ms}.$$ Rendering. The soundboard stays out of the microphone signal. Every pad press is stored as an event, and the final stereo mix is rebuilt afterwards from that log plus a content-addressed copy of each sound file. Replace a sound six months later and the old episode still renders exactly as it did. The agent layer sits on top and touches none of that. Eight tools registered in the top-level document through document.modelContext.registerTool : four read-only ones, preview_edit_batch which validates and displays without saving anything, and three writing tools behind the approval dialog. Every schema is closed with additionalProperties: false and bounded lengths, and each tool carries an honest readOnlyHint so the host can tell reading from writing. Cuts are stored as batches of source-time regions with a revision number that only ever goes up. Every write has to state the revision it believes is current, so a command built on stale information gets rejected instead of landing on a take that has moved on. The preview you hear is the complement of what was removed: for regions $R = {[s_i, e_i)}$ over a take of length $T$, it plays $[0, T) \setminus \bigcup_i [s_i, e_i)$, concatenated. Challenges we ran into Approval turned out to be a concurrency problem. Our worst bug had nothing to do with audio. Two approval requests could share the same dialog element: the second one overwrote the text of the first while it was still open, and one click then resolved both promises. You would have read "render a preview?" and, with that same click, authorized a permanent edit. Approvals are serialized now, and a second request arriving while one is open gets refused. Cancelling, losing the connection, leaving the dialog unanswered, and a dialog that fails to open all count as a no. Undo had a hole in it. If you queued a preview render and then hit undo, the render was still holding the old region list and would cheerfully write the audio you had just discarded back to disk. A render now re-reads the take before it starts and gives up if the batch is gone. We reviewed our own work adversarially, and it was uncomfortable. Before calling it finished we ran an independent audit against our own commit. It came back with one critical finding and a handful of serious ones. Then, while fixing those, I introduced a new one: my approval guard could get stuck and silently refuse every write for the rest of the session. It only surfaced because a browser test behaved oddly and I chased it instead of writing it off as flakiness. I'm glad I did. Testing a modal dialog under browser automation was miserable. The automation pane we used never dispatches the <dialog> close event. showModal() opens it, close() sets the return value, and the listener simply never runs. Instead of calling it working on a harness we couldn't trust, we proved the flow two other ways: unit tests with a DOM stub where the event does fire, and an end-to-end test that drives the real FFmpeg binary over a synthetic take, checks the output is exactly 48 kHz stereo 16-bit at the expected length, and confirms the source file's SHA-256 is identical before and after. We have no transcript and no speech recognition, so we can't do it. get_edit_capabilities says so directly in its output, to the model, in the tool response itself. Search, inspect, import, and prepare each have a clear contract and permission level. We also learned that human approval is not friction to eliminate when identity, reputation, or money is involved. That balance let us ship the extension into Favor instead of maintaining a separate hackathon demo. Like Comment Story Updates OpenDeal OpenDeal is agent-native negotiation for the open web. It demonstrates how AI agents can discover suppliers, compare structured offers, negotiate price, verify delivery constraints, and recommend a transaction through WebMCP — while the human retains explicit authority over the irreversible financial action. The mission Buy 100 ANSI Z89.1 Type II safety helmets with four hard constraints: total budget ≤ $2,500 delivery by Friday no substitutions final purchase requires human approval Three supplier contexts expose WebMCP tools for inventory discovery, product specifications, quotes, counteroffers, and delivery checks. The deterministic negotiation produces: Alpha Industrial: $2,350, Friday — compliant Bravo Safety Supply: $2,325, Friday — compliant and selected Charlie Works: $2,290, Monday — cheaper, but rejected because it violates the delivery deadline The agent therefore optimizes for the buyer's actual constraints instead of simply choosing the lowest price. The core WebMCP idea: bounded authority OpenDeal does not treat human approval as a cosmetic modal. Authorization changes the browser's actual executable capability surface. Before approval, the suppliers' search, specification, quote, negotiation, and delivery tools are available — but bravo.create_purchase_order does not exist. When the human approves Bravo's exact $2,325 offer, OpenDeal dynamically registers the purchase capability. After the order executes, that capability is removed and replaced with bravo.get_order_status . A live Chrome/WebMCP run verified the lifecycle directly from document.modelContext : PRE-APPROVAL: 15 tools; purchase capability absent APPROVED: 16 tools; bravo.create_purchase_order present ORDERED: 16 tools; purchase capability absent; bravo.get_order_status present The workflow is: discover → evaluate → negotiate → recommend → human authorize → execute → observe Why WebMCP is a strong fit Without WebMCP, an agent would usually need supplier-specific APIs, DOM scraping, brittle browser automation, or a centralized marketplace integration. With WebMCP, each website intentionally exposes structured actions using explicit schemas. The website controls which capabilities exist, their scope, and their lifecycle. The agent gets reliable machine-usable actions without guessing how to click through a UI. That creates a better user experience: the agent handles repetitive discovery, comparison, negotiation, and constraint checking, while the person keeps control over the action that commits money. What people and agents can do together The human provides the objective and authority boundary. The agent performs the multi-supplier work that would otherwise require repetitive browsing, quote collection, comparison, and follow-up. The human only steps back in when the transaction crosses the approval boundary. The important distinction is that the system does not merely say the agent lacks purchasing authority — the purchase operation is genuinely unavailable until authorization occurs. Implementation OpenDeal is intentionally dependency-light so the WebMCP behavior remains visible and auditable. It uses: modern JavaScript ES modules HTML and CSS browser Web APIs document.modelContext.registerTool() getTools() and executeTool() structured tool input schemas deterministic supplier pricing and delivery policies AbortController -scoped tool lifecycles an explicit transaction state machine a visible execution ledger and WebMCP inspector automated domain and lifecycle tests Each supplier registers five base capabilities. The selected supplier's purchase tool is dynamically registered only after approval, then removed after use and replaced by an order-status capability. The implementation also includes explicit buyer-origin allowlisting support for deployments where supplier contexts live on distinct origins; the challenge deployment is kept same-origin on Netlify for simple, reproducible judging. Why it matters Procurement is only the demonstration domain. The same capability-boundary pattern can apply to travel, logistics, reservations, insurance, professional services, or any workflow where agents need meaningful autonomy without unlimited authority. OpenDeal explores a web where agents do not need to imitate human clicking. Websites become intentional participants in agent-mediated transactions, and humans retain explicit control over the boundaries that matter most.
