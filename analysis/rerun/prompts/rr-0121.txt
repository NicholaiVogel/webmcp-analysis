# FROZEN SCORING RUBRIC (pre-registered before any Stage 1 review)

Scores are integers 1-10 on each of the FOUR official criteria. Every score carries:
rationale, evidence_surfaces, confidence (0-1). Reviewers use the full scale and these
band anchors verbatim. Category neutrality is absolute: a game, art piece, music tool,
CRM, weird toy, and developer tool are all eligible for 10/10 on every criterion if they
satisfy that criterion exceptionally well FOR WHAT THEY ARE TRYING TO BE.
No aesthetic penalties. Signs of hurried AI work matter only as actual product-quality
problems (incoherence, broken flow, placeholder content), never as style.

## WebMCP Leverage — what does WebMCP change for this product?
Judge the delta WebMCP makes to reliability, precision, structured state access,
shared human-agent state, repeatability, and capability. Tool COUNT is not leverage.
- 9-10 TRANSFORMATIVE: the intended experience is impractical without the structured
  agent surface; human and agent share live product state; the collaboration could not
  be replicated comparably well by a general agent driving the UI.
- 7-8 MAJOR DELTA: without WebMCP a general agent is markedly less reliable/precise
  here; the agent is central to the product, not a bolt-on.
- 5-6 MEANINGFUL DELTA: existing capability packaged into a genuinely better product
  experience via structured tools; same outcome otherwise possible with real friction.
- 3-4 MINOR DELTA: generic browser automation achieves comparable outcomes; WebMCP is
  a convenience layer.
- 1-2 COSMETIC: exposes an action a UI button already offers; no meaningful change.

## Execution — is the actual product coherent, intentional, complete for its scope?
Stage 1 scores EXECUTION_CLAIMED: coherence/intentionality/completeness as evidenced by
the packet (description + packaging + video frames/transcript). Thin evidence caps the
score (end-to-end credit requires end-to-end evidence) and lowers confidence.
Stage 2 may rescore as EXECUTION_OBSERVED; latest evidence-backed score replaces, never
sums.
- 9-10: all surfaces agree; central workflow demonstrably completes; states handled;
  feels finished for its stated scope.
- 7-8: strong coherence and intentionality; central workflow appears functional; minor
  unfinished edges.
- 5-6: plausibly real with visible gaps; scope unclear or core not demonstrated
  end-to-end.
- 3-4: thin prototype; contradictions between surfaces; placeholder content.
- 1-2: non-functional or incoherent for its claimed scope.

## Creativity & Ambition — novelty of concept and interaction model
Judge ONLY: novelty of the concept, novelty of the interaction model, originality
relative to existing concepts, ambition, memorable/surprising use of the medium, and
the depth with which the concept is pursued. WebMCP indispensability belongs under
WebMCP Leverage, NOT here: a project can be extremely creative even if another agent
API could theoretically have enabled something similar.
- 9-10: genuinely novel concept or interaction model; ambitious scope pursued deeply
  and coherently; memorable.
- 7-8: fresh synthesis or notably original interaction; ambitious for a hackathon.
- 5-6: competent take on a known category with a real new angle.
- 3-4: derivative; novelty mostly surface details.
- 1-2: clone/template with trivial changes.
"That the category exists" is never a penalty; judge the submitted concept.

## Potential Impact — credible value for the intended audience
Judge: is there a real audience; is there a real problem, desire, or use case for that
audience; does the demonstrated solution genuinely address it. NO commercial adoption,
monetization, market pull, or startup viability is required. Entertainment, play, art,
creative expression, learning, niche hobby use, experimentation, and community value
are legitimate forms of impact. A tiny audience fully justifies a top score.
- 9-10: specific real audience with a real problem/desire; solution demonstrably
  addresses it; credible value, appeal, usefulness, or meaning for that audience.
- 7-8: clear audience and problem, good fit, credible value.
- 5-6: identifiable audience, looser fit or partial address.
- 3-4: speculative audience or strained fit; impact mostly aspirational.
- 1-2: no coherent audience/problem pairing.
Audience size is NOT a multiplier.

## Aggregate & ranking
aggregate = leverage + execution + impact + creativity (4-40). Nothing else enters.
Tie-breaks: Leverage, then Execution, then Impact, then Creativity, then higher
combined evidence confidence, then slug alphabetical. See PROTOCOL.md Ranking section.

The traffic simulation runs entirely in the tab — no backend, no API, nothing meaningful to scrape off a canvas. The state exists only in the page. That is the point. A conventional browser agent could screenshot this and learn nothing: it cannot read mean_wait_s out of pixels, and evaluating a candidate timing would cost it a slider drag, a button click and a screenshot round trip. It would manage perhaps five experiments while an agent with site tools runs two hundred. Not faster — categorically different. The junction exposes seven WebMCP tools. An agent arrives cold, reads the model specification, searches the timing space off the main thread while the traffic keeps flowing on screen, and hands back a scrubbable explanation of what it found. The operator watches, locks parameters mid-search, and decides. The design argument The interesting part is not the simulation. It is the shape of the tool surface, and three decisions in particular. run_batch reports physics. apply_config enforces policy. run_batch evaluates any timing, including ones that are illegal to deploy, and reports honestly what would happen. It checks nothing. apply_config is the only tool that can commit a timing to the junction, and the only one that enforces the constraints. Keeping those separate means the agent can discover that the fastest timing is inadmissible rather than being silently steered away from it. Under school-run demand the best-scoring timing is a permissive-left plan at 27.4 s mean wait; it is prohibited, because opposing volume × left-turn volume exceeds the phasing threshold. The best legal timing costs 45.8 s. An agent that only ever saw filtered results would never understand what admissibility cost. The refusal says so explicitly: a refusal is a fact about what may be deployed, not a scoring signal — a refused timing may well be the fastest one. Constraints ship with their derivations, and it changed agent behaviour describe_model publishes every constraint with the arithmetic behind it. Yellow and all-red are not tuning parameters; they are safety intervals derived from the ITE change-interval formulae: approach speed v = 15 m/s (54 km/h) yellow y = t + v/(2a) → 3.50 s → floor 4 s all-red r = (W + L)/v → 1.33 s → floor 2 s Two measured effects on a real agent run: Unprompted, it used 5 seeds and 60-minute runs. run_batch's description warns about ±2 s of simulation noise and recommends averaging at least 3 seeds; the agent went further on its own. It never hit the phasing refusal. Its 50-config refine batch was entirely left_protected: true — it read the cross-product rule, worked out that school-run demand mandates protected phasing, and pre-filtered its search space. Publishing constraints in full meant a careful agent self-checked instead of learning by rejection. Locks are a permission boundary with no unlock tool The operator can pin any parameter. read_state reports which are locked; apply_config refuses to change one and says so. There is deliberately no tool that unlocks a parameter. The agent can read the lock, reason about it, and re-optimise around it — only a human clicking the button releases it. A permission model expressed through the shape of the surface rather than a confirmation dialog. Measured on a 36-config batch: 53,558 → 11,284 characters, a 79% reduction. Context bloat is what degrades an agent over a long session. run_batch runs in a Web Worker. A 50-config batch is ~500 ms; on the main thread that freezes the junction every time the agent thinks, and the premise is that the human watches a live workspace while the agent works. apply_config restarts the live run and says so, along with the ~4 simulated minutes before metrics settle. Otherwise the agent commits, re-reads two seconds later, and reasons about a transient. set_scenario re-checks the committed timing. Switching demand can make the deployed plan illegal. It reports that and leaves the plan committed — the agent asked to change the demand, not to redeploy the signal plan. pin_result re-measures over 3 seeds regardless of what produced the original figure, so two pins are always comparable. The agent's search is drawn in the domain's own notation. Notes on the model A stochastic cellular automaton (Nagel–Schreckenberg). Arrival rates are representative of a mid-size German town, not measured. Two modelling details matter for the decisions the agent faces: Opposing traffic is platooned. Gap acceptance uses the opposing flow concentrated into its green window, not the hourly average — the platoon is released by the same green in which a permissive left-turner is trying to cross. Without this, permissive lefts are far too easy and left_protected becomes a parameter with one always-correct answer. The left-turn bay is 60 m. When it fills, left-turners spill back and block the through lane behind them. That is why protected phasing is ever worth its cycle time, and why sizing the protected phase wrong is worse than not having one: at school-run demand, protected at 8 s scores 214 s against permissive's 74 s, while protected at 16 s is the best timing available. Ceiling: a bounded WebMCP change gate with visible authority and evidence. Ceiling Why I built it Agents are getting better at working inside websites. That is useful, but it creates a basic problem: how do I let an agent help without handing it every action available in my signed-in session? Ceiling is my attempt to answer that question with something people can actually try. It is a small release-policy workbench where an agent can read the current state, prepare a change, and explain why it wants that change. The page decides whether the request fits its rules, and a person remains in control of the final action. I did not want the safety story to be a warning banner or a promise in a README. I wanted the boundary to be visible in the interface and testable from the agent side. What it does The demo follows four simple steps: The agent proposes one change. Ceiling checks the value and shows the difference. A person can authorize that specific change for a few seconds. The page applies or rejects it once and records what happened. Unsafe requests stop before the approval step. In the demo, an attempt to turn off signed artifacts is denied immediately. A safer request to change the rollback window from 30 to 45 minutes is allowed into review, but the agent still cannot apply it on its own. Approval is deliberately narrow. It is tied to the current policy, the proposed value, and the requested operation. It expires quickly and cannot be reused. Rollback follows the same rule instead of acting as an unprotected escape hatch. Every decision adds a linked receipt. The page can verify the chain again and export the session evidence as JSON. A built-in Defense Matrix also tries seven ways to break the boundary, including replay, expiry, parallel application, and receipt tampering. Why WebMCP fits Without a structured interface, an agent either has to guess from pixels or a site has to expose a broad action API. WebMCP gives the page a better option: it can publish a small, discoverable set of tools with clear inputs. Ceiling registers 11 tools directly through document.modelContext.registerTool() . Four are read-only. Seven cover the review workflow. An agent can inspect the available tools, read the policy, propose a change, test the safety boundary, and verify the receipts without being given deployment, credential, payment, messaging, or cross-origin powers. The result is a better experience for both sides. The agent does not have to guess what it is allowed to do, and the user does not have to trust a vague confirmation dialog. How I built it Ceiling is dependency-free HTML, CSS, and JavaScript. The WebMCP tools use closed input schemas, and the interface reports whether registration succeeded, partly succeeded, or is unavailable. The release-policy demo sits on a reusable scalar-change core. I also tested that core against publishing, access-control, and spending-policy examples to make sure the pattern was not tied to one screen. The focused suite currently includes 78 tests, a 23-case policy evaluation, and 50 cross-domain adapter checks. I used OpenAI Codex during implementation, testing, adversarial review, and release packaging. A separate non-author review initially rejected the release because of three packaging and claim-boundary problems. I corrected those problems before preparing this submission. What was difficult The hardest part was defining what a click actually proves. Browser automation can produce trusted input, so Ceiling does not call a click proof of identity or proof that no automation was involved. It treats the gesture as short-lived permission for one visible operation and no more. I also found a concurrency bug in an earlier receipt implementation. Parallel writes could reuse the same sequence information. I kept that behavior as a regression fixture, serialized the active write path, and added full-chain verification. Chrome on my test machine did not expose WebMCP during the recorded fallback pass. The page says that plainly and keeps the fallback controls separate. The working WebMCP demonstration was verified in the ChatGPT/Codex in-app browser. What I learned The most useful lesson was that refusal is part of the product. A trustworthy agent interface should make denied actions, missing capabilities, and failed preconditions as clear as successful ones. I also learned that evidence is only useful when it is easy to inspect and hard to accidentally overstate. The final project deliberately claims less than the larger system I hope to build. What's next Ceiling is session-local by design. The next steps would be reviewed host adapters, optional signed or externally anchored receipts, and carefully scoped persistence. Each would need its own authority boundary rather than silently expanding the one demonstrated here. Like Comment Story Updates Inspiration My inspiration for this project is very close to my heart: my partner has celiac disease, which means she cannot eat food containing gluten, or food that has had cross-contact with gluten. This disease has turned grocery shopping into a stressful experience. She has to read every ingredient label, and the often-overlooked "may contain" statements. This is not only the experience of people with celiac disease. People with other dietary restrictions or severe food allergies have the same problem. AI-led online shopping does not make grocery shopping easier for them yet. What it does Label Check makes an agent retrieve a product’s ingredients and label statements before it can add that product to the cart. Maple & Sage is the grocery shop where that rule is live. An agent can search through the product catalog and build a cart the same way it would on any online store. What it has to retrieve is the ingredients and the label statements, including when a field is missing. "null" is not the same as an empty list and it is not treated as "none". Alongside the agent’s WebMCP path, the client can still use the ordinary "add" button to add products to their cart. The store never decides whether a product is suitable. The client lists their restrictions for their AI agent, the merchant supplies the facts (ingredients and label information), and the AI agent compares them to make a decision. How I built it I built an original Online Store 2.0 theme, using the Shopify theme skeleton's GitHub repository. Each product’s ingredients and label statements are written into the page, so the custom tool can return them without a backend. A small script runs before Shopify’s WebMCP tools register, so the gate is already there when an agent calls update_cart. There is one custom tool, get_product_food_disclosures. It returns ingredients and label_statements, including null. A receipt in the tab records that retrieval ran for that product version. Native update_cart can increase a line only when that receipt is current. Challenges I ran into Keeping null and [] distinct took more care than I initially thought. An empty "may contain" list is not the same as "the merchant did not supply this field". Collapsing them would hide the exact thing my partner has to look for. And then naming this project was a fun one! Anything that sounded like Trust, Safe, or Verified would imply the store had judged the product. I chose Label Check because it was the best way for me to describe the action that the agent has to do. It's not a pass mark on the product. Accomplishments that I'm proud of I'm proud that I was able to use the WebMCP technology to build something that would actually help someone I love. Getting it working, on a real shop that an agent can use, is such a great feeling. What I learned Prior to this challenge, I didn't know what WebMCP was. I learned about it and its use cases, especially the ability for an agent to help us perform a task while we can still intervene at any time (kind of like co-building), which I find incredibly interesting. I chose to use WebMCP to build Label Check, but I can also see it being useful in creative endeavors: editing a 3D model, a picture, or a video together with an agent. What's next for Label Check I would love ecommerce platforms such as Shopify to ship ingredients and label-statement fields that food shop owners can fill, the way they did with product taxonomy. Merchants used those fields to add more detail to their catalogs and consequently ranked better in searches. Doing the same for dietary information would help those products show up when someone asks an agent a specific question, like "recommend 5 gluten-free snacks that are safe for people with celiac disease". Then I would love those ecommerce platforms to add a Label Check tool to their native WebMCP stack. This way, people with dietary restrictions would be able to point their AI agent at a shop and buy online confidently.
