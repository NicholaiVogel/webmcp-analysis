# FROZEN SCORING RUBRIC (pre-registered before any Stage 1 review)

Scores are integers 1-10 on each of the FOUR official criteria. Every score carries:
rationale, evidence_surfaces, confidence (0-1). Reviewers use the full scale and these
band anchors verbatim. Category neutrality is absolute: a game, art piece, music tool,
CRM, weird toy, and developer tool are all eligible for 10/10 on every criterion if they
satisfy that criterion exceptionally well FOR WHAT THEY ARE TRYING TO BE.
No aesthetic penalties. Signs of hurried AI work matter only as actual product-quality
problems (incoherence, broken flow, placeholder content), never as style.

## WebMCP Leverage — what does WebMCP change for this product?
Judge the delta WebMCP makes to reliability, precision, structured state access,
shared human-agent state, repeatability, and capability. Tool COUNT is not leverage.
- 9-10 TRANSFORMATIVE: the intended experience is impractical without the structured
  agent surface; human and agent share live product state; the collaboration could not
  be replicated comparably well by a general agent driving the UI.
- 7-8 MAJOR DELTA: without WebMCP a general agent is markedly less reliable/precise
  here; the agent is central to the product, not a bolt-on.
- 5-6 MEANINGFUL DELTA: existing capability packaged into a genuinely better product
  experience via structured tools; same outcome otherwise possible with real friction.
- 3-4 MINOR DELTA: generic browser automation achieves comparable outcomes; WebMCP is
  a convenience layer.
- 1-2 COSMETIC: exposes an action a UI button already offers; no meaningful change.

## Execution — is the actual product coherent, intentional, complete for its scope?
Stage 1 scores EXECUTION_CLAIMED: coherence/intentionality/completeness as evidenced by
the packet (description + packaging + video frames/transcript). Thin evidence caps the
score (end-to-end credit requires end-to-end evidence) and lowers confidence.
Stage 2 may rescore as EXECUTION_OBSERVED; latest evidence-backed score replaces, never
sums.
- 9-10: all surfaces agree; central workflow demonstrably completes; states handled;
  feels finished for its stated scope.
- 7-8: strong coherence and intentionality; central workflow appears functional; minor
  unfinished edges.
- 5-6: plausibly real with visible gaps; scope unclear or core not demonstrated
  end-to-end.
- 3-4: thin prototype; contradictions between surfaces; placeholder content.
- 1-2: non-functional or incoherent for its claimed scope.

## Creativity & Ambition — novelty of concept and interaction model
Judge ONLY: novelty of the concept, novelty of the interaction model, originality
relative to existing concepts, ambition, memorable/surprising use of the medium, and
the depth with which the concept is pursued. WebMCP indispensability belongs under
WebMCP Leverage, NOT here: a project can be extremely creative even if another agent
API could theoretically have enabled something similar.
- 9-10: genuinely novel concept or interaction model; ambitious scope pursued deeply
  and coherently; memorable.
- 7-8: fresh synthesis or notably original interaction; ambitious for a hackathon.
- 5-6: competent take on a known category with a real new angle.
- 3-4: derivative; novelty mostly surface details.
- 1-2: clone/template with trivial changes.
"That the category exists" is never a penalty; judge the submitted concept.

## Potential Impact — credible value for the intended audience
Judge: is there a real audience; is there a real problem, desire, or use case for that
audience; does the demonstrated solution genuinely address it. NO commercial adoption,
monetization, market pull, or startup viability is required. Entertainment, play, art,
creative expression, learning, niche hobby use, experimentation, and community value
are legitimate forms of impact. A tiny audience fully justifies a top score.
- 9-10: specific real audience with a real problem/desire; solution demonstrably
  addresses it; credible value, appeal, usefulness, or meaning for that audience.
- 7-8: clear audience and problem, good fit, credible value.
- 5-6: identifiable audience, looser fit or partial address.
- 3-4: speculative audience or strained fit; impact mostly aspirational.
- 1-2: no coherent audience/problem pairing.
Audience size is NOT a multiplier.

aggregate = leverage + execution + impact + creativity (4-40). Nothing else enters.

combined evidence confidence, then slug alphabetical. See PROTOCOL.md Ranking section.

Like 1 Comment Story Updates Inspiration Many schools in Indonesia teach chemistry without a laboratory that is safe or complete enough for students to run experiments themselves. Teachers describe reactions on a whiteboard, and students memorise colour changes they have never actually seen. The missing piece is not information. It is the instructor standing beside you who pours the reagent, points at the bubbles as they form, and stops you before you make an unsafe combination. We wanted to find out whether an AI agent could take that role, and WebMCP is the first thing that makes it possible. A chatbot can describe a reaction. With WebMCP, it can run one. What it does EZI ChemLab is a 3D chemistry and physics lab that an AI agent drives from inside the browser. The student talks to the agent, and the agent operates the bench. There are three practical modes, mapped to Phases D, E and F of the Indonesian national science curriculum: Acids and bases , with indicator colour change and gas evolution Electrolyte and non-electrolyte solutions , tested with a conductivity lamp Density and buoyancy , the classic egg in fresh water against salt water The agent can switch topics, mix substances, read the current state of the bench, explain each stage, raise an educational safety warning, reset the experiment, and write up the observation report at the end. Reagents are not teleported into the beaker. Each one is measured out into its own graduated cylinder first, or lifted on a spatula if it is a powder, and only then poured in one at a time. The amount_ml the agent asked for is something you can read off the glass rather than take on trust. How we built it Seven tools are registered client side through the browser model context API: switch_experiment_mode , mix_substances , get_lab_state , explain_reaction_step , trigger_safety_alert , reset_experiment and render_lab_report . The frontend is Next.js with React Three Fiber. Every object on the page is built from geometric primitives in code, with no third party assets: the glassware is turned on a lathe from a profile the way real glassware is, and the floor tiles and the room the glass reflects are drawn to a canvas at runtime. Bubbles are capped at 110 instances so the simulation runs on the standard school laptops we are aiming at. The backend is Rust with axum, acting as a proxy so that no API key ever reaches the client. One decision shaped everything else: reaction outcomes come from a static lookup table, never from a language model. The visual result of a reaction is a scientific fact, and a hallucinated colour change would teach a student something false. The model writes prose. The table decides the chemistry. Challenges we ran into The WebMCP specification moved the API from navigator.modelContext to document.modelContext in July 2026, and browsers in the field are still split across both locations. Every registration goes through a resolver that checks both, because a page that registers nothing looks exactly like a page that was never built. Refracting glass nearly cost us the experiment. The renderer builds the buffer that transmissive glass reads from opaque objects only, so anything marked transparent inside a beaker is not merely faint, it is absent: the liquid, the bubbles and the electrodes all disappeared behind the very glass that was meant to show them. Worse, our whole browser test suite stayed green while the beaker was empty on screen, because every check ran at 900 pixels and refraction only switches on from 1024 up. We now have a check that runs wide, hides the interface panels so that a text update cannot stand in for a reaction, and insists the contents are actually visible. We also had to design for a judging period that runs for weeks. An API quota running out in mid September would leave the lab looking broken, so step explanations are generated once, reviewed for scientific accuracy, and served as static content. Only the lab report, which depends on what the student actually did, calls a model at run time, and the backend accepts several numbered providers and moves to the next one when a call fails. That is not a claim on paper: in production the first provider currently fails on every request and the second answers, and the report still arrives. Every remaining failure path returns vetted static text with a 200 response rather than an error. Accomplishments that we're proud of Every execute handler returns a structured result containing the visual state, the temperature change and a plain language observation, so the agent narrates what actually happened instead of guessing. Unknown reagent pairs return a structured error listing the valid pairs, which lets the agent correct itself without the student ever noticing. We also built the lab so that it stays usable when WebMCP is not available. A status badge reports what the browser supports, a manual panel calls the exact same handlers rather than mock versions, and the topic buttons in the header run switch_experiment_mode the same way an agent does. The tool layer is genuinely separate from the interface. What we learned Reading matters as much as writing. get_lab_state returns the same view of the bench that the interface shows, so the agent can check what is actually there before it acts rather than assuming its last call succeeded. Together with the structured errors, it is what keeps a long conversation from drifting away from the simulation. We also learned that visible tool call indicators are not decoration. Every call is written to an activity console with the tool name, the outcome, and whether it came from an agent or was run by hand. Watching that line appear at the exact moment the liquid changes colour is what makes the whole thing legible, both to a student and to anyone evaluating it. What's next for EZI ChemLab Reaction rate is the next topic, and it is the one that rewards an agent most. It has four variables to manipulate and two beakers to compare, so the agent stops triggering effects and starts designing an experiment. Thermochemistry follows closely behind, since temperature data already flows through mix_substances . Beyond that: teacher accounts with printable worksheets, offline packaging for schools with limited connectivity, and coverage extending into Phase F electrochemistry. Like Comment Story Updates Inspiration Complex cloud architecture and migration decisions are rarely solved by a single recommendation. Teams need to compare costs, technical constraints, operational trade-offs, and implementation risks before committing to a strategy. We built StrategyShifu to explore a better model for human–agent collaboration: an AI agent that can work directly with a live decision-making application through WebMCP, while the user can see every change, review the reasoning, and make the final decision. What it does StrategyShifu is a WebMCP-powered decision workspace for cloud cost planning and data-platform migration strategy. The current version supports two decision workflows: Databricks cost planning: Configure DWH, ETL, and development workloads and compare compute strategies using regional pricing, schedules, networking requirements, Databricks Genie compatibility, and project budgets. Redshift-to-Databricks migration planning: Compare Iceberg coexistence, Lakehouse Federation, UNLOAD to S3, and JDBC-based migration strategies using workload characteristics, downtime tolerance, synchronization plans, target formats, and execution readiness. Through WebMCP, an agent can inspect the current project, modify inputs, test alternatives, calculate costs, compare strategies, and explain the recommendation. Every agent action is reflected immediately in the interface, allowing the user and the agent to work from the same decision context. How we built it StrategyShifu is built with Next.js, React, and TypeScript. WebMCP forms the interaction layer between the application and browser-based AI agents. We followed several WebMCP best practices: Registered focused, task-oriented tools using document.modelContext.registerTool() . Designed tools around user goals instead of exposing low-level UI actions. Used clear tool names, descriptions, and input schemas. Added runtime validation for agent-provided inputs. Returned both human-readable text and structured content. Included tool annotations to communicate read-only and mutating behavior. Used AbortSignal cleanup to prevent duplicate or stale tool registrations. Kept tool operations deterministic and safe. Used stable identifiers for selecting and updating workloads. Returned updated state after mutations so agents can continue without scraping the page. Provided clear errors and recovery guidance for invalid operations. Gracefully preserved the human interface when WebMCP was unavailable. Most importantly, WebMCP tools and human controls operate on the same React state. There is no separate agent-only state or hidden decision model. The application currently exposes: 17 WebMCP tools for workload configuration, pricing, cost estimation, strategy comparison, and recommendation retrieval. 7 WebMCP tools for migration inputs, capability evidence, and Redshift-to-Databricks assessment. The recommendation engines remain deterministic and catalog-driven. The agent explores and configures scenarios, while the application owns calculations, validation, compatibility gates, scoring, and ranking. Challenges we ran into One of the main challenges was designing a WebMCP tool surface that represented meaningful decision operations rather than browser automation. For example, instead of asking an agent to find and click several form fields, StrategyShifu provides tools such as add_workload , set_budget , compare_strategies , and get_recommendation . This makes agent interactions more reliable, understandable, and reusable. Another challenge was synchronizing agent actions with human interactions. We solved this by connecting WebMCP tools directly to the same state update functions used by the visible interface. We also had to balance agent flexibility with predictable recommendations. Agents can explore many scenarios, but technical compatibility, budget enforcement, pricing calculations, and migration scoring must remain reproducible and auditable. Finally, migration decisions depend on evolving platform capabilities. We introduced structured capability evidence with documented-source links and a fallback mechanism so temporary documentation failures do not prevent an assessment. Accomplishments that we're proud of We are proud that StrategyShifu demonstrates WebMCP as a foundation for genuine collaboration rather than a collection of isolated agent commands. Key accomplishments include: A shared live workspace where humans and agents operate on the same state. 24 task-oriented WebMCP tools across cost and migration planning. Structured tool responses that support multi-step agent workflows. Runtime input validation and actionable error messages. Safe tool registration with lifecycle cleanup. Deterministic and explainable recommendation engines. Full-project cost analysis across multiple workloads. Explicit Pass/Fail gates for budget and technical requirements. Transparent DBU and infrastructure cost breakdowns. Migration strategy scoring, alternatives, risks, and readiness evaluation. Suggested implementation steps and production cutover checks. Giving agents semantic tools is more dependable than asking them to infer application state from the visual interface. Structured inputs and outputs also make multi-step reasoning easier to validate and debug. We also learned that agentic interaction should be separated from business logic. WebMCP provides the agent with controlled access to the application, while deterministic engines remain responsible for calculations and recommendations. Finally, transparency is essential. Users are more likely to trust an agent-assisted recommendation when they can see the inputs, understand the failed requirements, compare alternatives, and reproduce the result themselves. One scene, two editors, shape ideas together Like Comment Story Updates Inspiration OpenAI’s WebMCP showcase inspired me to explore what agent collaboration could look like inside a 3D modeling application. I wanted to build something tangible: a scene people could directly inspect, edit, and reshape while an agent works through the same interface. What it does SynMod is a local-first 3D modeling workspace where one person and a browser agent can edit the same open project. People can model through the visual editor, while an agent uses WebMCP tools registered by the page. Both can add primitives, adjust transforms and PBR materials, organize objects, perform non-destructive Boolean operations, and use the same undoable history. Why WebMCP fits SynMod 3D modeling combines visual judgment with precise, structured operations. A person may decide that a rover’s cab looks too small, while an agent can translate that direction into exact changes to position, scale, geometry, or material. WebMCP connects those two ways of working. SynMod registers its modeling capabilities directly with the browser through document.modelContext.registerTool(...) . This lets ChatGPT inspect and modify the open scene using tools defined by the application. SynMod does not need an embedded chatbot or a separate OpenAI API key. The person and agent operate on the same scene state. A person can select and reshape one component through the visual editor while the agent adds or refines another component. Together, people and agents can: Find objects by name and inspect their dimensions and transforms. Add boxes, spheres, cylinders, cones, and toruses. Move, rotate, scale, rename, recolor, duplicate, group, or hide objects. Adjust PBR properties such as roughness, metalness, opacity, and emission. Create non-destructive Boolean unions, subtractions, and intersections. Change scene lighting, exposure, shadows, and environment presets. WebMCP is useful here because the agent does not need to guess where to click or manipulate the canvas indirectly. It receives focused modeling tools, stable object references, validation errors, scene revisions, and structured results. The person remains responsible for creative direction and visual evaluation, while the agent can handle precise or repetitive scene operations How we built it I developed SynMod with ChatGPT and Codex as coding collaborators. I supplied the product direction, visual references, feature priorities, and feedback while the agents helped implement, test, debug, and deploy the application. The editor uses React, Three.js, React Three Fiber, Drei, Zustand, IndexedDB, and WebMCP. Human controls and agent tools share one validated command layer so their edits follow the same rules for history, autosave, activity records, and undo/redo. Challenges we ran into One of the hardest parts was deciding which modeling features were important enough for the first release. I also needed to determine how to demonstrate real collaboration instead of showing an agent making isolated changes. Technical challenges included keeping human and agent operations consistent, handling edits made close together, validating multi-step transactions before committing them, and making dropdowns, panels, and 3D controls behave reliably across browsers. Accomplishments that we're proud of A person and a browser agent can work on different parts of the same live scene without using separate copies of the project. Agent changes appear in the viewport, are identified in the activity history, and can be undone or redone by the person. Multi-part requests can also be applied as one validated transaction and one undo step. Simple modeling requests often require only one WebMCP tool call after the agent identifies the target. What we learned Exposing focused, well-described tools to a browser agent can make modeling requests more reliable than asking the agent to manipulate the interface indirectly. Designing concise tools reduces unnecessary reads and round trips, but it does not eliminate that variation.
