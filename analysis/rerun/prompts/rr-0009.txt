# FROZEN SCORING RUBRIC (pre-registered before any Stage 1 review)

Scores are integers 1-10 on each of the FOUR official criteria. Every score carries:
rationale, evidence_surfaces, confidence (0-1). Reviewers use the full scale and these
band anchors verbatim. Category neutrality is absolute: a game, art piece, music tool,
CRM, weird toy, and developer tool are all eligible for 10/10 on every criterion if they
satisfy that criterion exceptionally well FOR WHAT THEY ARE TRYING TO BE.
No aesthetic penalties. Signs of hurried AI work matter only as actual product-quality
problems (incoherence, broken flow, placeholder content), never as style.

## WebMCP Leverage — what does WebMCP change for this product?
Judge the delta WebMCP makes to reliability, precision, structured state access,
shared human-agent state, repeatability, and capability. Tool COUNT is not leverage.
- 9-10 TRANSFORMATIVE: the intended experience is impractical without the structured
  agent surface; human and agent share live product state; the collaboration could not
  be replicated comparably well by a general agent driving the UI.
- 7-8 MAJOR DELTA: without WebMCP a general agent is markedly less reliable/precise
  here; the agent is central to the product, not a bolt-on.
- 5-6 MEANINGFUL DELTA: existing capability packaged into a genuinely better product
  experience via structured tools; same outcome otherwise possible with real friction.
- 3-4 MINOR DELTA: generic browser automation achieves comparable outcomes; WebMCP is
  a convenience layer.
- 1-2 COSMETIC: exposes an action a UI button already offers; no meaningful change.

## Execution — is the actual product coherent, intentional, complete for its scope?
Stage 1 scores EXECUTION_CLAIMED: coherence/intentionality/completeness as evidenced by
the packet (description + packaging + video frames/transcript). Thin evidence caps the
score (end-to-end credit requires end-to-end evidence) and lowers confidence.
Stage 2 may rescore as EXECUTION_OBSERVED; latest evidence-backed score replaces, never
sums.
- 9-10: all surfaces agree; central workflow demonstrably completes; states handled;
  feels finished for its stated scope.
- 7-8: strong coherence and intentionality; central workflow appears functional; minor
  unfinished edges.
- 5-6: plausibly real with visible gaps; scope unclear or core not demonstrated
  end-to-end.
- 3-4: thin prototype; contradictions between surfaces; placeholder content.
- 1-2: non-functional or incoherent for its claimed scope.

## Creativity & Ambition — novelty of concept and interaction model
Judge ONLY: novelty of the concept, novelty of the interaction model, originality
relative to existing concepts, ambition, memorable/surprising use of the medium, and
the depth with which the concept is pursued. WebMCP indispensability belongs under
WebMCP Leverage, NOT here: a project can be extremely creative even if another agent
API could theoretically have enabled something similar.
- 9-10: genuinely novel concept or interaction model; ambitious scope pursued deeply
  and coherently; memorable.
- 7-8: fresh synthesis or notably original interaction; ambitious for a hackathon.
- 5-6: competent take on a known category with a real new angle.
- 3-4: derivative; novelty mostly surface details.
- 1-2: clone/template with trivial changes.
"That the category exists" is never a penalty; judge the submitted concept.

## Potential Impact — credible value for the intended audience
Judge: is there a real audience; is there a real problem, desire, or use case for that
audience; does the demonstrated solution genuinely address it. NO commercial adoption,
monetization, market pull, or startup viability is required. Entertainment, play, art,
creative expression, learning, niche hobby use, experimentation, and community value
are legitimate forms of impact. A tiny audience fully justifies a top score.
- 9-10: specific real audience with a real problem/desire; solution demonstrably
  addresses it; credible value, appeal, usefulness, or meaning for that audience.
- 7-8: clear audience and problem, good fit, credible value.
- 5-6: identifiable audience, looser fit or partial address.
- 3-4: speculative audience or strained fit; impact mostly aspirational.
- 1-2: no coherent audience/problem pairing.
Audience size is NOT a multiplier.

## Aggregate & ranking
aggregate = leverage + execution + impact + creativity (4-40). Nothing else enters.
Tie-breaks: Leverage, then Execution, then Impact, then Creativity, then higher
combined evidence confidence, then slug alphabetical. See PROTOCOL.md Ranking section.

Before they ever go live, they may have to manage chat commands, alerts, timed promotions, overlays, clipping highlights for TikTok, and countless other pieces of production across a dozen different tabs. Meanwhile, AI agents are still largely stuck outside the product. They screen-scrape interfaces, guess which buttons to click, and operate without a real understanding of the application's state. We wanted to change that. StreamBot is a real streamer dashboard already in production use. The WebMCP Challenge gave us the missing piece: instead of bolting an AI chatbot onto the side of the product, we gave the agent a first-class seat inside the application . The agent uses the same tools as the streamer, operates against the same state, and leaves the same audit trail. That led to the question driving this project: What does setup feel like when a streamer and their AI agent share one workspace, while the human remains in control of anything that matters? What It Does Three experiences define StreamBot Agent Edition. 1. Propose, Don't Just Do The agent isn't simply given unrestricted control. A streamer can say: "Set up my horror night." Instead of immediately changing their dashboard, the agent creates a complete themed proposal: !scary and !brave commands 👻 Follow alerts Timed promotional messages A JUMPSCARE channel-point reward A follower goal bar Other relevant stream configuration The entire setup appears as a review card . The streamer can inspect the proposal, remove anything they don't want, and then hit Approve . Only after approval does the agent execute the changes. Proposal → Review → Approval → Execution It's collaboration with a veto, rather than blind automation. 2. Guides That Teach on the Live UI Agents shouldn't teach users from outdated screenshots or hallucinated interfaces. If a streamer asks: "How do chat alerts work?" The agent first uses discover_targets to inspect the actual DOM and identify the elements currently available on the page. It then uses compose_tour to build a spotlight walkthrough directly against those discovered elements. The result is a guide that operates on the real product, in its current state . No stale screenshots. No guessed buttons. No hallucinated UI. The agent discovers the interface first, then teaches from reality. 3. Skilled Hands, Not Chat The agent isn't limited to answering questions. Inside StreamBot's ClipIt editor, it can stage vertical social edits directly against the mounted editor state. That includes: Vertical layouts Facecam resizing Caption placement Real transcribed captions Designer-created caption presets Editor configuration The agent isn't pretending to edit a clip through text. It's operating the actual editor. How We Built It with WebMCP Route-Scoped Tool Suites Each StreamBot dashboard route registers the tools relevant to the feature currently being viewed. Examples include: Commands Alerts Timed Messages Rewards Canvas ClipIt This is implemented through src/webmcp/suites.ts . The agent only sees tools that are valid for the current context and available to its seat. The Agent Uses the Same API as the UI We didn't create a separate "AI version" of StreamBot's functionality. Every agent tool calls the same endpoints used by the human interface . That means the resulting state is identical regardless of who performed the action. For example: Agent creates !scary → the command immediately appears in the streamer's command list. There is no synchronization layer or second source of truth. Agent actions and human actions operate on the same application state. Tool executions are also written to the account's audit log, just like actions performed by other members of the team. A Bridge into Live Editors For ClipIt, we needed the agent to interact with a mounted editor rather than a traditional API. We built mountClipItEditorCommands , which connects the WebMCP suite directly to the ClipIt store. The bridge dispatches real reducer actions and waits for state convergence before reporting success. That seemed reasonable until we encountered a simple case: A facecam was already in the requested position. The operation succeeded, but because the pixels didn't change, the bridge waited 12 seconds and eventually reported a timeout. We rewrote the system around state convergence instead. A no-op move is now considered a successful operation if the editor is already in the requested state. We also added fail-fast validation using valid-ID lists. 2. Making the Demo Deterministic Transcription and streaming are inherently live, variable, and slow. A two-minute demo can't wait around for production systems to finish processing. We built a simulated backend using: An in-browser API shim A simulated stream engine Deterministic application state This gives us a world that behaves like the real product without depending on unpredictable external timing. Every walkthrough begins with discover_targets . The tour is then composed against what actually exists. What We Learned The biggest lesson was simple: Agents Don't Need More Autonomy. They Need Better Seats. Once the agent shared the human's: Tools Application state Permissions Audit trail Context the interaction fundamentally changed. It stopped feeling like: "Prompt and pray." And started feeling like genuine teamwork: Proposal → Review → Approval → Execution The guardrails aren't friction. Scoped tool suites, approval cards, permission boundaries, and discover-before-teach aren't there to make the agent weaker. They're what make the agent trustworthy enough to delegate to . That's the direction we believe the agent-native web should move toward: AI agents shouldn't sit outside applications, guessing how to use them. They should have a real seat inside the product with the right tools, the right context, and a human who stays in control. Like Comment Story Updates Inspiration WebMCP made the web callable by agents, and it works. But reading through the demos, one thing kept recurring. Here is a real registration from a well-built storefront: useWebMCP({ name: "checkout", description: "Place the order for everything in the cart. Irreversible. Only call when the user explicitly asks to buy.", annotations: { readOnlyHint: false }, execute: async () => placeOrder() }); The author knew exactly how dangerous that call was and said so. But a WebMCP tool descriptor supports exactly two annotations — readOnlyHint and untrustedContentHint — so the warning had nowhere to go except an English sentence. Nothing enforces it. No agent can check it before acting. It isn't testable in CI. I wanted to know how widespread that was. So I measured it. What it does I ran a census. Every public WebMCP repository discoverable from awesome-webmcp , the spec repo, and GoogleChromeLabs, scanned with one command: 75 tools across 5 applications. 49 mutate state — book_table , submit_order , confirm_order , delete_note . Zero express enforceable risk. Two more findings fell out: 21 call sites still use navigator.modelContext while Chrome's docs specify document.modelContext , and there are at least four distinct registration patterns with no common point where anyone could ask "is this tool safe to expose?" I shipped the missing layer. @aicorg/webmcp registers tools through the real document.modelContext API but refuses to register a consequential tool with no authorization, no confirmation metadata, and no completion verifier. Those semantics are then published in /.well-known/agent.json so an agent can read them before it calls anything: "webmcp": { "api": "document.modelContext", "tools": [{ "name": "complete_checkout", "risk": "critical", "requires_confirmation": true, "requires_permission": "checkout.submit_order", "read_only": false }] } I retrofitted it onto somebody else's real app. Not a toy. Crema & Co. is a Wasp storefront with 16 WebMCP tools, a product catalog, cart, coupons and order history. I governed 2 of its 16 tools and left 14 exactly as its author wrote them: Tool Risk Confirmation Verified by checkout critical required order id returned, and the cart is empty afterwards apply_coupon medium none the returned cart carries the requested coupon code the other 14 — — untouched The gradient is the point. apply_coupon moves money and is reversible; checkout charges and is not. A layer that marked all 16 critical would be unusable. Then I let Claude Code loose on the store through Chrome DevTools MCP. It discovers the tools itself. It searched the catalog, checked a water filter actually fits the machine, found a coupon and filled a cart — five tool calls, no permission asked, because none of that is consequential. Then it tried to check out and stopped, because checkout declares risk: critical . Decline the confirmation and the order is refused with the cart still holding both items. Like Comment Story Updates Inspiration For as much as marketers have optimized for conversion, they haven't been able to cut through the number one reason that people leave open carts. they're not ready to buy. That's just one glimpse of how web-interfaces of the future will adapt to their users' needs through a Live Canvas. What it does Each demo walks you through a conventional user interface, and then cordons off a bounded portion of it as WebMCP to redesign (the Live Canvas) The Storefront demo lets the agent curate the search results with a toolbox of approved card presets and badges. This constrained design is a good fit for the untrusted nature of the public internet, keeping users safe from XSS attacks. The Dashbord demo scales the idea up by giving you an entirely blank page. It imagine how the future of CRM / ERP dashboards might function. Prompt the agent to onboard you, collaborate on a vision with the agent as you would with your analytics team, and the end result is an entirely personal dashboard of controls, visualizations and reports. The Distribution demo scales the idea outwards by asking whether you distribute a Live Canvas as part of conventional component libraries. It demonstrates what a future Datatable component could be, with the ability to generate bespoke reports and save them to your browser - all without backend engineering or ai-vendor lock-in. How we built it The architecture north star is "agent composition, application execution". The application exposes its UI primitives through WebMCP. The agent supplies a composition, and then the live canvas renders that composition using application-owned components. This solution prevents data from leaking out of the organization's security boundary, and also prevents token bloat. Challenges we ran into and accomplishments that we're proud of There are two large challenges that required creativity: The largest challenge was discoverability. WebMCP is not bidirectional: an agent can invoke tools, but there's no mechanism for the website to initiate a conversation. The past 30 years of the web have trained users for the affordance of buttons and forms. I built a new set of visible prompts and verbs to communicate to surface the new modality which was an interesting accomplishment, but does highlight an apparent shortcoming in the standard. The second challenge is the built-in approval flow of agentic browsers. We wanted a space where people could first think naturally—dropping in notes, links, files, drawings, equations, and unfinished ideas—and let organization emerge later. We also wanted AI collaboration to feel native to the interface. Instead of making an agent interpret every interaction from screenshots or manipulate the page through coordinates, Mundaneum uses WebMCP to expose the board’s actual content and permitted actions directly to the agent. What it does Mundaneum is an infinite visual research board designed for people and AI agents to work on together. People can add notes, links, images, documents, media, files, widgets, freehand drawings, YouTube videos, Spotify songs, PDFs, Excel, and expandable document canvases. Document canvases combine positioned text, handwriting, shapes, and equations into a single visual document that a multimodal model can inspect as an image. Users can select a card or Shift-click multiple cards to establish the current focus. WebMCP returns those selected items first, allowing ChatGPT to work on a specific group without scanning an entire large board. Selected drawings are provided as a cropped image, while cards retain their structured text and metadata. Users can also copy a card’s exact ID and reference it directly in a prompt. Through WebMCP, an agent can: Read the board and its current selection View a document canvas or the entire board as a PNG Add provisional cards Connect related cards Group and label information Annotate cards visually Identify and merge likely duplicates Request grid, masonry, cluster, tree, row, or column arrangements Create sketches without controlling their final position The central rule is that agents can contribute meaning, but they cannot provide coordinates. The page remains responsible for placement, collision detection, and layout. How we built it Mundaneum is a React and TypeScript application powered by Vite. Board state is stored locally in IndexedDB, while Cloudflare Workers and Durable Objects support sharing, capture, and synchronization. The WebMCP layer registers imperative tools through document.modelContext . The same internal tool definitions are used by the visible application, ensuring that agent actions follow the same rules as human actions. The board performs its own organization work in the browser: Transformers.js generates semantic embeddings in a Web Worker. Pairwise similarity creates a graph of related cards. Louvain community detection identifies clusters. D3 Force calculates spatial arrangements. Collision detection and empty-space placement prevent cards and widgets from overlapping. An RBush spatial index supports efficient selection and region queries. Rich media uses a lightweight facade until the user activates it, preventing large boards from loading many expensive embeds simultaneously. Documents and spreadsheets are parsed through lazy-loaded libraries, while drawing and document canvases can be rasterized into PNG images for multimodal agents. Stylus input uses Pointer Events, pressure data, pointer capture, coalesced events, palm rejection behavior, and hardware-eraser handling to improve writing on tablets. ChatGPT, OpenAI Codex, and Anthropic Claude Code were used during development for research, implementation, debugging, testing, and documentation. Challenges we ran into One of the hardest problems was deciding how much information should be structured data and how much should remain visual. Text cards are easier for an agent to process structurally, but handwriting, equations, diagrams, and overlapping annotations lose meaning when converted into isolated text. Our solution was hybrid: preserve structured card data while supplying selected drawings and document canvases as images. Selection also required careful design. On a large board, returning everything equally can waste the model’s context and make focused requests unreliable. We made human selection an explicit attention signal, returning selected cards first and combining selected drawings into a single cropped image. Spatial layout created another challenge. Simple force-directed layouts often produced overlapping clusters, particularly in masonry, cluster, and tree arrangements. We separated semantic intent from geometry: agents request an arrangement, while the browser calculates positions, measures real card dimensions, and searches for available space. Tablet drawing introduced a different class of input problems. Pen input, touch gestures, palm contact, scrolling, erasing, and card dragging can all compete for the same pointer events. Supporting pressure-sensitive writing without breaking ordinary mouse and touch interaction required dedicated pointer handling and repeated device-oriented testing. Finally, converting the complete board into an agent-readable image was more complicated than returning its SVG representation. We added real PNG rasterization so multimodal models receive an actual screenshot-like image containing cards, links, drawings, and spatial relationships. Accomplishments that we're proud of We are proud that Mundaneum treats WebMCP as part of the product’s interaction model rather than as a thin demonstration layer. The agent operates on meaningful concepts—cards, selections, links, groups, drawings, and arrangements—without controlling screen coordinates. This creates a clear division of responsibility: the person directs attention, the agent contributes understanding, and the application protects the visual workspace. We are also proud of the document canvas. It allows typed text, handwriting, shapes, and equations to coexist in one movable and resizable document while remaining readable by a multimodal agent. Other accomplishments include: Selection-aware agent context Multi-card selection through Shift-click Image-based transfer of selected drawings Whole-board PNG capture Copyable card IDs Non-overlapping card placement Resizable document and widget cards Pressure-sensitive stylus support Multiple browser-computed arrangements A shared tool registry for the interface and WebMCP A public, MIT-licensed implementation What we learned We learned that good agent interfaces are less about exposing every possible action and more about defining strong boundaries. Preventing agents from setting coordinates initially appeared restrictive, but it simplified the tool surface and made agent contributions safer. The agent expresses intent, while deterministic browser code handles geometry. We also learned that visual and structured context should complement each other. Screenshots are excellent for handwriting, diagrams, and spatial meaning, while structured data is better for exact text, IDs, links, and targeted actions. Combining both creates a much stronger interface than relying exclusively on either representation. Human selection proved to be a powerful form of context engineering. A simple Shift-click can communicate scope more reliably than a long prompt explaining which objects matter. Finally, pen input demonstrated how much interaction design depends on hardware. A drawing system that works with a mouse is not automatically usable with a stylus and touchscreen. Part of that registration process involved finalizing which classes I would take, and which ones I would not. There were three classes, all of which I was interested in taking, all scheduled for the same time. I spent days deliberating which class to take, which class would teach me the most, and which class would just leave me with the highest feeling of FOMO. I later mentioned this to my friend, and we discussed tradeoffs, compared value vs. effort vs. interest. This structured process made the decision way more straightforward to make. I've noticed that I tend to deliberate endlessly when I have many options to choose from (whether it is deciding what classes to take, what laptop to buy, or how to spend my afternoon). I thought that if there was a tool to help me structure and visualize my decision making process, I would greatly benefit from it. So, I decided to build Decision Desk :). It has already streamlined the decision making process in my life, and I truly believe this tool will be immensely beneficial to many others. What it does Decision Desk gives its users a visual space to structure a decision, instead of keeping all of the options and tradeoffs in their head. Users can create a "Desk" for a given problem or situation that they need to address. They can then add columns to the desk representing various decisions that they can take. users can add virtual sticky notes of pros and cons for each option, and unresolved considerations that they value. Pros and cons can be weighted based on how important they are, and users can pin certain judgments that matter especially strongly to them. Where Decision Desk becomes more interesting is through WebMCP. An AI agent can inspect the same Desk the user is looking at, understand the options and tradeoffs that the user has already added, add new options that the user might not have thought about, contribute pros and cons, and pose unresolved questions or considerations the user may have missed. The user can then accept, edit, remove, reweight, or pin the agents' contributions. The goal is not for the AI to make the decision, but to help structure it for the user. The agent contributes reasoning and perspective, while the user remains in control of what actually matters to them. How I built it I started Decision Desk by first coming up with the higher level idea of what I wanted the application to be. From there, I worked with Codex to break that idea down into a staged implementation plan. Each stage had a specific goal, a set of tasks, and some exit criteria that had to be satisfied before I moved on. I would refine a stage, implement it, test and verify it, and only move onto the next stage once I was happy with how it worked. This ended up being a really useful way to build the project because I always had a relatively small problem in front of me instead of the entire application at once. I have also included the original Plan.md in the repository. Keep in mind that several design changes were made during implementation, so the Plan.md isn't an active source of truth for the codebase. Decision Desk itself is a fully client-side React and TypeScript application built with Vite and deployed as a static site through Cloudflare Pages. I intentionally avoided adding a backend, database, or authentication system as it is intended to be a lightweight tool. Desks are persisted locally in the browser using localStorage, and that saved state is validated before the application restores it. Probably the most important architectural decision I made was having the React interface and WebMCP share the exact same application state and business logic. I created a central store and action layer containing operations such as addOption, addPro, addCon, setThoughtWeight, pinThought, and addConsideration. React does not directly mutate the underlying state, and the WebMCP tools do not implement their own separate version of these operations. If I click "add pro" in the interface or an agent calls the add_pro tool, they both eventually call the same underlying action. State updates are then published through useSyncExternalStore. Because of this, something I change manually is immediately visible the next time an agent reads the Desk, and something the agent changes immediately appears in front of me. Decision Desk exposes 21 WebMCP tools using document.modelContext.registerTool, with 7 read tools and 14 mutation tools. The read tools allow an agent to inspect things like the current Desk, options, thoughts, weights, pins, unresolved considerations, tradeoffs, and the current lean. The mutation tools allow the agent to actually contribute to that same Desk by adding and modifying options, pros, cons, weights, pins, and considerations. I also spent quite a bit of time thinking about trust between the human and the agent. Agent-created content is marked with an agent tag so that it is always possible to tell what came from the agent. Humans can pin thoughts that are especially important to them, and the WebMCP tool descriptions tell agents to respect those pinned judgments. I also settled on a simple rule for uncertainty: if something is known, it can become a pro or con, but if something is unknown, it should become an unresolved consideration instead. The scoring system follows the same philosophy of keeping things simple. An option's score is just the sum of its pro weights minus the sum of its con weights, and Decision Desk presents this as a "lean" rather than pretending that a personal decision has some objectively correct mathematical answer. Challenges I ran into One of the biggest challenges was figuring out how to make the human and the agent actually feel like they were working on the same thing. It would have been fairly easy to build the interface normally and then bolt a few WebMCP tools onto it afterward, but I did not want the WebMCP integration to feel like a separate technical demo. The agent needed to read the exact state I was looking at, and if either of us changed something, the other needed to immediately see that change. This is what pushed me toward the shared store and action layer. It took more care when designing the state updates and APIs, but I think it made the final human-agent interaction much more natural. Another challenge was deciding how much control the agent should actually have. There is a weird problem with decision making where some pieces of information are completely objective, while some of the most important pieces are incredibly subjective. An agent can understand that one apartment has a shorter commute than another, but it cannot decide for me how much I care about living near my friends. That is where weighted thoughts and pinned judgments came from. I wanted the AI to have enough control to genuinely help me reason through a decision, while still making it clear that the human's judgment is irreplaceable. Uncertainty was another problem I had to think through. I did not want an agent to fill up a Desk with assumptions simply because those assumptions sounded plausible. Open the inbox. Scroll to find the thread you need. Re-read it to remember where it stood. Think about how to answer. Type it out. Re-read it. Send. Repeat X times. None of those steps is hard. Together they eat an hour or two every single day, and most of it isn't thinking, it's finding and typing . EmailBooster exists to give that hour back. You say one sentence, "reply to Milan and tell him to look for cheaper alternatives", and the finding, the context, and the typing are done for you, in your own words, in your own compose window, waiting for your approval. What it does EmailBooster is a mail client with no AI inside. Instead, it teaches your browser agent, via WebMCP, to do the repetitive part of email for you: Finding the thread : search_emails and resolve_contact turn "Milan's email about S3" into the right conversation in one call. No scrolling, no search box, no guessing the subject line. Reloading the context : read_thread gives the agent the whole conversation so you don't have to re-read it to remember what was said. Writing the reply in your voice : get_writing_style hands the agent a voice profile built from your sent mail. The draft sounds like you, so you edit two words instead of rewriting three paragraphs. Keeping you in control : the draft lands in the compose window, visible and editable. send_email requires your explicit approval in the UI. Every tool call is logged live in the sidebar. The daily flow becomes: read the inbox, say what you want to reply, glance at the draft, approve. Minutes instead of an hour. Why WebMCP is a strong fit Email is the most repeated task of the working day and the worst one for DOM-scraping agents: search, threading and tone all live behind the UI. WebMCP lets the mail client expose exactly the three things an agent needs to save you time, structured search, full thread context, and your writing style, as tools. No API key, no LLM inside the app, no copy-pasting your private mail into a chatbot. Mail never leaves the tab, and the agent you already have does the work. What people and agents can now do together Before, delegating email meant either doing it all yourself or handing your inbox to a bot that writes like a press release. Now the split is clean and the same every day: the human decides what to say and to whom in one sentence; the agent finds the thread, recovers the context, and writes it in the human's voice; the human edits and approves. Multiply that by every reply you send in a day, and it's the difference between an inbox that owns your morning and one you clear before coffee. How I built it Nine tools registered with document.modelContext.registerTool() (fallback to navigator.modelContext ), each with a JSON inputSchema , a description written for an agent, and an execute handler. Read-only tools ( search_emails , read_thread , get_open_email , resolve_contact , get_writing_style ) carry annotations: { readOnlyHint: true } . Write tools ( draft_reply , archive_email ) update app state and the DOM in one place, so whatever the agent does, you see it happen. Two tools are dynamic : update_draft and send_email are registered when the compose window opens and removed with unregisterTool() when it closes. The agent cannot send mail when there is nothing on screen to send. Static HTML/CSS/JavaScript, no build step, no server. The mailbox is seed data in localStorage . Hosted on GitHub Pages.
