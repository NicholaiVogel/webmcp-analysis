# FROZEN SCORING RUBRIC (pre-registered before any Stage 1 review)

Scores are integers 1-10 on each of the FOUR official criteria. Every score carries:
rationale, evidence_surfaces, confidence (0-1). Reviewers use the full scale and these
band anchors verbatim. Category neutrality is absolute: a game, art piece, music tool,
CRM, weird toy, and developer tool are all eligible for 10/10 on every criterion if they
satisfy that criterion exceptionally well FOR WHAT THEY ARE TRYING TO BE.
No aesthetic penalties. Signs of hurried AI work matter only as actual product-quality
problems (incoherence, broken flow, placeholder content), never as style.

## WebMCP Leverage — what does WebMCP change for this product?
Judge the delta WebMCP makes to reliability, precision, structured state access,
shared human-agent state, repeatability, and capability. Tool COUNT is not leverage.
- 9-10 TRANSFORMATIVE: the intended experience is impractical without the structured
  agent surface; human and agent share live product state; the collaboration could not
  be replicated comparably well by a general agent driving the UI.
- 7-8 MAJOR DELTA: without WebMCP a general agent is markedly less reliable/precise
  here; the agent is central to the product, not a bolt-on.
- 5-6 MEANINGFUL DELTA: existing capability packaged into a genuinely better product
  experience via structured tools; same outcome otherwise possible with real friction.
- 3-4 MINOR DELTA: generic browser automation achieves comparable outcomes; WebMCP is
  a convenience layer.
- 1-2 COSMETIC: exposes an action a UI button already offers; no meaningful change.

## Execution — is the actual product coherent, intentional, complete for its scope?
Stage 1 scores EXECUTION_CLAIMED: coherence/intentionality/completeness as evidenced by
the packet (description + packaging + video frames/transcript). Thin evidence caps the
score (end-to-end credit requires end-to-end evidence) and lowers confidence.
Stage 2 may rescore as EXECUTION_OBSERVED; latest evidence-backed score replaces, never
sums.
- 9-10: all surfaces agree; central workflow demonstrably completes; states handled;
  feels finished for its stated scope.
- 7-8: strong coherence and intentionality; central workflow appears functional; minor
  unfinished edges.
- 5-6: plausibly real with visible gaps; scope unclear or core not demonstrated
  end-to-end.
- 3-4: thin prototype; contradictions between surfaces; placeholder content.
- 1-2: non-functional or incoherent for its claimed scope.

## Creativity & Ambition — novelty of concept and interaction model
Judge ONLY: novelty of the concept, novelty of the interaction model, originality
relative to existing concepts, ambition, memorable/surprising use of the medium, and
the depth with which the concept is pursued. WebMCP indispensability belongs under
WebMCP Leverage, NOT here: a project can be extremely creative even if another agent
API could theoretically have enabled something similar.
- 9-10: genuinely novel concept or interaction model; ambitious scope pursued deeply
  and coherently; memorable.
- 7-8: fresh synthesis or notably original interaction; ambitious for a hackathon.
- 5-6: competent take on a known category with a real new angle.
- 3-4: derivative; novelty mostly surface details.
- 1-2: clone/template with trivial changes.
"That the category exists" is never a penalty; judge the submitted concept.

## Potential Impact — credible value for the intended audience
Judge: is there a real audience; is there a real problem, desire, or use case for that
audience; does the demonstrated solution genuinely address it. NO commercial adoption,
monetization, market pull, or startup viability is required. Entertainment, play, art,
creative expression, learning, niche hobby use, experimentation, and community value
are legitimate forms of impact. A tiny audience fully justifies a top score.
- 9-10: specific real audience with a real problem/desire; solution demonstrably
  addresses it; credible value, appeal, usefulness, or meaning for that audience.
- 7-8: clear audience and problem, good fit, credible value.
- 5-6: identifiable audience, looser fit or partial address.
- 3-4: speculative audience or strained fit; impact mostly aspirational.
- 1-2: no coherent audience/problem pairing.
Audience size is NOT a multiplier.

## Aggregate & ranking
aggregate = leverage + execution + impact + creativity (4-40). Nothing else enters.
Tie-breaks: Leverage, then Execution, then Impact, then Creativity, then higher
combined evidence confidence, then slug alphabetical. See PROTOCOL.md Ranking section.

Overview A shared browser verifier where people and AI agents inspect the same cryptographic evidence, chronology, rights assertions, and local-file match. Problem Creators, agencies, reviewers, counsel, and journalists increasingly receive public evidence records that combine cryptographic facts with human claims. Understanding one record can require moving among a verification page, machine-readable JSON, a signature, an Arweave transaction, and the original local file. A person can miss a distinction; a general-purpose agent scraping the visible page can do worse by promoting a publisher statement or authorship claim into a verified fact. The difficult part is not merely retrieving data. It is preserving evidentiary boundaries: what this browser independently checked, what the publisher reported, what the claimant asserted, what is temporarily retryable, and what the public format does not yet support. Solution UCE Evidence Lens is a static, browser-first public record inspector. A person can load a CbyUCE verification URL, a direct Arweave manifest, pasted public JSON, or the bundled demonstration. The browser validates the record, checks its public ES256 signature, binds chronology to public Arweave block metadata, separates recorded assertions from verified results, and can compare a user-selected file by locally calculated SHA-256. The same page exposes seven read-only WebMCP tools. An agent can load, summarize, verify, inspect chronology, list assertions, inspect rights declarations, and report an already-selected local file comparison through structured operations rather than guessing from the interface. The human sees the same underlying state and wording. Why This Matters Evidence review is a strong fit for WebMCP because the workflow is structured, multi-step, and precision sensitive. Without page-defined tools, an agent has to infer controls and semantics from layout, possibly leaving out technical details or overstating a legal conclusion. With WebMCP, the site defines exactly which evidence operations are available and returns typed, source-bound results. For a reviewer, the experience changes from manually correlating several technical sources to asking one focused question while retaining a visible and easily understood inspectable record. For an agency or production team, the demonstration also shows a credible market path: a work can originate in the published Copyright by UCE Figma plugin ( the Figma plugin is now available for use), become a public UCE record, and then be inspected by both a person and an agent before delivery. This does not turn evidence into a legal verdict. It makes the evidence easier to inspect without erasing its limits. How We Used AI The product is agent-enabled without placing a model inside the trust boundary. Deterministic browser functions perform parsing, cryptography, chronology retrieval, and digest comparison. WebMCP lets an AI agent select and compose those operations, explain the structured results, and guide a person through the same evidence the interface displays. The key collaboration is intentional: the agent can inspect an already-selected digest but cannot open a file picker or access file bytes. The person controls the sensitive local input; the agent works with the resulting evidence state. Like Comment Story Updates "What matched" panel showing the actual JavaScript from the checked page Inspiration We already run an index of 14,351 sites whose agent-facing capabilities are probe-verified and dated. When we turned that machinery on WebMCP, the finding was awkward: registered tools round to zero outside demos and the checker sites themselves. There were more tools for checking WebMCP than there were sites using it. So the missing thing was never another checker. It was a denominator . Nobody could tell a site what its result meant, because nobody had a population to compare it against. Then a bug decided the rest of the product. Our first detector reported a clean no tools found for a page whose script robots.txt had disallowed us from reading. It wasn't a no. It was a question we weren't allowed to ask, and reporting it as a no is the same error as counting a locked door as an empty room. Across the index that turned out to be 1,224 hosts . Every checker we could find reports those as failures. That distinction became the product. What it does Give it a hostname. It fetches the page, then opens it in a real browser, and reports three things a checker normally collapses into one: whether the site declares WebMCP in its source, whether it actually registers tools when the page executes, and — the state that matters — whether we were allowed to ask at all. Then it does the thing no other checker can: it places the answer. 647 sites in our index carry the code, 580 register something at runtime, and 997 distinct tool names have been observed across them. If you're deciding which tools to expose, the distribution is the answer — search_site on 122 sites, fetch_as_markdown on 105, search_content on 28, get_contact_info on 18, get_pricing and search_products on 17 each. Measured and dated, not guessed. Why WebMCP is the right shape for this . What anyone wants from this page is one action with one parameter: check this host. That's a tool call, not a document. An agent forced to fetch our HTML, locate the form, synthesise a POST and parse a verdict out of the markup is doing four fragile things to accomplish one simple one — and all four break the next time we change the layout. So the page declares its own WebMCP tools. An agent calls it rather than reading it. The checker is an instance of the thing it checks. What that unlocks . An agent can audit fifty hosts in a loop with no HTML parsing and no brittle selectors. More interesting is the reverse direction: "does this host expose tools I can call, and were they observed running or merely declared?" is a question an agent should ask before it interacts with an unfamiliar site. Declared-versus-verified is exactly the distinction an autonomous agent needs and rarely has. This turns it into one call, with a date attached. And a boundary that matters more as agents get more capable: we list tools, we never invoke them . Enumerating a stranger's tools is safe. Executing one could place an order, send a message, or spend someone's money. How we built it Two layers, mapping onto the verification tiers we already run. Declared — a static fetch matching navigator.modelContext, document.modelContext, provideContext and registerTool. It matches both namespaces, because the spec renamed one mid-origin-trial and a site written against the earlier draft still counts. And it matches a call, not a mention — an article explaining WebMCP doesn't get scored as a site using it. Registered — headless enumeration in a real browser via Cloudflare Browser Rendering, the tools/list equivalent. This is what separates a site that ships the code from a site where the code actually runs. 647 carry it; 580 register something. It runs on Cloudflare Workers with D1 behind it. The page's own tools are declared with the declarative form attributes — toolname, tooldescription, toolparamdescription — so this is a WebMCP surface, not a page about WebMCP. Consent is enforced before any fetch , because the tool probes third-party hosts on demand: robots.txt read before the resource request; ai-input=no treated as an absolute stop, with the host reported as excluded rather than as failing; the user-agent KnownGood-Verifier never disguised; Retry-After honoured. And running a check has zero effect on ranking in our index — listings there are earned by probe and never sold. Challenges we ran into Cloudflare Browser Rendering caps at two concurrent browsers and two new per minute, with a 60-second inactivity kill — a different problem at index scale than at demo scale. The detector took two rewrites. The first version matched modelContext inside comments, and fixing that surfaced the worse case: WebMCP discussed in HTML body prose. Separating a call from a mention is most of the work in a detector like this, and neither failure was obvious until we looked at what had matched. And the spec renamed navigator.modelContext to document.modelContext mid-origin-trial, so anything matching a single namespace silently under-counts. We match both. Accomplishments that we're proud of We shipped the state that makes our own number smaller. Adding "could not ask" means 1,224 hosts don't count toward anything, where every checker we found reports them as failures. It's the right number and it cost us the bigger one. The checker passes its own check. Run it against https://knowngood.sh/verify/webmcp and it comes back registered, with its tools enumerated. We didn't want to ship a tool telling sites to do something we hadn't done. The population produced a finding nobody else could. Treating each site's tool signature as a fingerprint puts 39.5% of the 580 registering sites in a cluster with at least one other site, and the largest single cluster is 18.1%. Most WebMCP "adoption" is one plugin — and you can only see that from a denominator. Every figure carries the population and date it was measured against. No number on the page floats free of what it was measured over. What we learned A checker that cannot distinguish "no" from "couldn't ask" is worse than no checker at all for the sites it mislabels — it manufactures a false negative and presents it with the same confidence as a real one. And adoption numbers in a young standard are mostly plugin numbers. Without the tool-signature clustering we would have reported 580 independent implementations. The truth is closer to a handful of implementations installed many times, and only a population makes that visible. What's next for Known Good WebMCP Verifier Declarative detection. The checker sees the imperative API but not toolname / tooldescription on form controls — the half of the standard a browser-side audit is most likely to score, and a real gap. When it lands, the attribute route will report as declared and never as registered . Attributes prove intent; only execution proves registration. Conflating the two is exactly the error this tool exists to avoid, and we'd rather ship the gap honestly than close it dishonestly. Like Comment Story Updates Inspiration A product can be right for a shopper and still lose the recommendation. Most commerce pages are written for people, who can infer meaning from photography, brand language and scattered specifications. Buying agents need something more explicit: product facts they can discover, verify and connect to the shopper's actual constraints. That creates a new growth problem for merchants. Optimising the human-facing page is no longer enough. Merchants also need to understand whether an agent can identify the right product, explain why it fits and carry the same approved product truth into organic discovery and paid preparation. Conversion Lab explores that problem as a real merchant workflow. WebMCP supplies the missing interaction layer: the website can expose purpose-built tools to an agent while the merchant and agent work with the same visible page state. The result is more than a chatbot or a generic automation. It is an evidence-led conversion lab for agent-mediated commerce. What it does Conversion Lab begins with a fictional Shopify-style merchant, Fieldwork Supply, and a £159 modular commuter backpack. The generic listing contains too little explicit information for our frozen buyer-intent battery and scores 0/8 . Through ten page-scoped WebMCP tools, an agent can: inspect eight merchant-controlled product and policy facts; audit organic and paid-channel readiness; create an evidence-bound product representation; test it against eight deterministic buyer needs; move the result from 0/8 to 8/8 , with no unsupported claims; stage the exact variant at a visible review checkpoint; publish the approved representation to the demo storefront; prepare a Shopify Admin update preview; generate a locally validated OpenAI product-feed CSV and a PAUSED Ads projection with £0 spend ; export a content-addressed optimisation receipt linking the evidence, evaluation, approval and channel projections; answer a shopper need constraint by constraint and update a bounded shared cart. Approval and reset are deliberately absent from the WebMCP tool surface. The public browser can record a digest-bound demo approval, but the app states clearly that this is not authenticated merchant authority. There is no live Shopify write, Ads upload, campaign activation, checkout or payment path in the public build. How we built it The reference application is built with React 19, TypeScript and Vite and deployed as a credential-free static application on Vercel. The interface contains a merchant growth studio and a shopper view backed by one shared workspace, so WebMCP actions and visible browser state remain consistent. The WebMCP layer registers ten narrowly scoped tools through document.modelContext.registerTool . Inputs reject unexpected properties, read-only operations declare their intent, and every result reports what changed, what authority was used and the safest next action. Browsers without WebMCP still receive a useful human interface and an honest fallback message. Under the interface, versioned commerce contracts preserve product identity, evidence provenance, lifecycle state, evaluation results, approval and channel projections. A versioned SHA-256 digest binds the exact product target, commercial snapshot, representation and complete evidence content before publication or export. Inputs are captured as immutable canonical snapshots so caller mutation cannot change an output after verification. The Shopify spine includes a version-pinned Admin GraphQL read adapter, product identity checks and an exact productUpdate preview. The OpenAI Ads spine derives every exported field from the approved product snapshot, validates the product-feed row locally, serialises deterministic CSV and keeps the campaign projection paused. A portable optimisation receipt packages the mutually consistent workflow into content-addressed JSON. We supported the build with unit, contract, infrastructure, adversarial and Playwright browser tests, plus change-aware and complete GitHub Actions gates. Tests cover lifecycle legality, evidence provenance, contradictory shopper constraints, approval substitution, asynchronous mutation, feed-schema boundaries, tool-registration failure and the absence of prohibited external effects. Challenges we ran into The hardest problem was preserving useful agent autonomy without quietly widening authority. An agent should be free to inspect, propose, test and explain. It should not be able to turn a readable product ID, a browser click or a provider credential into merchant approval or paid spend. That led us to separate representation approval from future external-effect authorization and to make the demo's current authority limitation visible rather than hiding it behind optimistic language. Product fit created another subtle problem. A keyword match is not a recommendation. A request for a waterproof bag for a 17-inch laptop must not pass because the product is waterproof when the evidence says it fits only a 16-inch device. We changed the shopper evaluator to classify each material constraint as supported, contradicted or unknown and to return an overall match only when every required constraint is supported. Cross-channel consistency was equally demanding. Copy, SKU, price, availability, URLs, brand and evidence all need to belong to the same approved product truth. We moved money to exact GBP minor-unit precision, bound every feed-bearing field to approval and independently recomputed validation results before producing receipts. Finally, WebMCP is not present in every browser environment. We kept the complete merchant workflow usable without it, then ran the deployed application in a WebMCP-enabled ChatGPT browser to verify native discovery of all ten tools and the end-to-end shared-state workflow. A deterministic, downloadable CSV and JSON receipt without credentials, activation, spend, checkout or payment. A public Vercel deployment, native ChatGPT WebMCP acceptance run and a comprehensive automated quality gate. We are especially proud that the safety model is part of the product experience. The demo does not merely avoid external effects; it explains which effects are absent, which validations are local and what additional authority a production integration would require. The strongest representation is the one whose claims can be traced to current, product-bound evidence and tested against concrete buyer needs. We also learned that WebMCP is most powerful as a collaborative surface. The agent is not operating in a hidden automation layer. It works inside the merchant's live page, over the same evidence, lifecycle and cart state the user can see. Approval, execution and observed outcomes are different claims. Approving a representation does not automatically authorize a Shopify write or an Ads operation.
