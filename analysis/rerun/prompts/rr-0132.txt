# FROZEN SCORING RUBRIC (pre-registered before any Stage 1 review)

Scores are integers 1-10 on each of the FOUR official criteria. Every score carries:
rationale, evidence_surfaces, confidence (0-1). Reviewers use the full scale and these
band anchors verbatim. Category neutrality is absolute: a game, art piece, music tool,
CRM, weird toy, and developer tool are all eligible for 10/10 on every criterion if they
satisfy that criterion exceptionally well FOR WHAT THEY ARE TRYING TO BE.
No aesthetic penalties. Signs of hurried AI work matter only as actual product-quality
problems (incoherence, broken flow, placeholder content), never as style.

## WebMCP Leverage — what does WebMCP change for this product?
Judge the delta WebMCP makes to reliability, precision, structured state access,
shared human-agent state, repeatability, and capability. Tool COUNT is not leverage.
- 9-10 TRANSFORMATIVE: the intended experience is impractical without the structured
  agent surface; human and agent share live product state; the collaboration could not
  be replicated comparably well by a general agent driving the UI.
- 7-8 MAJOR DELTA: without WebMCP a general agent is markedly less reliable/precise
  here; the agent is central to the product, not a bolt-on.
- 5-6 MEANINGFUL DELTA: existing capability packaged into a genuinely better product
  experience via structured tools; same outcome otherwise possible with real friction.
- 3-4 MINOR DELTA: generic browser automation achieves comparable outcomes; WebMCP is
  a convenience layer.
- 1-2 COSMETIC: exposes an action a UI button already offers; no meaningful change.

## Execution — is the actual product coherent, intentional, complete for its scope?
Stage 1 scores EXECUTION_CLAIMED: coherence/intentionality/completeness as evidenced by
the packet (description + packaging + video frames/transcript). Thin evidence caps the
score (end-to-end credit requires end-to-end evidence) and lowers confidence.
Stage 2 may rescore as EXECUTION_OBSERVED; latest evidence-backed score replaces, never
sums.
- 9-10: all surfaces agree; central workflow demonstrably completes; states handled;
  feels finished for its stated scope.
- 7-8: strong coherence and intentionality; central workflow appears functional; minor
  unfinished edges.
- 5-6: plausibly real with visible gaps; scope unclear or core not demonstrated
  end-to-end.
- 3-4: thin prototype; contradictions between surfaces; placeholder content.
- 1-2: non-functional or incoherent for its claimed scope.

## Creativity & Ambition — novelty of concept and interaction model
Judge ONLY: novelty of the concept, novelty of the interaction model, originality
relative to existing concepts, ambition, memorable/surprising use of the medium, and
the depth with which the concept is pursued. WebMCP indispensability belongs under
WebMCP Leverage, NOT here: a project can be extremely creative even if another agent
API could theoretically have enabled something similar.
- 9-10: genuinely novel concept or interaction model; ambitious scope pursued deeply
  and coherently; memorable.
- 7-8: fresh synthesis or notably original interaction; ambitious for a hackathon.
- 5-6: competent take on a known category with a real new angle.
- 3-4: derivative; novelty mostly surface details.
- 1-2: clone/template with trivial changes.
"That the category exists" is never a penalty; judge the submitted concept.

## Potential Impact — credible value for the intended audience
Judge: is there a real audience; is there a real problem, desire, or use case for that
audience; does the demonstrated solution genuinely address it. NO commercial adoption,
monetization, market pull, or startup viability is required. Entertainment, play, art,
creative expression, learning, niche hobby use, experimentation, and community value
are legitimate forms of impact. A tiny audience fully justifies a top score.
- 9-10: specific real audience with a real problem/desire; solution demonstrably
  addresses it; credible value, appeal, usefulness, or meaning for that audience.
- 7-8: clear audience and problem, good fit, credible value.
- 5-6: identifiable audience, looser fit or partial address.
- 3-4: speculative audience or strained fit; impact mostly aspirational.
- 1-2: no coherent audience/problem pairing.
Audience size is NOT a multiplier.

## Aggregate & ranking
aggregate = leverage + execution + impact + creativity (4-40). Nothing else enters.
Tie-breaks: Leverage, then Execution, then Impact, then Creativity, then higher
combined evidence confidence, then slug alphabetical. See PROTOCOL.md Ranking section.

That is the main idea behind the project: AI speed. Verified truth. Human control. How we built it The challenge edition of ListingPilot is built with Next.js and TypeScript and deployed on Vercel. I connected it to WebMCP through four tools: search_products — search the product catalog inspect_product — inspect Product Truth and product health prepare_listing_improvement — prepare a safer listing publish_approved_changes — publish only after human approval One decision was especially important to me: approval is not a WebMCP tool. The AI agent can do the repetitive work and prepare a proposal, but it cannot approve its own proposal. That decision stays with the merchant. The challenge version uses synthetic product data, so judges can test the workflow without needing a Shopify account or accessing real merchant information. Challenges we ran into The biggest challenge was deciding how much control to give the AI agent. I wanted the agent to be genuinely useful, not just give suggestions. But ecommerce changes can affect real customers, so I also didn't want an agent to freely publish information without a human decision. That is why I separated preparation from approval. Another challenge was conflicting product information. If one source says a TV has one number of HDMI ports and another source says something different, the easiest solution would be to choose one. ListingPilot doesn't do that. It marks the information as conflicting and keeps it out of the proposed listing. I also spent a lot of time testing the WebMCP tool lifecycle and how the tools behave inside ChatGPT's browser. Accomplishments that we're proud of I'm most proud that ListingPilot does more than give an AI agent access to an ecommerce page. The agent can actually understand something useful about the catalog. In the demo, it starts with 500 products , finds the listing that needs the most attention, checks the Product Truth, understands which facts it can trust, and prepares a safer listing. And it still cannot make the final decision for the merchant. For me, that balance is the most important part of ListingPilot: Verified facts are used. Conflicting facts are excluded. Missing facts are not invented. Approval stays human. What we learned Building this challenge changed how I think about AI agents. Before working with WebMCP, it was easy to think about AI mainly as something that generates content. But an agent becomes much more useful when an application gives it specific tools and reliable information to work with. I also learned that giving an agent more power is not always better. Sometimes the better design is to let AI handle the repetitive work while keeping an important decision with the person using the product. And for ecommerce, I learned something that has become central to ListingPilot: Good writing based on bad product data is still a bad listing. What's next for ListingPilot This challenge version uses synthetic data and focuses on one clear workflow. My bigger goal for ListingPilot is to bring the same idea to real ecommerce catalogs: better product intelligence, merchant-specific listing rules, SEO, structured product data, bulk operations, and safe Shopify publishing. I want ListingPilot to help merchants use AI across large catalogs without having to choose between automation and control. Fix the arrow. Realize the box is slightly misaligned. Move everything again. Architecture diagrams are incredibly useful for READMEs, system-design discussions, demos, presentations, and explaining how a project actually works, but creating them manually is still tedious. Meanwhile, AI agents have become good at understanding codebases, architectures, and technical ideas. They can explain the system perfectly, but when it comes to actually putting that understanding onto a visual canvas, the human usually ends up doing the drawing. So I built Excalidraw WebMCP . The idea is simple: What if Excalidraw wasn't just a canvas for humans, but a canvas where humans and AI agents could work on together? What it does Excalidraw WebMCP makes Excalidraw agent-native. Humans and browser agents operate on the same live canvas . An agent can understand what is currently on the canvas, reason about the diagram, make structured changes, connect elements, and export the final result. The human stays in the loop and can continue moving, editing, deleting, or adding elements normally through Excalidraw. There is no separate "AI-generated preview" that has to be copied back into the editor. There is just one canvas shared between the human and the agent. How I built it The project is built around a simple rule: There should be one source of truth for the canvas, regardless of whether a human or an agent changes it. The central canvas state and mutation boundary lives in: src/canvas/canvas-controller.ts All changes to the canvas flow through this layer. On top of that, I created a semantic operation layer which translates higher-level operations into actual Excalidraw elements in: src/canvas/semantic-operations.ts The WebMCP layer lives inside src/webmcp/ . It contains the schemas, tool registration, validation, and the execution bridge used by the exposed tools. When the browser provides document.modelContext , the application registers its WebMCP capabilities and allows compatible browser agents to interact with the current board. The canvas is stored locally in the browser using localStorage , keeping the project intentionally lightweight. Challenges I faced 1. Turning drawing actions into semantic operations Drawing applications are very coordinate-heavy. Agents, however, work much better with concepts. I didn't want the interface to expose something like: drawRectangle(x: 417, y: 263, width: 188...) as it would confuse the agent. Instead, I needed an abstraction where the agent could express meaningful operations while the application handled the Excalidraw-specific details. Finding that between agent-friendly semantics and precise canvas geometry was one of the biggest challenges. 2. Keeping connectors and elements stable A diagram is not just a collection of rectangles. Elements have relationships. Connectors need to stay attached to the right shapes, elements need stable identifiers, and updates must avoid unexpectedly breaking the rest of the canvas. 3. Letting humans and agents edit the same state I didn't want an “AI mode” and a separate “human mode.” The agent needed to modify exactly the same board the user was looking at, while keeping normal Excalidraw interaction intact. Designing a single mutation boundary helped make both interaction models coexist without creating two competing versions of the canvas. What I learned WebMCP showed me how powerful browser-native agents can be, and we are only getting started on this tech. I think it could become a major frontier for browser agents, with applications far beyond this project. What's next This project is a prototype , and there is a lot more in Excalidraw that I would love to bring into WebMCP. Right now, the agent can work with the core canvas primitives, but Excalidraw itself has a much richer set of capabilities. The next step would be to expose more of those features through WebMCP so an agent can interact with the canvas almost as completely as a human can. That could include things like image insertion, richer element controls, and Mermaid-to-Excalidraw , where Mermaid syntax can be turned directly into editable Excalidraw diagrams. I also want to make the natural-language → diagram experience much more robust. The current prototype works well for many diagrams, but there is still room to improve how agents reason about layout, spacing, connector placement, and iterative refinement instead of trying to get everything perfect in one pass. Beyond that, I would love to explore: Canvas history and revisions so agents can understand and restore previous states Better real-time human + agent collaboration on the same board Multi-agent collaboration , where multiple agents can contribute to one canvas Deeper support for Excalidraw's existing tools instead of maintaining a smaller agent-specific subset And if I get the opportunity to take this further, I would love to contribute these ideas back toward the actual open-source Excalidraw ecosystem . Excalidraw already has an MCP App Server. I think WebMCP could complement that by making the Excalidraw web app itself natively understandable and operable by browser agents, without treating the browser as just a screen to click around. The end goal is simple: Take everything humans can do inside Excalidraw and make those capabilities equally accessible to agents through WebMCP.
