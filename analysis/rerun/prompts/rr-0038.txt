# FROZEN SCORING RUBRIC (pre-registered before any Stage 1 review)

Scores are integers 1-10 on each of the FOUR official criteria. Every score carries:
rationale, evidence_surfaces, confidence (0-1). Reviewers use the full scale and these
band anchors verbatim. Category neutrality is absolute: a game, art piece, music tool,
CRM, weird toy, and developer tool are all eligible for 10/10 on every criterion if they
satisfy that criterion exceptionally well FOR WHAT THEY ARE TRYING TO BE.
No aesthetic penalties. Signs of hurried AI work matter only as actual product-quality
problems (incoherence, broken flow, placeholder content), never as style.

## WebMCP Leverage — what does WebMCP change for this product?
Judge the delta WebMCP makes to reliability, precision, structured state access,
shared human-agent state, repeatability, and capability. Tool COUNT is not leverage.
- 9-10 TRANSFORMATIVE: the intended experience is impractical without the structured
  agent surface; human and agent share live product state; the collaboration could not
  be replicated comparably well by a general agent driving the UI.
- 7-8 MAJOR DELTA: without WebMCP a general agent is markedly less reliable/precise
  here; the agent is central to the product, not a bolt-on.
- 5-6 MEANINGFUL DELTA: existing capability packaged into a genuinely better product
  experience via structured tools; same outcome otherwise possible with real friction.
- 3-4 MINOR DELTA: generic browser automation achieves comparable outcomes; WebMCP is
  a convenience layer.
- 1-2 COSMETIC: exposes an action a UI button already offers; no meaningful change.

## Execution — is the actual product coherent, intentional, complete for its scope?
Stage 1 scores EXECUTION_CLAIMED: coherence/intentionality/completeness as evidenced by
the packet (description + packaging + video frames/transcript). Thin evidence caps the
score (end-to-end credit requires end-to-end evidence) and lowers confidence.
Stage 2 may rescore as EXECUTION_OBSERVED; latest evidence-backed score replaces, never
sums.
- 9-10: all surfaces agree; central workflow demonstrably completes; states handled;
  feels finished for its stated scope.
- 7-8: strong coherence and intentionality; central workflow appears functional; minor
  unfinished edges.
- 5-6: plausibly real with visible gaps; scope unclear or core not demonstrated
  end-to-end.
- 3-4: thin prototype; contradictions between surfaces; placeholder content.
- 1-2: non-functional or incoherent for its claimed scope.

## Creativity & Ambition — novelty of concept and interaction model
Judge ONLY: novelty of the concept, novelty of the interaction model, originality
relative to existing concepts, ambition, memorable/surprising use of the medium, and
the depth with which the concept is pursued. WebMCP indispensability belongs under
WebMCP Leverage, NOT here: a project can be extremely creative even if another agent
API could theoretically have enabled something similar.
- 9-10: genuinely novel concept or interaction model; ambitious scope pursued deeply
  and coherently; memorable.
- 7-8: fresh synthesis or notably original interaction; ambitious for a hackathon.
- 5-6: competent take on a known category with a real new angle.
- 3-4: derivative; novelty mostly surface details.
- 1-2: clone/template with trivial changes.
"That the category exists" is never a penalty; judge the submitted concept.

## Potential Impact — credible value for the intended audience
Judge: is there a real audience; is there a real problem, desire, or use case for that
audience; does the demonstrated solution genuinely address it. NO commercial adoption,
monetization, market pull, or startup viability is required. Entertainment, play, art,
creative expression, learning, niche hobby use, experimentation, and community value
are legitimate forms of impact. A tiny audience fully justifies a top score.
- 9-10: specific real audience with a real problem/desire; solution demonstrably
  addresses it; credible value, appeal, usefulness, or meaning for that audience.
- 7-8: clear audience and problem, good fit, credible value.
- 5-6: identifiable audience, looser fit or partial address.
- 3-4: speculative audience or strained fit; impact mostly aspirational.
- 1-2: no coherent audience/problem pairing.
Audience size is NOT a multiplier.

## Aggregate & ranking
aggregate = leverage + execution + impact + creativity (4-40). Nothing else enters.
Tie-breaks: Leverage, then Execution, then Impact, then Creativity, then higher
combined evidence confidence, then slug alphabetical. See PROTOCOL.md Ranking section.

When your home is already damaged, even figuring out which form to start with can be overwhelming. The problem isn't always that nobody wants to help. It's that the help is spread across different websites, forms, and systems that don't really talk to each other. We started wondering: what if an agent could handle some of that coordination for you, without making decisions on your behalf? That is what made WebMCP interesting to us. A website can expose specific tools to an agent while still controlling exactly what the agent is allowed to do. The agent can take care of repetitive work, while the resident stays in control of what gets shared, approved, or submitted. What it does 2030MCP LinkUp is designed for the days after a flood, fire, earthquake, storm, or other disaster. Instead of entering the same information into multiple systems, you explain what happened once. LinkUp turns that into a simple Situation Map showing what it understood, what information is still missing, and which services may be relevant. For example, the system may identify that you need temporary housing, utility support, replacement documents, or disaster relief. The agent can then read provider requirements, organize the information you already provided, and prepare the next step for you. The important part is that it does not make the final decision. It cannot decide whether your story is true, approve your case, or submit something on your behalf without your review. You see what the agent plans to do first and choose whether to continue. How we built it We built LinkUp with Next.js App Router, TypeScript, and Tailwind CSS. The WebMCP layer is built around document.modelContext.registerTool. Instead of registering every tool all the time, LinkUp rebuilds the available tool registry from the current signed case state. That means a tool only becomes available when the user has reached the right step and given the required consent. We also enforce the same boundaries on the server. Route handlers validate signed session data including the user's role, disaster scenario, and origin. We use strict Zod schemas, state version checks, idempotency keys, packet hashes, expiring grants, and rate limits to prevent replay, tampering, or unintended submissions. Every action that changes the case is also recorded in a server-side audit log. For the disaster flows, we created separate provider fixtures for floods, fires, earthquakes, and storms. The provider policy version is selected before evidence is collected, so changing the incident later cannot silently change the rules applied to the case. Testing was a major part of the project. We currently have 108 Vitest tests covering the WebMCP contract, dynamic tool registration and removal, replay prevention, tamper rejection, and session integrity. We also run strict TypeScript checks and GitHub Actions with pinned revisions. Most importantly, we tested the real WebMCP flow in a browser using the native document.modelContext interface. We verified that the send tool appears only after consent and disappears again after it has been used. For Chrome testing, the project also includes a Chrome 149+ path using chrome://flags/#enable-webmcp-testing. To keep the demo easy to run, the entire resident session survives serverless cold starts without requiring a database. Case state is stored in signed, Brotli-compressed cookie chunks with strict size limits. We also test the deployed version with npm run test:golden to make sure a fresh incognito session can complete the full flow from beginning to end. Challenges we ran into The hardest part was making "the human is in control" something the system actually enforces. At first, it is tempting to put instructions in the tool description and say that the agent should ask before doing something important. But that still depends on the model following the instruction. We wanted the restriction to exist at the system level instead. So we moved permissions into the tool registry itself. A consequential tool does not exist until the user has explicitly given consent. After a one-time action is used, the tool removes itself from the registry. That gave us something we could actually test: not just whether the agent behaved correctly, but whether the tool was available at all. Another challenge was writing tool descriptions for an agent rather than for a person. We added clear authority boundaries such as CONSEQUENTIAL, ONE USE, explicit fields that prevent the agent from implying provider approval, and untrustedContentHint for information that comes from free-text user stories. Another challenge was keeping a complete resident session working across serverless instances without a database while still staying inside browser cookie limits. Accomplishments that we're proud of We're proud that LinkUp actually works as a deployed product, rather than being just a mockup of an agent workflow. The WebMCP registry changes live as the resident moves through the process. A one-use send tool appears after consent, can be used once, and then disappears. We also built a read-only audit interface so another agent can inspect what happened without being able to change the case. And instead of using one generic disaster demo, we built separate provider contracts for floods, fires, earthquakes, and storms so the workflow behaves differently depending on the situation. What we learned If a tool should not be available yet, the safest option is not to register it at all. We also learned that tool descriptions matter much more than we expected. They are not just documentation for developers; they are part of the interface the model uses to understand its authority and responsibilities. Clear descriptions made the agent much more likely to ask for review instead of acting as if it had approval from a provider. WebMCP also showed us a different way to think about agent safety. Instead of putting every restriction inside a chat prompt, some of the rules can be enforced directly by the website and its tool lifecycle. What's next for 2030MCP LinkUp First, a server-issued human-presence challenge so consequential actions can verify that a real resident is still present. Second, an encrypted case database for longer-term recovery cases and larger documents. Third, real provider integrations for housing agencies, utilities, local government, and relief organizations. The WebMCP contracts are already structured so providers could publish their own requirement and submission tools. The county, housing, and utility portals in our demo are examples of how those provider-owned integrations could work. The long-term goal is simple: people should not have to rebuild the same disaster story for every organization they need help from. They should explain what happened once, review what gets shared, and stay in control of the process. We run developer programs like hackathons and accelerators that help companies get builders using their technology, mostly in gaming and AI. We also perform transformations for our clients, getting them ai-ready. And when we have some extra time, we build/incubate our own products, one of them is Methora.io, which is about getting AI-assisted work all the way to done instead of just started. So a WebMCP hackathon was right up our alley. I browsed the public directories of WebMCP-enabled sites to see what people were connecting. What I found was that most sites had tools for searching, pricing, signing up and checking out (the start of the funnel). But very few had tools for cancelling, returning, or closing an account. I couldn't find anyone measuring that, so I built something that does. I don't think WebMCP really works until an agent can do everything a human can on a site. And you can't fix what you can't measure, so hopefully Full Loop helps WebMCP take the next step! What it does Full Loop takes a website's WebMCP tools and sorts them into eight stages of a customer relationship: Discover, Evaluate, Commit, Use, Modify, Reverse, Exit, Support. It shows which stages the site covers and gives it a score. If an agent can commit on a user's behalf (buy, book, sign up) but has no tool to cancel, return or leave, the site is flagged as an open loop. I ran it over the 475 sites in the public webmcp.com directory, about 2,989 tools in total. 148 sites let an agent commit. 144 of those give it no way to undo. No site in the directory has an exit tool. The page also registers eight WebMCP tools of its own, so you can do the audit through an agent in ChatGPT's browser instead of clicking: audit_site and audit_tool_list run the audit and render it on the page find_sites_by_stage , compare_sites and get_corpus_stats answer questions about the directory (for example "which travel sites let you cancel a booking?", which currently returns nothing) draft_missing_tools writes registerTool stubs for the missing stages into an editor on the page set_stub_code lets the agent rewrite a stub in that editor while you edit it too add_review_note lets the agent leave a short note on a stage card Tool calls are logged on the page so you can see what the agent did. How we built it It's a static site: HTML, CSS and plain JavaScript, hosted on GitHub Pages. There's no backend and no AI API. The classification is a set of regular expressions run over tool names and descriptions, checked in a fixed order (Exit and Reverse first, so cancel_order doesn't get caught by the generic order rule). The scoring weights each stage. Stub templates are picked by stage and site category. The tools are registered with document.modelContext.registerTool , with a fallback to navigator.modelContext . Each has a JSON Schema inputSchema and an execute function that updates the DOM and returns JSON in content[0].text . The directory data is a snapshot fetched by a small Node script and committed to the repo, so the site doesn't depend on webmcp.com being up. I tested it in ChatGPT's in-app browser with Codex on GPT-5.6 Sol. Challenges we ran into Word boundaries in regex don't work on snake_case. \bpolicies\b never matched search_shop_policies_and_faqs , so the tool fell through to matching on its description, which mentions "return policy", and it got filed under Reverse. That made a lot of Shopify stores look like they had closed loops when they didn't. Splitting names into words before matching fixed it, and the open-loop rate went from 77% to 97%. Deciding what counts as Reverse also took some thought. Shopify's cancel_cart undoes a cart, not an order, so I put it under Use. The number would have looked better the other way, but it would have been wrong. Accomplishments that we're proud of The 97% figure is reproducible from public data, and as far as I can tell nobody had computed it before. The site works the same whether or not an agent is present. And it has no dependencies, no keys and no server, so there's nothing to break during judging. What we learned With WebMCP the page doesn't need its own model. The agent in the browser supplies the reasoning and the page supplies data and functions. Once I understood that, I stopped trying to add an AI API and the project got simpler. Also, the WebMCP ecosystem right now is mostly acquisition tooling. Like Comment Story Updates Edgewright turns your redirect and rewrite rules into a live console a human and an AI agent work together, over WebMCP. Test any URL, catch the rule that shadows another, translate a config to another host, then prove nothing breaks, all in the browser. The problem: redirect rules are ordered and first-match-wins. A broad rule above a specific one makes the specific one dead code, so a link works locally then 404s in production. Edgewright makes that visible and fixable, by a person or by an agent on the same page. How the tools are used: the console registers fifteen tools with document.modelContext.registerTool. In the demo the agent runs test_url to find the 404, audit_rules to name the shadowed rule, reorder_rule to fix the order, then test_url again. Read-only tools carry readOnlyHint so the agent runs them without a prompt. The tools that change rules omit it, so the agent asks first. The UI and the tools mutate one shared store, so a human clicking a button and an agent calling a tool run the exact same code. The product is three apps on one engine: the Console, Preflight (a pre-deploy gate), and Studio (migrate a config between hosts and prove every URL still resolves). The last step of most HR work is a number being written down, a salary, a bonus, a reimbursement, and a figure that came out of a language model is not a figure you can pay somebody. So the reading gets automated, the deciding does not, and the gap between them is where the errors live. Dayflow is an Indian HRMS, denominated in rupees and lakh, applying Indian rules: provident fund, professional tax, CGST and SGST on a restaurant bill. So the case for it should be made with Indian numbers. Finding Source 48% of Indian companies have no way to detect travel and expense fraud , because claims are checked by hand. About 55% cannot retrieve past claims at all. Happay, via Economic Times Every ₹1 lost to fraud costs an Indian firm ₹4.00 , and 54% say fraud is rising. So full autonomy makes this worse, not better. It removes the human control the fraud data says is load bearing, and hands the arithmetic to the part of the stack with the weakest claim on it. A better model does not fix that. A better place to put the human does. The insight came from the WebMCP explainer , in the line about tools running inside the page the human is already looking at. If the tool lives in the page, the proposal can live in the page too, so the agent never has to be the one holding the number. That is why the three tools that move money accept no amounts at all. Not validated amounts. None. There is no numeric parameter anywhere in their schemas, so a model that invented a total has nowhere to put it.
