=== RUBRIC (frozen) ===
# FROZEN SCORING RUBRIC (pre-registered before any Stage 1 review)

Scores are integers 1-10 on each of the FOUR official criteria. Every score carries:
rationale, evidence_surfaces, confidence (0-1). Reviewers use the full scale and these
band anchors verbatim. Category neutrality is absolute: a game, art piece, music tool,
CRM, weird toy, and developer tool are all eligible for 10/10 on every criterion if they
satisfy that criterion exceptionally well FOR WHAT THEY ARE TRYING TO BE.
No aesthetic penalties. Signs of hurried AI work matter only as actual product-quality
problems (incoherence, broken flow, placeholder content), never as style.

## WebMCP Leverage — what does WebMCP change for this product?
Judge the delta WebMCP makes to reliability, precision, structured state access,
shared human-agent state, repeatability, and capability. Tool COUNT is not leverage.
- 9-10 TRANSFORMATIVE: the intended experience is impractical without the structured
  agent surface; human and agent share live product state; the collaboration could not
  be replicated comparably well by a general agent driving the UI.
- 7-8 MAJOR DELTA: without WebMCP a general agent is markedly less reliable/precise
  here; the agent is central to the product, not a bolt-on.
- 5-6 MEANINGFUL DELTA: existing capability packaged into a genuinely better product
  experience via structured tools; same outcome otherwise possible with real friction.
- 3-4 MINOR DELTA: generic browser automation achieves comparable outcomes; WebMCP is
  a convenience layer.
- 1-2 COSMETIC: exposes an action a UI button already offers; no meaningful change.

## Execution — is the actual product coherent, intentional, complete for its scope?
Stage 1 scores EXECUTION_CLAIMED: coherence/intentionality/completeness as evidenced by
the packet (description + packaging + video frames/transcript). Thin evidence caps the
score (end-to-end credit requires end-to-end evidence) and lowers confidence.
Stage 2 may rescore as EXECUTION_OBSERVED; latest evidence-backed score replaces, never
sums.
- 9-10: all surfaces agree; central workflow demonstrably completes; states handled;
  feels finished for its stated scope.
- 7-8: strong coherence and intentionality; central workflow appears functional; minor
  unfinished edges.
- 5-6: plausibly real with visible gaps; scope unclear or core not demonstrated
  end-to-end.
- 3-4: thin prototype; contradictions between surfaces; placeholder content.
- 1-2: non-functional or incoherent for its claimed scope.

## Creativity & Ambition — novelty of concept and interaction model
Judge ONLY: novelty of the concept, novelty of the interaction model, originality
relative to existing concepts, ambition, memorable/surprising use of the medium, and
the depth with which the concept is pursued. WebMCP indispensability belongs under
WebMCP Leverage, NOT here: a project can be extremely creative even if another agent
API could theoretically have enabled something similar.
- 9-10: genuinely novel concept or interaction model; ambitious scope pursued deeply
  and coherently; memorable.
- 7-8: fresh synthesis or notably original interaction; ambitious for a hackathon.
- 5-6: competent take on a known category with a real new angle.
- 3-4: derivative; novelty mostly surface details.
- 1-2: clone/template with trivial changes.
"That the category exists" is never a penalty; judge the submitted concept.

## Potential Impact — credible value for the intended audience
Judge: is there a real audience; is there a real problem, desire, or use case for that
audience; does the demonstrated solution genuinely address it. NO commercial adoption,
monetization, market pull, or startup viability is required. Entertainment, play, art,
creative expression, learning, niche hobby use, experimentation, and community value
are legitimate forms of impact. A tiny audience fully justifies a top score.
- 9-10: specific real audience with a real problem/desire; solution demonstrably
  addresses it; credible value, appeal, usefulness, or meaning for that audience.
- 7-8: clear audience and problem, good fit, credible value.
- 5-6: identifiable audience, looser fit or partial address.
- 3-4: speculative audience or strained fit; impact mostly aspirational.
- 1-2: no coherent audience/problem pairing.
Audience size is NOT a multiplier.

## Aggregate & ranking
aggregate = leverage + execution + impact + creativity (4-40). Nothing else enters.
Tie-breaks: Leverage, then Execution, then Impact, then Creativity, then higher
combined evidence confidence, then slug alphabetical. See PROTOCOL.md Ranking section.

Like Comment Story Updates Why this use case is a strong fit for WebMCP Seating a room is a two-brain problem. One brain holds context no form can capture: who is divorced, which sponsor is feuding with whom, which aunt needs to sit near the exit. The other has to satisfy a combinatorial pile of constraints: table capacities, conflicts, introductions, dietary needs, accessibility, company-mixing rules, and table balance. Humans are great at the first and bad at the second. Agents are the opposite. Before WebMCP, neither interface worked well. A drag-and-drop floor plan is a terrible surface for an agent to operate through pixels, while moving the task into chat throws away the human's spatial judgment. Duet lets both work on the same live state, in the same tab: the human drags, pins, and visually evaluates the room while the agent uses structured tools such as seat_guest , add_constraint , set_group_rule , and a real constraint solver. How it creates a better user experience Duet gives human-agent collaboration an explicit contract, enforced in code. Pins are law. Any guest placed manually is pinned 📌. The solver plans around those decisions, and agent tools are refused if they attempt to move or remove a pinned guest. Agents propose; humans decide. The preferred agent tool, propose_arrangement , does not mutate the room. It displays the reasoning and complete move list first. Only the human can Accept or Dismiss it. The tool surface follows human attention. Selecting a table dynamically exposes table-scoped tools such as seat_at_selected_table and clear_selected_table . Deselecting it removes them. The agent's available capabilities therefore reflect what the human is currently focused on. Everything is visible and reversible. Agent actions appear as a violet cursor on the board, every action enters a shared activity feed labeled ✳ agent or ● you, and undo/redo works across both human and agent actions. What becomes possible — together — that wasn't before Load a 120-guest fundraising gala. The human manually places two important guests, pinning them in place. The agent records "press away from the board", applies "maximum two people from the same company per table", and proposes a complete arrangement for the remaining 118 guests. The solver honors every constraint without touching the human's pins. The human reviews the proposal visually and accepts it in one click. Create a conflict deliberately and Duet pans to the affected table, identifies who conflicts and why, and lets the agent call explain_seating to justify individual placements. Then ask: "Give me the caterer's brief." The agent calls export_plan and returns dietary counts by table. That loop — human judgment steering an agent and a constraint solver through conversation, directly on a shared visual surface — is what WebMCP makes possible. The same engine includes five scenarios: wedding, fundraising gala, corporate networking dinner, office seating, and classroom seating. How we implemented WebMCP Duet exposes 21 base tools through document.modelContext.registerTool , covering: guest and table CRUD pair constraints with notes group spread and cluster rules solver execution arrangement proposals pins dietary reports Markdown export template loading undo explain_seating get_activity_log , allowing a returning agent to understand what the human changed while it was away It also exposes 4 dynamic selection-scoped tools registered and removed with AbortController as the user's selection changes. The constraint solver is dependency-free and combines greedy assignment, local search, targeted repair, and three-way relocation. A 120-guest room solves in roughly 75 ms while respecting pins, pair constraints, group rules, accessibility requirements, and table balance. Duet is built with React, Vite, and TypeScript and has no backend. The entire collaborative state lives in the browser tab — exactly where WebMCP operates. Qianshou turns everyday travel requests into clear journeys that people can review while AI agents plan routes, describe locations, and switch alternatives through WebMCP. Like Comment Story Updates ** Inspiration ** Planning a public-transit journey often begins with an ordinary sentence, not a perfectly structured form. People may say, “I want to go to Taipei Main Station,” “from here to Tamsui,” or simply “where am I?” Traditional websites require users to translate that intention into separate fields and controls. Browser agents face the opposite problem: they must inspect the interface, guess what each control means, and hope that the resulting route still matches what the person sees. Qianshou means “holding hands.” The project explores a more collaborative model: a person expresses an intention naturally, an AI agent performs the structured work through WebMCP, and both continue from the same visible journey on the webpage. The project is motivated by travelers who benefit from clearer and lower-friction journey planning, including older adults and people with visual impairments. The current pilot does not claim that every suggested route is fully accessible. Missing accessibility information remains explicitly unknown. ** What it does ** Qianshou is a WebMCP-powered journey-planning web application for Taipei and New Taipei City. A person can describe a journey in natural language without deciding which fields to fill in. Qianshou interprets the request, resolves the places, plans a public-transit route, and presents the journey as readable, step-by-step guidance. When the request depends on “here” or omits an origin, the page can request a fresh browser location. Ambiguous place names are presented for human confirmation instead of being silently guessed. The page combines several kinds of journey information: Step-by-step walking and public-transit instructions Total travel time, walking time, and transfers Alternative routes that can be selected after the first result Arrival information matched to the route’s first transit leg Short-range destination weather Explicit data-source and coverage limitations Qianshou exposes three task-level WebMCP tools: prepare_accessible_journey interprets a natural journey request and prepares the complete result. describe_current_location obtains a fresh location and describes it in human-readable form. select_journey_alternative switches the current journey to an existing alternative and updates the visible result. The person does not need to tell the agent which tool to use. The tool descriptions provide enough meaning for the agent to select the appropriate action. Most importantly, a WebMCP action updates the same page the person is viewing. The route is not trapped inside an isolated chat response: the person and the agent share one journey state and can continue from the same result. ** How we built it ** Qianshou is built with Next.js 16, React 19, TypeScript, and native imperative WebMCP. The three tools are registered in the browser using document.modelContext.registerTool . They expose meaningful travel actions rather than individual buttons or form fields. Both the WebMCP tools and the manual interface reuse the same server-side journey services, so agent actions and human actions produce the same visible application state. A loopback-only Codex CLI service converts natural-language requests into a strict structured intent. It runs with an explicit schema, timeout, and concurrency limit, without shell, browser, plugin, or project-write access. Place resolution combines Taiwan transportation data with OpenStreetMap Nominatim. OpenTripPlanner 2.9 calculates journeys using GTFS schedules and OpenStreetMap streets. TDX provides public-transit and arrival information, while Taiwan’s Central Weather Administration supplies short-range destination forecasts. Arrival information is bound to the selected journey’s actual first transit leg using its stop, route, direction, and transport mode. Switching an alternative route therefore also refreshes the related arrival context. The application is deployed on Google Cloud Platform with Docker and Nginx. To operate within a small VM, we reduced the routing graph to the relevant service region and applied explicit container memory limits. ** Challenges we ran into ** Natural language contains important spatial ambiguity. “From here to Tamsui” and “from Tamsui to here” use the same word but require the current location in opposite roles. A destination-only request also needs different handling from a fully specified journey. Place names introduce another challenge. A station, district, landmark, and bus stop can share similar names. Combining duplicate search results without erasing meaningful ambiguity required careful normalization and human confirmation. Public-transit routing also involves trade-offs. The fastest route may require excessive walking, while minimizing transfers may produce an impractical detour. We evaluate total duration, walking burden, and transfers together, while allowing no more than two transfers. Real-world data is incomplete. Public feeds do not always contain enough information about station pathways, elevators, stairs, or vehicle accessibility to justify a complete accessibility guarantee. We therefore distinguish confirmed information from unknown data instead of converting missing fields into a positive claim. Finally, route planning, geocoding, arrival matching, and weather services have different response times and failure modes. The interface needed to preserve a coherent result without fabricating data when an upstream service was unavailable. ** Accomplishments that we're proud of ** Implemented three working, non-trivial native WebMCP tools. Made WebMCP tool calls update the same visible journey used by the human interface. Allowed agents to act from natural user intent without requiring users to name a tool. Combined place resolution, transit planning, arrival matching, alternatives, and weather in one journey operation. Implemented correct semantics for omitted origins, “from here,” “to here,” and “where am I?” Preserved human confirmation when a place remains ambiguous. Kept exact browser coordinates out of the result returned to the agent. Added keyboard navigation, responsive layouts, live regions, reduced-motion support, and itinerary speech. Preserved a complete natural-language interface when WebMCP is unavailable. Reported unavailable or incomplete data honestly instead of generating a convincing but unsupported route. ** What we learned ** Agent-native does not mean agent-only. The strongest experience came from giving the agent structured, task-level actions while preserving a complete interface for the person. WebMCP works best when it communicates domain meaning—such as preparing a journey or switching an alternative—rather than exposing a collection of low-level interface controls. We also learned that tool descriptions are part of the product. They must explain scope, intent, and important boundaries clearly enough for an agent to choose the right action without forcing the user to speak in implementation terms. Finally, route quality depends on data quality as much as routing algorithms. An explicit “unknown” is more responsible and useful than an unsupported accessibility or availability promise. ** What's next for Qianshou ** Conduct moderated usability testing with older travelers and people using screen readers. Expand verified accessibility-data coverage before making stronger accessibility claims. Integrate official operational elevator information where available. Add nearby-place discovery for stations, convenience stores, and accessible toilets. Improve arrival coverage and multimodal route comparison. Continue refining English and Chinese place understanding. Expand beyond Taipei and New Taipei only after the data coverage and operating model have been validated. Built With codex docker gcp gtfs

Different place, same problem. It isn't just me. Every fall a wave of people do exactly this: new students, new arrivals, first apartments, furnishing a room they can only picture in fragments. What you want in that moment is simple: what do I need, how would it look, what will it cost, and once it's all in, is there any floor left to stand on? That's a planning problem, and planning is exactly what an agent should be good at. But a chatbot can only describe your room back to you. It can't move anything in it. WebMCP changes that: a page can hand its own capabilities to an agent as tools. So I built the page worth handing over. What it does Roomwright is a 3D room planner that runs entirely in the browser. You draw a room, set the doors and windows, and place furniture from a catalogue of parametric pieces. Every item has real dimensions and required clearances. Orbit it, drop to a floor plan, resize or recolor anything, and it keeps a running shopping list with a total. Every one of those actions is exposed to an agent as a WebMCP tool . There are 19 of them. So instead of clicking, you can say: "Studio apartment, 4.6 by 7 metres, door on the south wall, two windows on the east. Bed against the back wall, a sofa splitting off the living area, a small dining set by the window." The agent reads the tools the page offers and builds the whole thing: resizing the room, cutting the openings, furnishing three zones, running the planner's own clearance checks, catching its own overlaps, and fixing them. Then you take over: "turn the sofa toward the TV, lose that chair." Same room, same undo history, you and the agent trading turns on one canvas. How WebMCP powers it The key decision: the UI and the agent mutate the exact same state. There's a single store, and a button click and a tool call go through the same action. So undo/redo, autosave, shareable-URL persistence, and live layout validation all just work for the agent. There is no agent-specific code path for any of them. Tools register against document.modelContext using the spec's AbortSignal lifecycle, in four groups: Read : get_room , list_catalog , get_shopping_list Build : add_item (resolves a product by id, name, or fragment, so the agent never has to guess), create_custom_item , duplicate_item (with an east-west / north-south mirror so "a chair on each side" is actually symmetric), remove_item Arrange and check : move_item , rotate_item , resize_item , set_variant , set_room_dimensions , set_opening , remove_opening , suggest_spot (grids the room and returns candidate positions), check_layout (overlaps, blocked doors/windows, clearance failures, walkways under 0.75 m, narrowest gap) Show : select_item (highlights a piece, opens its inspector), set_view , reset_layout suggest_spot and check_layout matter most. They give the agent a feedback loop, not just setters. It places, checks, notices a conflict, and re-plans, visibly, in the activity log. genMC(P) turns Minecraft skin creation into human–AI collaboration. With WebMCP, agents edit real pixels while you guide them through messages and visual masks—all in a shared 3D studio. Like Comment Story Updates skin gallery manual skin creation shimmering while agent is working on the skin Inspiration Creating Minecraft skins has always been a personal benchmark for AI model performance—especially spatial reasoning. A model must understand how a flat texture wraps around a 3D character, not just produce a convincing front view. With genMC(P), I wanted to turn that benchmark into a shared workspace where humans and agents create and refine skins together. How I built it I built the editor with React, TypeScript, and Three.js. WebMCP gives agents structured tools to inspect skins, edit pixels, and respond to user instructions. Users can paint directly, mark areas, and send messages while the agent works. Each skin has its own session. Gallery skins open as private copies, and publishing requires human confirmation. The backend uses Cloudflare Workers, D1, and R2, deployed through ChatGPT Sites. Challenges The biggest challenges were mapping 2D textures correctly onto 3D body parts and keeping human and agent edits coordinated. Session and revision checks prevent stale edits. Message tracking distinguishes “read” from “completed.” I also learned that a marked area should be guidance by default—not a restriction that prevents the agent from completing the task. What I learned Good agent integration needs more than tools: it needs clear state, visible feedback, and human control. Minecraft skins remain a surprisingly demanding spatial-reasoning test. WebMCP turns that test into an interactive creative process, where users can guide the model and improve the result together.
