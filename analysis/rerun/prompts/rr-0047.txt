# FROZEN SCORING RUBRIC (pre-registered before any Stage 1 review)

Scores are integers 1-10 on each of the FOUR official criteria. Every score carries:
rationale, evidence_surfaces, confidence (0-1). Reviewers use the full scale and these
band anchors verbatim. Category neutrality is absolute: a game, art piece, music tool,
CRM, weird toy, and developer tool are all eligible for 10/10 on every criterion if they
satisfy that criterion exceptionally well FOR WHAT THEY ARE TRYING TO BE.
No aesthetic penalties. Signs of hurried AI work matter only as actual product-quality
problems (incoherence, broken flow, placeholder content), never as style.

## WebMCP Leverage — what does WebMCP change for this product?
Judge the delta WebMCP makes to reliability, precision, structured state access,
shared human-agent state, repeatability, and capability. Tool COUNT is not leverage.
- 9-10 TRANSFORMATIVE: the intended experience is impractical without the structured
  agent surface; human and agent share live product state; the collaboration could not
  be replicated comparably well by a general agent driving the UI.
- 7-8 MAJOR DELTA: without WebMCP a general agent is markedly less reliable/precise
  here; the agent is central to the product, not a bolt-on.
- 5-6 MEANINGFUL DELTA: existing capability packaged into a genuinely better product
  experience via structured tools; same outcome otherwise possible with real friction.
- 3-4 MINOR DELTA: generic browser automation achieves comparable outcomes; WebMCP is
  a convenience layer.
- 1-2 COSMETIC: exposes an action a UI button already offers; no meaningful change.

## Execution — is the actual product coherent, intentional, complete for its scope?
Stage 1 scores EXECUTION_CLAIMED: coherence/intentionality/completeness as evidenced by
the packet (description + packaging + video frames/transcript). Thin evidence caps the
score (end-to-end credit requires end-to-end evidence) and lowers confidence.
Stage 2 may rescore as EXECUTION_OBSERVED; latest evidence-backed score replaces, never
sums.
- 9-10: all surfaces agree; central workflow demonstrably completes; states handled;
  feels finished for its stated scope.
- 7-8: strong coherence and intentionality; central workflow appears functional; minor
  unfinished edges.
- 5-6: plausibly real with visible gaps; scope unclear or core not demonstrated
  end-to-end.
- 3-4: thin prototype; contradictions between surfaces; placeholder content.
- 1-2: non-functional or incoherent for its claimed scope.

## Creativity & Ambition — novelty of concept and interaction model
Judge ONLY: novelty of the concept, novelty of the interaction model, originality
relative to existing concepts, ambition, memorable/surprising use of the medium, and
the depth with which the concept is pursued. WebMCP indispensability belongs under
WebMCP Leverage, NOT here: a project can be extremely creative even if another agent
API could theoretically have enabled something similar.
- 9-10: genuinely novel concept or interaction model; ambitious scope pursued deeply
  and coherently; memorable.
- 7-8: fresh synthesis or notably original interaction; ambitious for a hackathon.
- 5-6: competent take on a known category with a real new angle.
- 3-4: derivative; novelty mostly surface details.
- 1-2: clone/template with trivial changes.
"That the category exists" is never a penalty; judge the submitted concept.

## Potential Impact — credible value for the intended audience
Judge: is there a real audience; is there a real problem, desire, or use case for that
audience; does the demonstrated solution genuinely address it. NO commercial adoption,
monetization, market pull, or startup viability is required. Entertainment, play, art,
creative expression, learning, niche hobby use, experimentation, and community value
are legitimate forms of impact. A tiny audience fully justifies a top score.
- 9-10: specific real audience with a real problem/desire; solution demonstrably
  addresses it; credible value, appeal, usefulness, or meaning for that audience.
- 7-8: clear audience and problem, good fit, credible value.
- 5-6: identifiable audience, looser fit or partial address.
- 3-4: speculative audience or strained fit; impact mostly aspirational.
- 1-2: no coherent audience/problem pairing.
Audience size is NOT a multiplier.

## Aggregate & ranking
aggregate = leverage + execution + impact + creativity (4-40). Nothing else enters.
Tie-breaks: Leverage, then Execution, then Impact, then Creativity, then higher
combined evidence confidence, then slug alphabetical. See PROTOCOL.md Ranking section.

A WebMCP workspace for repairing constrained plans while keeping humans in control Like Comment Story Updates Inspiration A surprising number of everyday operational problems are really placement problems in disguise. You have a set of people, tasks, items, vehicles, or events, a limited number of places or resources, and a collection of rules that determine what can go where. We see this everywhere. Guests need to be assigned to tables. Patients need beds. Employees need shifts. Classes need rooms. Aircraft need gates. Jobs need machines. Inventory needs storage locations. In every case, the basic question is the same: how do you put the right thing in the right place while respecting all the constraints around it? Creating the first plan is only part of the challenge. The harder part often comes later, when reality changes after the plan has already been accepted. A guest suddenly needs an accessible table. Two people can no longer sit together. A VIP needs a specific position. A resource becomes unavailable. A manager makes a last-minute decision that should not be undone. At that point, the goal is not to throw everything away and optimize from scratch. It is to repair the existing plan while preserving as much of what already works as possible. Traditional optimization systems are good at solving structured problems, but people do not usually describe operational changes as formal constraints. They say things like "keep these two apart", "do not move this person", or "this group needs to stay together". An AI agent can help translate that kind of natural-language intent into structured constraints, but there is another problem: the agent may be working from an older version of the plan while a person is still editing the live page. That creates a very real failure mode. A person can make an important manual change, and the agent can later overwrite it simply because it is still acting on stale state. We wanted to explore a collaboration model where that cannot happen silently. We chose seating because it makes the larger idea easy to see. A guest represents an entity that needs to be assigned. A table represents a limited-capacity resource. "Together" and "apart" rules behave like clustering constraints. Accessibility and fixed-table rules represent eligibility and placement requirements. A locked guest represents a human decision that the agent is not allowed to override. Seating Repair is our way of exploring how WebMCP can bring these pieces together: natural-language agency, deterministic constraint solving, live application state, and direct human control, all operating on the same shared planning surface. What it does Seating Repair is a WebMCP application for solving and repairing constrained placement problems while a person and an agent are both working on the same live plan. The demo uses 12 fictional guests and three tables because that makes the idea easy to understand at a glance. But underneath the seating interface, the application is dealing with a more general planning problem: limited-capacity resources, people who must stay together or apart, fixed placements, eligibility requirements such as accessibility, decisions that a human has explicitly locked, and the need to keep as much of the existing plan unchanged as possible. Through WebMCP, a browser agent can read the exact current state of the board, understand the active constraints, add new hard constraints from a person's request, repair the plan, and then validate the result. The goal is not simply to press an "optimize" button and generate a completely new seating arrangement. In a real planning workflow, many existing decisions are already correct and should stay that way. A useful repair needs to preserve what still works, respect every hard constraint, treat human-locked placements as authoritative, change only what is necessary, and verify that the final result is actually valid. This becomes especially important when a person and an agent are working at the same time. If the agent reads the board and a person then moves or locks a guest, the agent is no longer allowed to write using the old version of the plan. That stale write is rejected with STATE_CHANGED. The agent has to read the page again and continue from the decision the person actually made instead of silently overwriting it. In the demo, this is easy to see: a person can move Grandma Rose to a different table and lock her there. The agent can then add another constraint, repair the remaining conflicts, and validate the board while leaving Grandma Rose exactly where the person placed her. Seating is the domain we chose to demonstrate the idea, but it is not the limit of the underlying pattern. The same kind of constrained allocation and repair appears in restaurant and event operations, protocol seating, workforce scheduling, room and resource allocation, patient-to-bed assignment, airport gate reassignment, and many other situations where a working plan has to adapt as constraints and human decisions change. The current build is intentionally focused on demonstrating that shared coordination model clearly. It is not meant to pretend that one seating demo is already a complete product for every one of those industries. Instead, it shows the core interaction pattern: an AI agent can help repair a constrained plan, while the live application remains the source of truth and human decisions remain in control. How we built it We built Seating Repair as a lightweight TypeScript app with Vite. Everything runs in the browser, with no backend and no separate database for the agent. The seating board that the person sees is the same state the WebMCP tools work with. We kept the WebMCP side intentionally simple and exposed only four tools. One reads the current seating state, one adds hard constraints, one repairs the plan, and one validates the result. That was enough to cover the full interaction we wanted to demonstrate without turning the project into a large collection of overlapping tools. We also did not want the language model itself to be responsible for deciding whether a seating plan is correct. The agent can understand a request such as "keep these two apart" or "do not move this guest", but the actual planning is handled by deterministic application code. The repair logic runs in the browser and searches for an arrangement that satisfies all of the current constraints. It checks table capacity, together and apart rules, fixed-table requirements, accessibility, and human locks. When there is more than one valid solution, it prefers one that changes as little of the current plan as possible. Human edits are treated as first-class state changes. A person can move a guest manually and lock that placement. Once a guest is locked, the agent cannot move them during a repair. There is no hidden tool option that lets the agent ignore the lock; the person has to unlock that guest first. One of the main things we wanted to get right was what happens when the human changes the board while the agent is working. Every change to the seating plan increments a revision number. When the agent tries to change the plan, it has to provide the revision it previously read. If the board has changed since then, the write is rejected with STATE_CHANGED. If the constraints cannot be satisfied, the current plan stays as it was instead of being left in a partially changed state. We wanted to show what happens when an AI agent can use WebMCP to access that context safely and directly. What it does SNIPR WebMCP lets an AI assistant answer real questions about a renovation assignment. In the demo, a contractor asks which shower mixer belongs to a specific bathroom. WebMCP reads the selected SNIPR assignment, address, room data, and resident choices, then returns the exact product information. The demo also shows the answer visually: the user opens the execution map, selects the address, opens the 3D building view, moves to top view, selects the bathroom, and clicks the shower element to see the attached specifications. Finally, the project demonstrates safe actions. WebMCP can propose an operational action, such as finding an inspection slot, but SNIPR requires explicit human approval before anything is written back. How we built it We integrated WebMCP endpoints into SNIPR so agents can access structured assignment context, address data, room information, planning data, communication state, documents, catalog information, and controlled actions. The frontend demo combines the normal SNIPR dashboard, WebMCP chat, an execution map, and a 3D building/room view. The video script uses Playwright and FFmpeg to record a reproducible demo with synchronized scene-based voice-over. Challenges we ran into The hardest part was making the demo coherent. Browser loading, 3D interactions, voice-over timing, and human approval flows all needed to line up. We also had to make sure WebMCP felt like part of the real operational workflow, not just a chatbot bolted onto the side. Accomplishments that we're proud of We are proud that the demo shows three important things together: structured context, visual proof, and controlled writes. The agent can answer useful questions, the user can verify the answer spatially, and the system still protects operational changes with human approval. What we learned WebMCP becomes most valuable when it connects AI to the existing source of truth, instead of replacing it. The best experience is not just “AI answers a question”, but “AI helps the user navigate, verify, and act inside the real system.” What's next Next steps are deeper WebMCP coverage, more robust permission scopes, richer 3D evidence, and production-grade audit trails for every agent-assisted action.
