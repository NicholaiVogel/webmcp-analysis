# FROZEN SCORING RUBRIC (pre-registered before any Stage 1 review)

Scores are integers 1-10 on each of the FOUR official criteria. Every score carries:
rationale, evidence_surfaces, confidence (0-1). Reviewers use the full scale and these
band anchors verbatim. Category neutrality is absolute: a game, art piece, music tool,
CRM, weird toy, and developer tool are all eligible for 10/10 on every criterion if they
satisfy that criterion exceptionally well FOR WHAT THEY ARE TRYING TO BE.
No aesthetic penalties. Signs of hurried AI work matter only as actual product-quality
problems (incoherence, broken flow, placeholder content), never as style.

## WebMCP Leverage — what does WebMCP change for this product?
Judge the delta WebMCP makes to reliability, precision, structured state access,
shared human-agent state, repeatability, and capability. Tool COUNT is not leverage.
- 9-10 TRANSFORMATIVE: the intended experience is impractical without the structured
  agent surface; human and agent share live product state; the collaboration could not
  be replicated comparably well by a general agent driving the UI.
- 7-8 MAJOR DELTA: without WebMCP a general agent is markedly less reliable/precise
  here; the agent is central to the product, not a bolt-on.
- 5-6 MEANINGFUL DELTA: existing capability packaged into a genuinely better product
  experience via structured tools; same outcome otherwise possible with real friction.
- 3-4 MINOR DELTA: generic browser automation achieves comparable outcomes; WebMCP is
  a convenience layer.
- 1-2 COSMETIC: exposes an action a UI button already offers; no meaningful change.

## Execution — is the actual product coherent, intentional, complete for its scope?
Stage 1 scores EXECUTION_CLAIMED: coherence/intentionality/completeness as evidenced by
the packet (description + packaging + video frames/transcript). Thin evidence caps the
score (end-to-end credit requires end-to-end evidence) and lowers confidence.
Stage 2 may rescore as EXECUTION_OBSERVED; latest evidence-backed score replaces, never
sums.
- 9-10: all surfaces agree; central workflow demonstrably completes; states handled;
  feels finished for its stated scope.
- 7-8: strong coherence and intentionality; central workflow appears functional; minor
  unfinished edges.
- 5-6: plausibly real with visible gaps; scope unclear or core not demonstrated
  end-to-end.
- 3-4: thin prototype; contradictions between surfaces; placeholder content.
- 1-2: non-functional or incoherent for its claimed scope.

## Creativity & Ambition — novelty of concept and interaction model
Judge ONLY: novelty of the concept, novelty of the interaction model, originality
relative to existing concepts, ambition, memorable/surprising use of the medium, and
the depth with which the concept is pursued. WebMCP indispensability belongs under
WebMCP Leverage, NOT here: a project can be extremely creative even if another agent
API could theoretically have enabled something similar.
- 9-10: genuinely novel concept or interaction model; ambitious scope pursued deeply
  and coherently; memorable.
- 7-8: fresh synthesis or notably original interaction; ambitious for a hackathon.
- 5-6: competent take on a known category with a real new angle.
- 3-4: derivative; novelty mostly surface details.
- 1-2: clone/template with trivial changes.
"That the category exists" is never a penalty; judge the submitted concept.

## Potential Impact — credible value for the intended audience
Judge: is there a real audience; is there a real problem, desire, or use case for that
audience; does the demonstrated solution genuinely address it. NO commercial adoption,
monetization, market pull, or startup viability is required. Entertainment, play, art,
creative expression, learning, niche hobby use, experimentation, and community value
are legitimate forms of impact. A tiny audience fully justifies a top score.
- 9-10: specific real audience with a real problem/desire; solution demonstrably
  addresses it; credible value, appeal, usefulness, or meaning for that audience.
- 7-8: clear audience and problem, good fit, credible value.
- 5-6: identifiable audience, looser fit or partial address.
- 3-4: speculative audience or strained fit; impact mostly aspirational.
- 1-2: no coherent audience/problem pairing.
Audience size is NOT a multiplier.

## Aggregate & ranking
aggregate = leverage + execution + impact + creativity (4-40). Nothing else enters.
Tie-breaks: Leverage, then Execution, then Impact, then Creativity, then higher
combined evidence confidence, then slug alphabetical. See PROTOCOL.md Ranking section.

Bridging the web accessibility gap with structured agent collaboration. Like Comment Story Updates Completed schedule booking screen aidra Inspiration Every year, WebAIM publishes the Screen Reader User Survey . In every edition, the #2 most cited barrier for screen reader users is "Interactive elements such as menus, tabs, and dialogs that don't behave as expected," second only to CAPTCHA. Compounding this, the WebAIM Million annual analysis tracked an average of 56.1 accessibility errors per home page in 2026, a 10.1% increase over the prior year. Inaccessible patterns such as drag-and-drop calendars, nested custom comboboxes, and unlabelled canvas widgets continue to create barriers for assistive technologies. Generic computer-use agents inherit many of these same failure modes. An agent that relies on vision, DOM inspection, clicking, or coordinate-based dragging can fail just as unpredictably when a complex widget has unexposed state or requires interactions that are difficult to interpret programmatically. I was inspired to build aidra to show how WebMCP (Web Model Context Protocol) can become an accessibility bridge. By exposing clean, typed, high-level tools directly inside the webpage, an AI agent can execute complex workflows without interacting with broken widgets. At the same time, every action can be narrated live through aria-live regions so the human remains informed and in control. What It Does aidra is an appointment booking portal built around one deliberately difficult interaction pattern: a drag-and-drop-only scheduling calendar with no click-to-select fallback. A user can complete the entire booking flow manually using a mouse or keyboard. They can also hand the workflow to an agent using natural language, such as: "Book me the earliest Tuesday morning appointment with Dr. Chen and add a note about my insurance card." Instead of trying to drag or click the calendar, the WebMCP agent uses 7 typed in-page tools: search_providers list_available_slots select_slot fill_intake_form submit_booking reschedule_booking cancel_booking These tools allow the agent to search providers, retrieve available slots, select an appointment, populate the intake form, and manage existing bookings without directly manipulating the inaccessible calendar widget. Every tool call is announced through an aria-live region and mirrored visually through an Agent Activity sidebar for sighted collaborators. Human Confirmation Is Enforced The agent cannot finalize an appointment. submit_booking only stages the appointment and opens an accessible confirmation dialog. The booking can only be finalized through an explicit human click on the Confirm button. This is enforced in the application logic, not simply described as a rule for the agent. Even if an agent calls submit_booking repeatedly, it cannot bypass the confirmation step because the only code path that marks an appointment as confirmed is the human-facing confirmation action. The agent handles the difficult interaction. The user retains authority over the action that matters. Why WebMCP? Without WebMCP, an AI agent has to perceive and manipulate the same interface a user does. That means guessing through pixels, DOM structure, focus states, and interaction patterns. With WebMCP, the webpage exposes semantic capabilities directly to the agent. Instead of asking: "Where is the calendar and how do I drag this appointment?" the agent can ask the page: "What providers are available?" "What appointment slots are available?" "Select this slot." The interaction becomes structured, typed, observable, and reviewable. This is what makes WebMCP particularly interesting for accessibility. The same abstraction that makes an agent more reliable can also make its actions easier for a human to understand and trust. How I Built It I designed aidra with a zero-build-step architecture for fast iteration, transparency, and auditability. Markup and UI: Semantic HTML5, Tailwind CSS via CDN, vanilla CSS custom properties, and native browser APIs. State Management: A centralized reactive store synchronizes manual DOM interactions and WebMCP tool executions through the same state and rendering pipeline. WebMCP Registration: I registered all 7 tools using document.modelContext.registerTool({...}) with strict JSON schemas and JSDoc @typedef annotations for editor-level type checking. WebMCP Compatibility: I built a small polyfill that detects whether document.modelContext is available natively and falls back to a local tool registry when it is not. Input Normalization: Tool arguments are normalized so each execute() function receives the same input shape across different invocation environments. Speech Pacing: I paced announcements so screen readers have time to finish communicating one action before the next tool executes. Live Observability: An in-page Agent Activity console streams tool invocations, parameters, and status changes so sighted collaborators can observe the agent working in real time. The shared state architecture was particularly important. Both manual interactions and agent tool calls write through the same state layer. This means the manual and agent-assisted experiences are not separate implementations that can quietly drift apart. Challenges I Faced 1. Modal Dismiss and ARIA Live Race Condition Problem: Closing the confirmation modal returned focus to the general page body. This caused screen readers to begin announcing unrelated page content at the same time the booking result was being announced. Solution: I gave the booking status region a tabindex="-1" focus anchor and moved focus there before triggering the announcement. This gives the screen reader a stable and meaningful landing point after the modal closes. 2. Form Input Cursor Jumping Problem: The render pipeline re-synced input values from state on every state change. During manual typing, this caused the cursor to jump to the end of the field after each keystroke. Solution: I added diff-checking guards so DOM values are only overwritten when they have actually diverged from the current state. 3. Cross-Environment WebMCP Tool Invocation Problem: Chrome's experimental WebMCP implementation and other agent-driven environments did not always pass tool arguments in the same shape. Some contexts provided objects while others provided JSON strings. Solution: I added an input normalization layer that parses and normalizes incoming arguments before they reach the tool execution logic. This keeps the tools consistent across environments. What I Learned 1. WebMCP Can Be Accessibility Infrastructure WebMCP should not only be viewed as an automation mechanism. Structured tools can provide a semantic interaction layer for web applications that would otherwise be difficult for both agents and assistive technologies to navigate reliably. 2. Co-Navigation Can Build More Trust Than Black-Box Delegation An agent performing actions on behalf of a user should not mean that the user loses visibility into what is happening. By combining WebMCP tools with live aria-live narration and a visual activity stream, aidra keeps the human involved throughout the workflow. The agent acts, but the user can hear and observe what it is doing. 3. Human Authority Should Be Architectural The confirmation gate taught me that trust is stronger when it is enforced by the system rather than communicated as an instruction. The agent can prepare the appointment, but it cannot cross the final confirmation boundary. That distinction makes the human's authority a property of the application itself. 4. Zero-Build Architecture Has Real Advantages Using native ES Modules, vanilla DOM APIs, and JSDoc typing gave me extremely fast iteration while working with an experimental browser API. There was no build pipeline between changing the code and testing the result, which made debugging and experimentation much faster. ClientWeave: An agent-native workspace where buyers and AI scope services, calculate seller-governed pricing, and book consultations—with humans retaining final control. Like Comment Story Updates buyer goal service discovery service details shared scope guided scope validation deterministic pricing consultation availability agent-capabilities human confirmation confirmed booking Inspiration Buying professional services is rarely a simple checkout flow. Buyers often begin with an incomplete goal, refine requirements through conversation, wait for a quote, and then schedule a consultation. AI agents can accelerate this process, but consequential actions—such as submitting a lead or booking time—should remain under direct human control. That tension inspired ClientWeave : an agent-native professional-services CPQ workspace where buyers and browser agents collaborate on the same scope, while the seller controls pricing and the buyer retains the final say. What it does ClientWeave creates a shared, attributed workspace for service discovery, scoping, pricing, and consultation booking. A browser agent can: Discover services relevant to the buyer’s goal Create and update a structured scope Calculate deterministic, seller-governed pricing Find available consultation times Finalize a confirmed lead or booking Every scope change records whether it came from the buyer or the agent, making the process transparent and auditable. The key safety boundary is intentionally simple: an agent cannot confirm an action on the buyer’s behalf. Before a lead is submitted or a consultation is booked, the buyer must directly confirm the exact current summary in the interface. If the scope or quote changes afterward, that confirmation becomes stale and finalization is rejected. How we built it ClientWeave is built with Next.js 16, React 19, TypeScript, Supabase, PostgreSQL, Drizzle ORM, Zod, and TanStack Query . The application exposes six browser-native WebMCP tools: discover_services create_scope update_scope price_scope find_consultation_slots finalize_confirmed_scope These tools are registered through document.modelContext.registerTool() with a compatibility fallback for older browsers. They use the same authenticated and audited HTTP boundaries as the regular user interface, so agent interactions do not bypass the application’s validation or security model. The codebase separates deterministic domain rules from framework and infrastructure concerns. Pricing uses canonical inputs, versioned rules, integer minor units, and per-line rounding to ensure that the same scope always produces a reproducible quote. Public scope links use restricted, expiring capabilities rather than owner-level access. We also added structured validation, origin checks, secure cookies, content security policies, rate limiting, redacted observability, audit events, and retention workflows. Challenges we faced The hardest challenge was designing collaboration without silently transferring authority from the person to the agent. Hiding a confirmation button from an agent was not enough; the server also needed to verify that a human directly confirmed the exact scope, quote, and intended action currently being finalized. Another challenge was keeping state consistent across the visual interface, WebMCP tools, pricing engine, and database. An agent could update a scope while the buyer was reviewing it, so we needed freshness checks and clear invalidation rules to prevent stale quotes or confirmations from being used. Designing safe public access was also demanding. Buyers should be able to participate without creating an account, but a private scope link must grant access to only one scope and must never provide workspace-owner privileges. Finally, we wanted the agent experience to be reliable without depending on brittle DOM scraping. Creating narrow, well-described WebMCP tools—and ensuring they behaved exactly like the human-facing workflows—required careful contract and integration testing. What we learned We learned that agent-native software works best when agents receive structured capabilities rather than instructions to imitate clicks. WebMCP made the available actions explicit, typed, and easier to audit. We also learned that human oversight should be enforced as a system invariant, not presented as a warning message. Direct confirmation, confirmation freshness, limited capabilities, and server-side authorization all work together to preserve meaningful human control. Most importantly, attribution changes the quality of collaboration. When every change clearly shows whether it came from a person or an agent, users can review the process instead of merely trusting the final result. What’s next Next, we want to expand ClientWeave with richer seller-defined pricing rules, reusable scope templates, additional scheduling integrations, and collaborative comments. We also plan to explore approval policies for larger deals while preserving the same core principle: agents can help move work forward, but people remain responsible for consequential decisions. Like Comment Story Updates Inspiration Production incidents are difficult because engineers have to investigate metrics, deployments, dependencies, and logs while customers are still affected. I was interested in the idea of letting an AI agent help investigate production incidents without giving it full control. That led me to build Agent Operations Center, where the agent investigates the problem and I stay in control of the actual fix. What it does Agent Operations Center is an incident-response control room where a human engineer and an AI agent work the same production outage together. The page exposes nine WebMCP tools the agent uses to investigate — but it cannot apply the fix itself. The agent investigates: service health against 7-day baselines, deployment timing against the incident, dependency direction for blast radius, and its own log queries. It records findings with cited evidence, so the human audits reasoning rather than a summary. The dashboard reacts live, with nobody touching the mouse. Cards highlight as it inspects them, the log panel narrows to its query, every call appears in an activity feed. The reasoning is genuinely the agent's. Tools return evidence, never conclusions — there is no "find the root cause" function. The scenario includes a decoy: a healthy service deployed more recently than the guilty one, so blaming the newest deploy gets it wrong. Then a human approval gate that is structural, not advisory. propose_remediation suspends mid-execution inside the browser until someone clicks APPROVE. The agent has no path to production — the click is what changes state, bound to the exact service and version proposed. One approval, one execution. The agent then verifies recovery and resolves the incident. How we built it Next.js 16, React 19, TypeScript, Tailwind v4, deployed on Vercel. A simulated production backend — services, deployments, dependencies, logs, metrics — lives in an in-memory store exposed to React through useSyncExternalStore, since tool calls originate outside React and there's no render pass to hook into. Nine tools are registered with document.modelContext.registerTool. Five read, two write the agent's reasoning back into the UI, one gates approval, one verifies recovery. The design rule that mattered most: tools return evidence, never conclusions. There is no analyze_incident(). get_service_health returns p95Ms: 8.13× baseline and stops. The diagnosis has to happen in the model, or the demo is just a scripted animation. Challenges we ran into The API isn't documented yet. I guessed wrong three times. There's no unregisterTool — registration is one-way for the document's lifetime, so React StrictMode's double-mount collides with InvalidStateError: Duplicate tool name; a window-level flag is the only guard that survives both StrictMode and Fast Refresh. And executeTool takes a RegisteredTool handle from getTools(), not a name, with arguments as a JSON string — an object throws Failed to parse input arguments. I found that by writing a probe that tried six encodings. The hardest problem wasn't technical. My rollback worked perfectly when I called it by hand, and the agent refused to call it. It would investigate, propose a plan, get approved — then ask "May I execute the rollback?" and end its turn. Assistants won't invoke destructive tools without re-confirming in chat, and no amount of rewording the tool description changed that. So I inverted it. The rollback now runs inside approvePlan — the click handler. The agent proposes; the human's click executes. That turned a limitation into the better architecture: the agent has no path to production at all. Accomplishments that we're proud of The approval gate is structural, not advisory. The agent has no path to production at all — a human's click is the only thing that changes system state, and approval is bound to the exact service and version proposed. The agent's reasoning is actually part of the workflow. The scenario includes a decoy: a healthy service deployed more recently than the guilty one. An agent that blames the newest deploy gets it wrong. Ours rules it out by combining deploy timing, service health, and dependency direction in the demo scenario. The dashboard reacts to the agent, not the mouse. Service cards highlight as it inspects them; the log panel narrows to its query. That works because the WebMCP tools run inside the page and can update the same state the dashboard displays. I documented an undocumented API. executeTool's signature, the absence of unregisterTool, and the JSON-string argument encoding are all recorded in webmcp.d.ts with the console output that proves them. ## What we learned Tool descriptions are prompts, not documentation. Writing "get valid ids from get_system_status" inside an inputSchema field is what produces tool chaining instead of the agent guessing an id and failing. Return errors, don't throw them. { error, knownIds: [...] } lets an agent self-correct on the next call. An exception dead-ends it, and a stalled agent is the fastest way to lose a demo. External state needs useSyncExternalStore. Tool calls originate outside React, so there's no render pass to piggyback on — and mutating state in place means React never re-renders and the dashboard silently goes stale. Design around the model's caution, not against it. Fighting it cost hours. Accommodating it produced a stronger safety guarantee than the one originally designed. ## What's next for Agent Operations Center More scenarios, randomized — config errors, dependency outages, resource leaks — so the agent can't pattern-match its way to the answer. Competing remedies. Restart, feature flag, and rollback all viable, so the agent has to choose the safest one rather than having only one option. Real backends behind the same tools. Swapping the simulated store for Kubernetes wouldn't change a single tool description. Policy tiers. Low-risk actions auto-approved, high-risk requiring two approvers. Like 2 Comment Story Updates Inspiration I have a project called Sonny with around 264,464 lines of code. When Bhavya tried to understand its architecture, going through hundreds of source files and Markdown documents was extremely inefficient. We realized that the easiest way to understand a codebase this large was to see its architecture visually. This was not only our experience. GitHub Next independently identified the lack of visual explanations as a major limitation of current agent workflows. Their research explains that agents generate text much faster than humans can process it, while text is often the wrong medium for understanding complex relationships and architectural systems. That is exactly the problem Mermaid Atlas addresses. Existing Mermaid viewers also struggled with large diagrams and made them difficult to explore. In our testing, Mermaid Atlas could handle diagrams with more than 500 nodes that mermaid.live could not handle properly. That is what sparked the idea, and Bhavya came up with Mermaid Atlas. What it does Mermaid Atlas is a visual workspace for exploring large Mermaid diagrams. A person can import Mermaid or Markdown files, search for nodes, inspect relationships, navigate through dependencies, and edit the source while viewing the rendered result. Through ten WebMCP tools, an AI agent can work with the same live graph. It can: Find components using natural language Inspect dependencies and blast radius Trace paths between services Detect cycles, isolated nodes, entry points, and dead ends Create guided walkthroughs Make validated source changes Undo its latest change safely Everything the agent does is visible on the canvas through selections, walkthroughs, source updates, and the Agent activity history. The app opens with a complete Northwind architecture, so judges can start using it immediately without creating an account or uploading anything. How we built it Mermaid Atlas originally started as a proof of concept built with HTML and JavaScript. When we joined the OpenAI WebMCP Challenge, we migrated it to React and built the current version. We used Mermaid for rendering and ELK for laying out large graphs. Different Mermaid diagram types generate completely different SVG structures, so we built adapters that connect the original Mermaid source to the correct rendered nodes, edges, groups, and source lines. We then added the WebMCP layer, the diagram library, Graph Index, walkthroughs, safe editing, activity history, themes, and automated tests. Challenges we ran into The most frustrating challenge was that WebMCP does not currently support prompt primitives. Registering tools was not enough because the agent also needed to understand when and how to use them. We solved this by creating get_atlas_guide , a tool that teaches the agent the correct workflows, tool order, limits, and safety rules. We also found that tool behavior differed between models. Some descriptions worked well with GPT-5.6 Sol but were not followed as reliably by GPT-5.6 Terra. We reworked our WebMCP tool descriptions, schemas, results, and error messages until both models could use the tools consistently. Another major challenge was supporting different Mermaid renderers. Flowcharts, sequence diagrams, class diagrams, states, and architecture diagrams all generate different SVG structures. A generic solution caused incorrect highlighting and sometimes changed the appearance of diagram elements. We had to build renderer-specific semantic adapters instead. Safe agent editing was also difficult. We needed to prevent invalid Mermaid syntax, stale edits, and undo operations that could overwrite newer human changes. Accomplishments that we're proud of We are most proud that Mermaid Atlas works reliably for humans and agents even with massive Mermaid diagrams. We built ten WebMCP tools that operate on the same graph the human is viewing, made agent actions visible and reversible, and tested the application across 46 Mermaid fixtures. We also made diagrams with more than 500 nodes practical to search, navigate, and understand. What we learned We learned that building agent tools is not only about exposing functions. Tool names, descriptions, schemas, limits, and error messages determine whether an agent can use them reliably. We also learned that human control means making agent actions visible and reversible, not just asking for confirmation. Most importantly, we learned that handling large diagrams is both a rendering problem and an information-retrieval problem. Agents need focused graph queries instead of the entire diagram. What's next for Mermaid Atlas Next, we want to add optional accounts, cloud synchronization, and real-time collaboration so teams can work on architecture diagrams together like they would in Google Docs. The current local, no-account version will remain available. My intent is to get it as soon as possible. Hence, it inspired me to create LightningPricePilot - a Two-sided Intent Market so that shopper can better communicate their intent to the merchant. What it does LightningPricePilot is a two-sided intent marketplace where shoppers and merchants use AI agents to discover fairer laptop prices. Instead of scraping product cards, a shopper’s agent uses structured WebMCP tools to search laptops, compare new and refurbished prices, negotiate within merchant-defined guardrails, join group buys, subscribe to target-price bargains, receive notifications, and prepare a mock checkout. On the other side, a merchant’s agent can analyze aggregate demand, review offer activity, adjust private pricing policies, and publish limited bargains to matching shoppers. The experience keeps people in control: commitments, policy changes, bargain publication, and checkout require visible human approval. Merchant price floors remain private, shopper targets are shown only in aggregate, and the demo never collects payment or personal information. Why your use case is a strong fit for WebMCP Product discovery and pricing depend on live website state: condition-specific inventory, current and group-buy prices, group progress, active bargains, offer expiry, and merchant pricing policies. These details are difficult for an agent to understand reliably by reading page text or clicking through filters. WebMCP turns that state into precise, discoverable actions. On the shopper page, an agent can search the laptop catalogue, compare condition, group, and bargain price paths, request a rule-bound offer, join a group buy, save a target-price subscription, read bargain notifications, and prepare a mock checkout. On the merchant page, a different agent can review aggregate demand and offer activity, update private pricing guardrails, and publish or close limited bargains. Each page registers only the tools appropriate to its role, with the room code already bound to every call. Both roles use the same validated room backend, so a shopper action can change aggregate demand and a merchant action can create a qualifying shopper notification without exposing the merchant’s private floor through the shopper tool surface. This transforms the storefront from a static catalogue into a structured negotiation space for people, shopper agents, and merchant agents. How it creates a better user experience The shopper describes the outcome once: “Find me a lightweight laptop under $900. Excellent refurbished condition is acceptable. Compare every available price path and negotiate toward $800 if sensible.” The agent can then present understandable options instead of another long list of products: Buy at the current price for the selected condition Join a group buy and show how many more commitments would unlock its next discount Use an active, inventory-limited merchant bargain Request a ten-minute offer or transparent counteroffer within the merchant’s private guardrails Save the product, acceptable condition, and target price for a future bargain Every price path states why it is available, whether it is currently unlocked, and what the shopper must do next. Offers include their rationale and expiry. Joining a group, saving a subscription, and placing the fictional mock order pause for visible human approval. The shopper no longer needs to repeatedly search the catalogue, compare product conditions, monitor group progress, or refresh the page looking for a relevant bargain. Matching merchant bargains create persisted in-app notifications, while server-sent events update the open room live. Merchants gain a complementary experience: they can see group commitments, subscriber counts, average target prices, and offer volume; tune the private floor and discount limits; and publish limited bargains that are matched against saved shopper intent. They do this without receiving a list of individual shopper budgets. Describe what people and agents can do together that was difficult or impossible before People remain responsible for the important trade-offs: Which laptop condition is acceptable What target price is worthwhile Whether to join a non-binding group buy Whether to save buying intent in the room Whether to complete a fictional checkout On the merchant side, whether to change pricing policy or publish or close a bargain Agents handle the structured, repetitive work: Search products by need, use case, condition, and budget Compare current condition, unlocked group, and active bargain prices Request an accepted offer or explain a policy-bound counteroffer Retrieve relevant bargain notifications Summarize aggregate demand and recent offer outcomes Turn a demand signal into a proposed limited bargain For example, the merchant agent can see that a product has five group commitments, three bargain subscribers, an aggregate average target, and recent offer activity. It can propose a price, condition, inventory allocation, expiry, and shopper-facing message. The merchant reviews the action before publication; matching subscribers then receive an in-app notification that the shopper agent can retrieve and explain. This creates something that was previously difficult: asynchronous negotiation around durable buyer intent . A shopper can save a condition and target price, leave the original search, and return to a persisted notification after a merchant publishes a qualifying bargain. Demand is used to unlock lower group prices and guide shared bargains—not to secretly calculate a different price for each shopper. Briefly explain how you implemented WebMCP LightningPricePilot implements two role-specific WebMCP surfaces with document.modelContext.registerTool . The shopper page registers seven tools: search_products — find up to five laptops by plain-language query, use case, condition, or maximum budget get_price_options — compare the current condition price, group-buy path, and any active bargain for one laptop request_offer — create a non-binding ten-minute accepted offer or counteroffer within merchant guardrails join_group_buy — record the shopper’s non-binding group commitment and unlock a tier when its threshold is reached subscribe_bargains — save a product, acceptable condition, and target price in the demo room get_notifications — retrieve persisted bargain notifications, treating merchant-authored messages as untrusted content prepare_mock_checkout — place a human-approved fictional order from a valid offer and decrement demo inventory The merchant page registers five tools: get_demand_summary — return group commitments, subscriber counts, aggregate average targets, and offer counts get_offer_activity — return recent accepted, countered, expired, and checked-out offers set_pricing_policy — update the private floor, maximum instant discount, and five- and ten-buyer discounts publish_bargain — publish a condition-specific, inventory-limited, expiring bargain and notify matching subscribers close_bargain — stop an active bargain while preserving its notification and audit history Each tool posts its role, action, and structured input to the room action API.
