# FROZEN SCORING RUBRIC (pre-registered before any Stage 1 review)

Scores are integers 1-10 on each of the FOUR official criteria. Every score carries:
rationale, evidence_surfaces, confidence (0-1). Reviewers use the full scale and these
band anchors verbatim. Category neutrality is absolute: a game, art piece, music tool,
CRM, weird toy, and developer tool are all eligible for 10/10 on every criterion if they

No aesthetic penalties. Signs of hurried AI work matter only as actual product-quality
problems (incoherence, broken flow, placeholder content), never as style.

## WebMCP Leverage — what does WebMCP change for this product?
Judge the delta WebMCP makes to reliability, precision, structured state access,
shared human-agent state, repeatability, and capability. Tool COUNT is not leverage.
- 9-10 TRANSFORMATIVE: the intended experience is impractical without the structured
  agent surface; human and agent share live product state; the collaboration could not
  be replicated comparably well by a general agent driving the UI.
- 7-8 MAJOR DELTA: without WebMCP a general agent is markedly less reliable/precise
  here; the agent is central to the product, not a bolt-on.
- 5-6 MEANINGFUL DELTA: existing capability packaged into a genuinely better product
  experience via structured tools; same outcome otherwise possible with real friction.
- 3-4 MINOR DELTA: generic browser automation achieves comparable outcomes; WebMCP is
  a convenience layer.
- 1-2 COSMETIC: exposes an action a UI button already offers; no meaningful change.

## Execution — is the actual product coherent, intentional, complete for its scope?
Stage 1 scores EXECUTION_CLAIMED: coherence/intentionality/completeness as evidenced by
the packet (description + packaging + video frames/transcript). Thin evidence caps the
score (end-to-end credit requires end-to-end evidence) and lowers confidence.
Stage 2 may rescore as EXECUTION_OBSERVED; latest evidence-backed score replaces, never
sums.
- 9-10: all surfaces agree; central workflow demonstrably completes; states handled;
  feels finished for its stated scope.
- 7-8: strong coherence and intentionality; central workflow appears functional; minor
  unfinished edges.
- 5-6: plausibly real with visible gaps; scope unclear or core not demonstrated
  end-to-end.
- 3-4: thin prototype; contradictions between surfaces; placeholder content.
- 1-2: non-functional or incoherent for its claimed scope.

## Creativity & Ambition — novelty of concept and interaction model
Judge ONLY: novelty of the concept, novelty of the interaction model, originality
relative to existing concepts, ambition, memorable/surprising use of the medium, and
the depth with which the concept is pursued. WebMCP indispensability belongs under
WebMCP Leverage, NOT here: a project can be extremely creative even if another agent
API could theoretically have enabled something similar.
- 9-10: genuinely novel concept or interaction model; ambitious scope pursued deeply
  and coherently; memorable.
- 7-8: fresh synthesis or notably original interaction; ambitious for a hackathon.
- 5-6: competent take on a known category with a real new angle.
- 3-4: derivative; novelty mostly surface details.
- 1-2: clone/template with trivial changes.
"That the category exists" is never a penalty; judge the submitted concept.

## Potential Impact — credible value for the intended audience
Judge: is there a real audience; is there a real problem, desire, or use case for that
audience; does the demonstrated solution genuinely address it. NO commercial adoption,
monetization, market pull, or startup viability is required. Entertainment, play, art,
creative expression, learning, niche hobby use, experimentation, and community value
are legitimate forms of impact. A tiny audience fully justifies a top score.
- 9-10: specific real audience with a real problem/desire; solution demonstrably
  addresses it; credible value, appeal, usefulness, or meaning for that audience.
- 7-8: clear audience and problem, good fit, credible value.
- 5-6: identifiable audience, looser fit or partial address.
- 3-4: speculative audience or strained fit; impact mostly aspirational.
- 1-2: no coherent audience/problem pairing.
Audience size is NOT a multiplier.

## Aggregate & ranking
aggregate = leverage + execution + impact + creativity (4-40). Nothing else enters.
Tie-breaks: Leverage, then Execution, then Impact, then Creativity, then higher
combined evidence confidence, then slug alphabetical. See PROTOCOL.md Ranking section.

Like Comment Story Updates 1 Inspiration Every AI video clipper works the same way: upload, wait, receive highlights, then fix them somewhere else. The model does the judging and the editing, and you get whichever boundaries it computed. I wanted the opposite division of labour. An agent is good at reading a transcript and saying which stretches stand on their own. A human is good at watching the footage and feeling where a cut lands. WebMCP made it possible to put both in the same page, editing the same clip, with the browser keeping both honest about who changed what. A cutlet is a small cut. Small cuts, served fresh. What it does Cutlet is a rough-cut editor for spoken video. It is not a clip finder; no model scores your footage. The page holds the video, a transcript broken into sentences with stable ids, and a rail of clips. The agent reaches all of it through ten WebMCP tools registered on the top-level document. The core loop is anchor and expand : You click the sentence that must survive. The agent reads a window around it and proposes a clip as a range of sentence ids, s0009–s0013 , never floating timestamps. The player runs exactly that range and stops. You drop a line or move an edge. The clip's revision bumps. The agent's next write carries the revision it last saw. Yours is newer, so the write is refused with the real revision in the error. It calls get_editor_state and revises from your edit, not over it. cut_clip c2 keep s0008,s0010,s0011,s0013 → revision 2, 3 pieces, 37s ↓ human drops s0011 in the transcript → revision 5 cut_clip c2 expectedRevision 4 → REFUSED "it is now 5" get_editor_state → sees the real cut The active clip is coloured by who last touched it: blue while it is the agent's proposal, amber the moment you edit it, in the transcript, the timeline and the card. Beyond the loop: "find the best clips in this" makes the agent skim the whole transcript and create one clip per moment, each with a title and a reason. "Cut this to forty seconds" turns a clip into several pieces with gaps, and playback jumps the gaps. Every dropped sentence stays visible, struck through, with a control to put it back. When the recogniser missed a full stop, you select the words you want and make them their own sentence with their own id, so a boundary you can point at becomes a boundary the agent can name. The agent can split too. The agent gets every cut twice: the source transcript, and the sequence as it plays , with each gap marked by what was dropped and how long it ran. That is how it catches a dangling "its conquest" whose antecedent left with the cut. Export re-encodes each piece in the tab with ffmpeg.wasm, so boundaries are frame-accurate rather than keyframe-snapped, joins them, and writes an .srt rebased across the cut. No backend, no UI library, no state library. 45 Node tests run directly against the TypeScript, covering parsing, sentence splitting, selection, reordering and tool registration. Transcript pipeline. Word-level JSON is primary; Whisper, Deepgram and ElevenLabs Scribe all parse, with SRT and VTT as coarse fallbacks. Sentence splitting handles abbreviations, initials, rhetorical pauses and a clause backoff at the length ceiling. Preview is the native <video> element, with the range boundary enforced on requestAnimationFrame , because timeupdate fires at about 4 Hz and visibly overshoots. Export is the single-thread @ffmpeg/core , lazy-loaded on first use. Single-thread means no SharedArrayBuffer , which means no COOP/COEP headers, which is why it deploys to plain static hosting. Tools register through document.modelContext.registerTool on the top-level document, with an AbortController for teardown. Read tools carry readOnlyHint . Keyboard. The transcript is a listbox with one tab stop: arrows move the anchor, shift extends it, Enter plays, ⌘-click banks a range. Challenges I ran into WebMCP is one-directional. A page cannot call an agent. There is no "find clips" button because there cannot be one, so the empty state's job is to tell you what to ask. Ids stop being positional the moment you split. Renumbering after a split would silently repoint every later id at its neighbour, and a stale id that resolves to the wrong sentence is worse than one that fails to resolve. So the new half gets a fresh number and nothing after it moves. The agent kept overwriting the human's anchor. preview_clip used to set the selection, so an agent auditioning a range destroyed the very mark it was told to build around. Auditions now have their own state and render dashed, in the agent's blue, beside the anchor. Bugs only testing found. That is why the sequence timeline is read-only, why sentence splitting exists, and why the revision check works at all. Optimistic concurrency turned out to be the right primitive for a human and an agent sharing one document. What's next More WebMCP hosts as they ship. Word-level trims inside a split. A WebMCP-native football play designer that helps coaches expose spatial and timing risks, preview tactical fixes, and approve every change before it reaches the playbook. Like Comment Story Updates Thumbnail overview of FieldCraft Agent suggestions remain staged in a playable Before/After review the coach sees the risk change from 2 to 1 and decides whether to apply it Design, time, animate, and export complete 11-on-11 concepts in a coach-first play workspace Fieldcraft's WebMCP tools identify the exact 2.15-second window where #52 can close the play-side lane, then focus the evidence on the field Inspiration A football play can look perfect on a whiteboard and still fail because one defender reaches the point of attack half a second before the blocker. Most play-design tools help coaches draw assignments. I wanted to build one that could also expose timing and spacing problems without pretending to predict the outcome of a game. I started with a strict rule: Fieldcraft had to be useful without AI. The agent would assist the coach, not replace the product or the coach’s judgment. What it does Fieldcraft is a complete 11-on-11 American football play designer. Coaches can: Start from eight editable common play presets Move players and build routes, runs, blocks, and motion Animate every assignment on a shared timeline Retiming assignments and immediately see every player’s dot trail update Edit Bézier paths, timing, technique, depth, color, and targets Undo, redo, duplicate, import, export, and save locally Export a clean play sheet as a PNG When Fieldcraft opens inside a WebMCP-compatible browser, it exposes seven site tools to ChatGPT or Codex. The agent can read the exact play state, run deterministic spatial checks, focus the field on a dangerous moment, and stage a proposed repair. My main demonstration starts with Pistol Counter . The analysis finds that #52 OLB can close within 1.29 yards of the halfback at 2.15 seconds before a blocker arrives. The agent focuses on that exact moment and proposes a new path for #66 RG. The proposal opens in a playable Before/After Coach Review . The coach can compare both versions and see the detected risks change from 2 to 1. Nothing enters the saved playbook until the coach explicitly approves it. All findings are presented as spatial and timing risks, not outcome predictions. How I built it Fieldcraft uses React 19, Vite 6, TypeScript, Zustand, Motion, and an interactive SVG field. The editor stores positions in football coordinates across a 40-by-53.3-yard surface. Playback, field trails, timeline dots, and spatial analysis all use the same interpolation engine, so the evidence shown to the agent matches what the coach sees. The analysis engine samples each play every 0.05 seconds. It checks for: Runner and defender threat windows Early, late, or absent blockers Rush and blitz pressure near the quarterback Sustained route congestion Uncovered route terminals Assignments extending beyond the play duration The page registers seven tools through document.modelContext.registerTool() : get_playbook get_play analyze_play focus_finding stage_play_changes commit_play_changes discard_play_changes Every human and agent edit crosses the same typed command boundary. Agent proposals remain separate from the canonical playbook, validate against the play revision, and become one undoable command only after approval. There is no embedded chatbot, model API, backend, or account system. The browser agent handles tactical reasoning while the site supplies structured play data, geometry, validation, and safe editor actions. Challenges I ran into The tactical canvas was the first major challenge. At shorter desktop heights, the field could compress until players, paths, and labels overlapped. I reworked the layout and field scaling so the editor remains legible at both 1440×1024 and 1280×800. The second challenge was keeping the analysis honest. An agent can describe a screenshot convincingly while getting the geometry wrong. I separated deterministic evidence from model reasoning and made every finding include player IDs, a time window, minimum distance, timing margin, and focus time. The hardest product decision was how agents should change a play. Direct edits were too easy to lose track of. Fieldcraft instead stages an immutable proposal, locks normal editing during review, supports Before/After playback, rejects stale revisions, and requires explicit confirmation before committing. Accomplishments that I'm proud of Fieldcraft remains a polished, useful editor when WebMCP is unavailable The agent and coach work on the same live page and the same play state All 22 players remain independently editable across eight starter concepts Analysis, animation, and the timeline share one timing engine Agent changes are visible, reversible, revision-safe, and undoable The complete application runs client-side with no external runtime dependencies What I learned Useful agent tools need narrower boundaries than ordinary application APIs. Reading a bounded section, focusing on one finding, and staging a review gives the agent enough control without hiding consequential changes from the user. I also learned that visual feedback matters as much as the tool call. Scrubbing to 2.15 seconds and drawing the danger directly on the field makes the agent’s reasoning understandable in a way that a paragraph alone cannot. What's next for Fieldcraft I want to add more defensive shells, coach-defined rules, coverage-specific analysis, reusable install packages, and cloud playbooks for staff collaboration. A future server-side MCP integration could support playbook work when the webpage is not open, while WebMCP remains the best interface for live visual review. Shop together with your AI agent, but only you can hit "buy." Like Comment Story Updates Inspiration The WebMCP spec's own authors flagged the sharpest risk in agentic commerce in their own draft. Section 6.3.2.3 walks through an ambiguously named tool, finalizeCart , that an agent could call and silently complete a real purchase. An agent shopping "on your behalf" today has two bad options: screen-scrape the UI (brittle, no ground truth), or call a private backend API directly (bypasses whatever safety rails the storefront's own UI enforces). WebMCP offered a third path: let the agent call the exact tool the UI itself uses, on the exact same page state a human is looking at. We wanted to build a storefront that took the spec's own warning seriously enough to actually engineer around it, not just write a paragraph about "responsible AI" in a README. What it does Shopfront Copilot is a storefront where a shopper and their AI agent browse, compare, and buy together. The agent can: Search & filter the catalog by price, category, and other bounded fields Compare 2-4 products side by side in a real on-page comparison table Check return policy for a product Add / remove cart items , reversible either direction Watch a product for price drops All of it runs through the same WebMCP tools registered on document.modelContext that the storefront's own UI calls. A shopper also controls an Autonomy Dial (read-only vs. browse-and-cart) and an optional spending cap , both enforced with machine-readable refusal codes the agent can act on. The checkout gate: the headline feature Action Exposed to the agent? Can it complete a purchase? checkout (WebMCP tool) Yes No. Always returns { status: "confirmation_required", cart_summary } . Never mutates the cart. Confirm-purchase click Not a registered tool at all. Yes, this is the only thing that finalizes an order, and it's gated behind WebAuthn on a plain onClick handler an agent can't reach. There is no tool an agent can call, no matter how it's worded, that completes a purchase. How we built it Stack: Next.js (App Router), TypeScript, React, Tailwind State: client-side React context persisted to localStorage , no backend database Tools: defined once in lib/webmcp/tools/ , registered via document.modelContext.registerTool() , so a human's click and an agent's tool call run through the identical code path into the identical shared cart The safety layer is factored out into its own framework-agnostic package, webmcp-guardrails : Autonomy tiers & spending caps that gate mutating calls Structural-absence check that proves confirm_purchase was never registered, rather than just asserting it in prose Flight recorder that logs every tool call as structured, timestamped data Intent-snapshot diffing that freezes what an agent saw when it called checkout and flags anything that changed by the time a human confirms To prove the pattern holds against a real model, not just scripted scenarios, we added a live agent mode backed by Groq , driving the exact same tool registry and guardrails a simulated harness uses. Checkout confirmation is server-verified through WebAuthn with zero database, and everything degrades gracefully in browsers without document.modelContext support, which today is most of them. Challenges we ran into Making the checkout gate airtight meant resisting the temptation to "just add a permission check" to a confirm_purchase tool, since a runtime check is something an agent, or a future refactor, could route around. We made the property structural instead of declarative : the function that finalizes an order simply is never wired into the tool registry. We also hit five places where the WebMCP spec doesn't yet define an answer: No standard classification for irreversible actions No agent identity to key a per-agent rate limit or trust tier on No way for a shopper's consent decision to persist and reach the agent before it fails once No push channel for telling an idle agent a watched price actually dropped No standard primitive for an agent to pause and ask a human a question We wrote up each gap and our workaround as an RFC rather than silently papering over them. Keeping the demo honest against a real model was its own challenge: a live Groq-backed agent will happily try creative phrasings to get a tool to do more than its contract allows, which made for a good adversarial test of whether our guardrails actually held. Accomplishments that we're proud of confirm_purchase cannot be reached by an agent under any circumstance, and we can prove it structurally , not just document it webmcp-guardrails is genuinely reusable: pure TypeScript, no dependency on React or this app's domain Live agent mode means the checkout gate is tested against a real model actively trying to complete a purchase, not just scripted scenarios What we learned The safest thing you can do with a dangerous capability isn't a well-worded permission check, it's not exposing the capability's implementation to the caller at all . We also came away convinced that several of the gaps we hit (agent identity, a standard human-question primitive) sit upstream of a lot of other trust problems in agentic web tooling, and are worth pushing at the spec level rather than solving per-site forever.
