# FROZEN SCORING RUBRIC (pre-registered before any Stage 1 review)

Scores are integers 1-10 on each of the FOUR official criteria. Every score carries:
rationale, evidence_surfaces, confidence (0-1). Reviewers use the full scale and these
band anchors verbatim. Category neutrality is absolute: a game, art piece, music tool,
CRM, weird toy, and developer tool are all eligible for 10/10 on every criterion if they
satisfy that criterion exceptionally well FOR WHAT THEY ARE TRYING TO BE.
No aesthetic penalties. Signs of hurried AI work matter only as actual product-quality
problems (incoherence, broken flow, placeholder content), never as style.

## WebMCP Leverage — what does WebMCP change for this product?
Judge the delta WebMCP makes to reliability, precision, structured state access,
shared human-agent state, repeatability, and capability. Tool COUNT is not leverage.
- 9-10 TRANSFORMATIVE: the intended experience is impractical without the structured
  agent surface; human and agent share live product state; the collaboration could not
  be replicated comparably well by a general agent driving the UI.
- 7-8 MAJOR DELTA: without WebMCP a general agent is markedly less reliable/precise
  here; the agent is central to the product, not a bolt-on.
- 5-6 MEANINGFUL DELTA: existing capability packaged into a genuinely better product
  experience via structured tools; same outcome otherwise possible with real friction.
- 3-4 MINOR DELTA: generic browser automation achieves comparable outcomes; WebMCP is
  a convenience layer.
- 1-2 COSMETIC: exposes an action a UI button already offers; no meaningful change.

## Execution — is the actual product coherent, intentional, complete for its scope?
Stage 1 scores EXECUTION_CLAIMED: coherence/intentionality/completeness as evidenced by
the packet (description + packaging + video frames/transcript). Thin evidence caps the
score (end-to-end credit requires end-to-end evidence) and lowers confidence.
Stage 2 may rescore as EXECUTION_OBSERVED; latest evidence-backed score replaces, never
sums.
- 9-10: all surfaces agree; central workflow demonstrably completes; states handled;
  feels finished for its stated scope.
- 7-8: strong coherence and intentionality; central workflow appears functional; minor
  unfinished edges.
- 5-6: plausibly real with visible gaps; scope unclear or core not demonstrated
  end-to-end.
- 3-4: thin prototype; contradictions between surfaces; placeholder content.
- 1-2: non-functional or incoherent for its claimed scope.

## Creativity & Ambition — novelty of concept and interaction model
Judge ONLY: novelty of the concept, novelty of the interaction model, originality
relative to existing concepts, ambition, memorable/surprising use of the medium, and
the depth with which the concept is pursued. WebMCP indispensability belongs under
WebMCP Leverage, NOT here: a project can be extremely creative even if another agent
API could theoretically have enabled something similar.
- 9-10: genuinely novel concept or interaction model; ambitious scope pursued deeply
  and coherently; memorable.
- 7-8: fresh synthesis or notably original interaction; ambitious for a hackathon.
- 5-6: competent take on a known category with a real new angle.
- 3-4: derivative; novelty mostly surface details.
- 1-2: clone/template with trivial changes.
"That the category exists" is never a penalty; judge the submitted concept.

## Potential Impact — credible value for the intended audience
Judge: is there a real audience; is there a real problem, desire, or use case for that
audience; does the demonstrated solution genuinely address it. NO commercial adoption,
monetization, market pull, or startup viability is required. Entertainment, play, art,
creative expression, learning, niche hobby use, experimentation, and community value
are legitimate forms of impact. A tiny audience fully justifies a top score.
- 9-10: specific real audience with a real problem/desire; solution demonstrably
  addresses it; credible value, appeal, usefulness, or meaning for that audience.
- 7-8: clear audience and problem, good fit, credible value.
- 5-6: identifiable audience, looser fit or partial address.
- 3-4: speculative audience or strained fit; impact mostly aspirational.
- 1-2: no coherent audience/problem pairing.
Audience size is NOT a multiplier.

## Aggregate & ranking
aggregate = leverage + execution + impact + creativity (4-40). Nothing else enters.
Tie-breaks: Leverage, then Execution, then Impact, then Creativity, then higher
combined evidence confidence, then slug alphabetical. See PROTOCOL.md Ranking section.

I wanted to see how close a website could get to that, now that it finally has a place to enforce it. What it does TaskFence is a delegation layer that sits on a site's WebMCP boundary. The clearest way to describe it is to follow what happens in the demo. You begin with a form you need completed. Rather than the site choosing what that form is, you upload the one you actually care about — I test with a real university scholarship PDF — and the page reads the fields out of your document. If you had already filled in parts of that PDF before uploading it, TaskFence treats those values as answers you have given and places them on the form as yours, which becomes important a moment later. You then describe the task in a sentence, in your own words, including anything you want left alone: "fill in the blanks from my documents, don't change anything I've already answered, ask me if something is missing, ask before you submit, and tell me if any field doesn't look right." That sentence is compiled into a structured contract of permitted, forbidden and approval-required actions. Before anything runs, the page shows you what it understood, and equally what it could not interpret — a boundary you believed you had set but which was quietly misread would undermine the whole idea, so it is surfaced rather than assumed. The agent then works. Everything within your boundary proceeds without interruption: it reads the form and your documents, checks the answers already present, and completes the blanks it is permitted to complete. You are not consulted about any of it, because you have already agreed to it. When it reaches something outside the boundary, the tool call is held before the write takes place. You are shown what the agent intended to do, which part of your own sentence stopped it, and precisely what permission is being requested. You can decline, or allow it — and if the value the agent proposed is wrong, you can correct it first, so what is written is your version. Afterwards there is a ledger of every decision alongside the rule that produced it, which can be replayed and exported. WCAG gives us solid guidance on consistent navigation, understandable content, motion, focus, and giving people enough time to complete tasks. Those standards matter, but they don't cover every way someone might struggle to process what's happening on a page. A site can check every accessibility box and still feel like a lot. Picture a typical homepage: a promotional banner scrolling across the top, a popup asking you to subscribe, several competing calls to action, maybe a video, and a nav menu with a dozen choices. None of that is necessarily inaccessible on its own. Put it all together, though, and it becomes hard to process, especially for someone with a cognitive or learning disability. Some sites offer controls that help, and browsers and operating systems provide preferences too. But if a site is already overwhelming, asking someone to hunt down another menu, parse a list of settings, and figure out which combination might help just adds more burden. WebMCP gave me a different way to think about the problem. What if I could just tell my AI agent, "There's too much happening on this page," and the site could tell the agent what it's actually capable of changing? What it does I built Adaptive WebMCP to explore that idea. It includes three demo experiences modeled after a state government site, a county government site, and a healthcare site, three categories where digital accessibility is especially important and often legally required. Each site can adapt in several ways: text can get larger, contrast can increase, spacing can change, movement can stop, and navigation can simplify. I also built in plain-language content, since cognitive accessibility isn't only a visual concern. Government and healthcare sites lean heavily on legal, institutional, or medical language that's genuinely hard to process. The experience can also reorganize around a person's goal. If someone needs to renew a driver's license, find urgent care, or pay property taxes, the agent can focus the whole site on that task and clear away the unrelated information competing for their attention. All of this is exposed through WebMCP. Someone might say, "The movement is distracting me," "I don't understand what this means," or just, "Make this easier to use." You drag a gain, you squint at a step response, you decide the wobble looks a bit better, you drag again. When we started reading the WebMCP spec, the first thing we wanted to know was what an agent could actually do on a page like that. The answer turned out to be almost nothing, and the reason was specific enough to be interesting. A step response, a Bode plot and a root locus are drawn on an HTML <canvas> . A canvas is coloured pixels. There is no text under it, no DOM, nothing to scrape. Hand a vision model a screenshot of a step response and ask for the overshoot and you get "roughly 25%" — a guess read off pixel positions. You cannot tune a controller on "roughly". The entire task is hitting a number like "under 10%", and 12% and 18% call for opposite corrections. So this was not a case of an agent being clumsy with a UI. The information the agent needed did not exist anywhere it could reach. It only existed as floating point numbers inside the running program. That felt like the sharpest possible answer to "why WebMCP?", so we built the page around it. What it does Nirnay is a control-system design bench. You pick a plant, drag sliders for the three PID gains, and three plots redraw instantly — step response, Bode, and a root locus / pole-zero map. On the right there is a scoreboard: every performance metric with a green PASS or red FAIL chip against design targets you set yourself. The page exposes eight WebMCP tools, so an AI agent in the same page can read the exact simulation numbers and tune alongside you. The important part is that there is one shared state behind everything. The sliders and the agent's tools write to the same place. When the agent changes a gain, the sliders physically move, the plots redraw and the scoreboard updates while you watch. You can grab a slider back mid-conversation and the agent's next reading reflects it. Neither side is driving a copy of the other's world. There is also an agent activity log on the page that prints a line every time a tool is called, so a human watching the screen can see exactly what just happened and why. The page opens in a deliberately failing state — 50.6% overshoot against a 10% target, three of four specs red. That is on purpose. It gives the agent something real to fix instead of a victory lap. The eight tools Five read, three write. Read: get_system (full current setup), run_step_response (exact overshoot, rise time, settling time, steady-state error), get_frequency_response (gain and phase margins, crossover frequencies), get_pole_zero_map (closed-loop poles, damping, and how far the gain can be pushed before instability), evaluate_design (score against targets, and what is still failing and by how much). Write: set_controller_gains , set_plant , set_design_spec . Three design decisions shaped how well this actually works: Every write tool returns the full measured result. set_controller_gains does not reply "done". It replies with the new overshoot, settling time, margins, pole positions and a pass/fail verdict. The obvious design is write-then-read, two calls per attempt. Returning the measurement with the write halves the round trips, but the real reason is that it keeps the agent anchored to a measurement instead of to its own prediction of what those gains would do. Agents are good at confidently predicting the wrong outcome. No tool ever returns a bare OK. Every response carries numbers the agent can check itself against. That is what lets it notice a change made things worse and back it out, rather than marching in the wrong direction. Bad input gets a sentence, not a stack trace. Pass a string where a number belongs and you get Error: Kp must be a finite number, got a string. A crashed tool call teaches an agent nothing. A sentence naming the mistake lets it retry. Read tools are marked with readOnlyHint so the browser does not interrupt with a confirmation prompt on every measurement, which matters a lot once the agent starts iterating. How we built it Everything runs in the browser. No server, no backend, no libraries, no build step — plain JavaScript files the browser loads directly. That was a deliberate choice: one deployable artifact, one failure point, and nothing between the plot and the tool that could make them disagree. The numerics, roughly in layers: Polynomial arithmetic as the base, since transfer functions are ratios of polynomials. Durand–Kerner for root finding, which chases all roots simultaneously. Roots sitting exactly at zero stall it, so those get peeled off first. RK4 integration for the step response, running twenty times finer than it records so the stored curve is smooth and trustworthy. Bisection to locate the gain and phase crossover frequencies exactly, rather than reporting the closest point on a sampled grid. Root locus by sweeping gain and matching each root to its nearest previous position, so drawn branches follow one root smoothly instead of jumping where two roots pass close together. Tool registration sits behind a feature check, so in a browser without WebMCP the page is simply a complete engineering tool with no agent. Nothing breaks and nothing looks broken. Who built what Nirnay was built by two of us. Challenges we ran into The bug that the cross-check caught. The gain at which a system goes unstable can be computed two entirely different ways: from the root locus, tracking roots in one mathematical space, and from the frequency response, tracking phase in a completely different one. They share no code. We wrote a test asserting they agree. They did not. The root locus said 6, the frequency path said 6.0577. The root locus was right. The bug was a rare edge case in the frequency path: the search grid happened to land exactly on the answer, which broke an assumption the refinement step depended on. We would never have found that by looking at a graph. Both curves looked fine. It is exactly the kind of quiet wrongness that makes a tool useless to an agent, because the agent has no way to sanity-check what it is handed — it will act on 6.0577 with total confidence.
