Scores are integers 1-10 on each of the FOUR official criteria. Every score carries:
rationale, evidence_surfaces, confidence (0-1). Reviewers use the full scale and these
band anchors verbatim. Category neutrality is absolute: a game, art piece, music tool,

## WebMCP Leverage — what does WebMCP change for this product?
Judge the delta WebMCP makes to reliability, precision, structured state access,
shared human-agent state, repeatability, and capability. Tool COUNT is not leverage.
- 9-10 TRANSFORMATIVE: the intended experience is impractical without the structured
  agent surface; human and agent share live product state; the collaboration could not
  be replicated comparably well by a general agent driving the UI.
- 7-8 MAJOR DELTA: without WebMCP a general agent is markedly less reliable/precise
  here; the agent is central to the product, not a bolt-on.
- 5-6 MEANINGFUL DELTA: existing capability packaged into a genuinely better product
  experience via structured tools; same outcome otherwise possible with real friction.
- 3-4 MINOR DELTA: generic browser automation achieves comparable outcomes; WebMCP is
  a convenience layer.
- 1-2 COSMETIC: exposes an action a UI button already offers; no meaningful change.

## Execution — is the actual product coherent, intentional, complete for its scope?
Stage 1 scores EXECUTION_CLAIMED: coherence/intentionality/completeness as evidenced by
the packet (description + packaging + video frames/transcript). Thin evidence caps the
score (end-to-end credit requires end-to-end evidence) and lowers confidence.
Stage 2 may rescore as EXECUTION_OBSERVED; latest evidence-backed score replaces, never
sums.
- 9-10: all surfaces agree; central workflow demonstrably completes; states handled;
  feels finished for its stated scope.
- 7-8: strong coherence and intentionality; central workflow appears functional; minor
  unfinished edges.
- 5-6: plausibly real with visible gaps; scope unclear or core not demonstrated
  end-to-end.
- 3-4: thin prototype; contradictions between surfaces; placeholder content.
- 1-2: non-functional or incoherent for its claimed scope.

## Creativity & Ambition — novelty of concept and interaction model
Judge ONLY: novelty of the concept, novelty of the interaction model, originality
relative to existing concepts, ambition, memorable/surprising use of the medium, and
the depth with which the concept is pursued. WebMCP indispensability belongs under
WebMCP Leverage, NOT here: a project can be extremely creative even if another agent
API could theoretically have enabled something similar.
- 9-10: genuinely novel concept or interaction model; ambitious scope pursued deeply
  and coherently; memorable.
- 7-8: fresh synthesis or notably original interaction; ambitious for a hackathon.
- 5-6: competent take on a known category with a real new angle.
- 3-4: derivative; novelty mostly surface details.
- 1-2: clone/template with trivial changes.
"That the category exists" is never a penalty; judge the submitted concept.

## Potential Impact — credible value for the intended audience
Judge: is there a real audience; is there a real problem, desire, or use case for that
audience; does the demonstrated solution genuinely address it. NO commercial adoption,
monetization, market pull, or startup viability is required. Entertainment, play, art,
creative expression, learning, niche hobby use, experimentation, and community value
are legitimate forms of impact. A tiny audience fully justifies a top score.
- 9-10: specific real audience with a real problem/desire; solution demonstrably
  addresses it; credible value, appeal, usefulness, or meaning for that audience.
- 7-8: clear audience and problem, good fit, credible value.
- 5-6: identifiable audience, looser fit or partial address.
- 3-4: speculative audience or strained fit; impact mostly aspirational.
- 1-2: no coherent audience/problem pairing.
Audience size is NOT a multiplier.

aggregate = leverage + execution + impact + creativity (4-40). Nothing else enters.
Tie-breaks: Leverage, then Execution, then Impact, then Creativity, then higher

An agent-native semantic workspace where ChatGPT proposes validated visual structures through WebMCP—and the human retains authority to accept, export, or undo. Like Comment Story Updates Inspiration Complex systems do not need more interface. They need better structure. Architecture diagrams, workflows, data models, and knowledge graphs already contain meaning, but turning that meaning into a useful visual usually requires authored coordinates, diagram syntax, manually positioned shapes, or generated output that is difficult to inspect. I built Orbweaver around a simpler principle: Understand complex systems while showing less. What it does Orbweaver turns coordinate-free semantic graphs into deterministic, inspectable visual structures. The input describes meaning: what exists; how things relate; which relationships matter; what evidence and claims support the structure. Orbweaver validates that meaning, computes the layout, and renders an accessible SVG artifact. The proposal contains no authored coordinates, SVG, HTML, CSS, or executable markup. The WebMCP playground extends this into a shared human-agent workspace. ChatGPT can read the live workspace contract, create or revise a semantic proposal, inspect an entity, and focus it in the visible diagram. The agent can propose meaning. It cannot accept its own work. Why WebMCP Without WebMCP, an agent would need to infer interface controls and repeatedly manipulate a large JSON editor. That is slow, fragile, and disconnected from the application’s actual domain model. With WebMCP, Orbweaver exposes six bounded, page-local tools: read the current workspace and revision; read the supported semantic contract; create a complete proposal; revise it using conflict-safe operations; inspect semantic identity and provenance; focus an entity in the visible workspace. The tools reuse the same application controller and validation boundary as the human interface. WebMCP does not introduce a second, privileged execution path. The human-agent experience A person describes an order-fulfillment system in ordinary language. ChatGPT discovers Orbweaver’s tools and supplies entities, relationships, groups, claims, and evidence—not pixels or drawing instructions. Orbweaver validates the entire proposal atomically, derives the geometry, and renders revision one as a validated but untrusted preview . The person and agent then work with the same live state. ChatGPT can add Delivery tracking, focus it, and expose its relationships for inspection. Expected-revision checks prevent stale writes when the shared workspace changes. Acceptance remains a human-only decision. The agent cannot accept a revision or enable export. The person can inspect, reject, or undo the change. In the demonstration, I manually undo the agent’s revision and Orbweaver restores the previous graph. How I built it Orbweaver’s core is a provider-neutral TypeScript library. A strict GraphProposal contract represents semantic content without layout coordinates. ELK.js computes the visual structure, while Orbweaver produces accessible SVG, stable semantic identities, selection behavior, provenance, and inspection details. The playground’s WebMCP adapter registers six tools through the browser’s page-local model context. Tool inputs are treated as untrusted data and pass through the existing validation layer. Create and revise operations are atomic, revisions are versioned, and semantic writes never bundle human acceptance. The implementation is tested with Vitest, Playwright, and axe-core accessibility checks. The npm package contains no AI provider, account requirement, telemetry, or cloud runtime dependency. Challenges The hardest problem was not drawing the graph. It was designing the authority boundary. A useful agent needed enough access to create meaningful structures without gaining the ability to silently approve its own output. This required explicit trust states, expected-revision conflict protection, atomic validation, reversible mutations, and accepted-only export. The other challenge was visual restraint: presenting enough information to understand a complex system without turning the workspace into an aircraft cockpit. Orbweaver keeps the interface secondary to the structure being examined. Accomplishments I’m proud of Published a provider-neutral open-source TypeScript library rather than embedding an AI service inside the product. Built six bounded WebMCP tools around the application’s existing controller and validation boundary. Made agent-authored changes versioned, atomic, inspectable, and reversible. Preserved human-only authority over acceptance and export. Produced deterministic, accessible SVG from semantic proposals containing no authored coordinates. Published the beta package, live playground, documentation, source, and reproducible demonstration. What I learned WebMCP is most valuable when it exposes an application’s domain semantics—not when it simply recreates button clicking as tools. The strongest human-agent experience is not full autonomy. It is shared context with asymmetric authority: the agent contributes reach and synthesis; the application enforces structure; the human retains judgment at the moment of consequence. What it does The official website of 油庫口蚵仔麵線 (Youkukou Oyster Vermicelli) now has a built-in "open a table" feature. The host gets a private link; only people with the link can order. Everyone orders from their own phone, every view syncs in realtime, and the host always sees the current combined order. Tables are fully isolated: three companies can each open a table at the same time without their orders mixing. Humans order through the UI; agents order through WebMCP tools declared on the same page. How we built it Lovable (React) + Supabase (anonymous auth, RLS, RPCs, realtime). The frontend is an interface, not a boundary: pricing, item validation, and permissions are all enforced in Postgres RPCs under RLS, so agents and humans go through the same rules. The page declares three WebMCP tools: get_table_status : marked readOnlyHint , returns structured JSON, with user-entered fields wrapped as user_content so they are treated as data, not instructions add_order : writes require an on-page human confirmation before execution close_table : registered only in the host's session; other participants' agents never see this tool All tools unregister when the table closes. Challenges we ran into The hardest part came first: breaking "+1 in a LINE group" into three actions (open, order, close) and deciding which rules belong in the database layer rather than the frontend. The second was environment setup: getting Chrome to actually call the WebMCP tools on the page took a fair amount of configuration and debugging. The third was pinning down where the latency actually lives. Measured on the page, tool execution is in the millisecond range: read tools return frontend state directly in 0.1–0.3 ms; write tools take 498 ms on the first call and settle at 66–72 ms after that. End to end, one order still takes about a minute. The gap sits entirely in the browser-side agent's tool discovery and reasoning; the page implementation itself is not the bottleneck. Accomplishments that we're proud of This is a 40-year-old street-food institution, and the second-generation owner built the system herself with AI vibe coding, so that customers' agents can place orders directly. Planning, architecture, and deployment were all done in-house, with no outside software vendor and no engineering team. The shop is the test bed: what gets built one day is in front of real customers the next. What we learned When the goal is clear enough, AI can build it. Writing code is not the barrier; knowing whose problem you are solving is. Then, service by service, connect Google Maps scooter-distance calculation, payment, the Lalamove delivery fleet, and e-invoicing, until every step from order to handoff is linked. JiuTuanZhuo is the entry point. From here we build inward, one piece at a time, into a full internal operating system for the shop that agents can run directly, ending with the POS itself. Ordering, ticketing, payment, fulfillment, and invoicing on one shared data layer is what a real closed loop looks like. Like Comment Story Updates Home Page Throughout high school, I led a nonprofit that impacted more than 20,000 people. Building the organization was one of the most meaningful experiences of my life, but when I moved to college, I could no longer give it the time and attention it deserved. I began trying to transition the organization to a new leader, only to discover how difficult nonprofit succession can be. The organization’s knowledge, relationships, documents, and responsibilities were spread across different places, and there was no simple way to prepare another student to take over. In college, I was selected to attend one of America’s premier youth service summits. At the conference, I met other young nonprofit founders who were experiencing the same problem. They had created organizations that were making a real difference, but graduation, college, and new responsibilities made it difficult for them to continue leading. Their nonprofits were not ending because their missions were no longer important. They were at risk because they did not have an effective way to find, prepare, and support their next leaders. That experience inspired me to build Nonprofit Pass, a WebMCP-powered platform that helps student-led nonprofits survive leadership transitions. The platform gives founders, potential successors, and AI agents a shared space in which they can plan and complete a responsible handoff. A student interested in becoming a successor can use Nonprofit Pass to discover leadership opportunities, search and filter organizations, save nonprofits to a shortlist, compare opportunities, and prepare an application. A founder can evaluate whether an organization is ready for succession, identify missing documents or institutional knowledge, create a structured 30-day transition plan, and leave important notes for the next leader. Both people can see the same transition progress, creating continuity between the person who built the organization and the person who will lead it next. I built the project using HTML, CSS, and JavaScript, with browser-based storage to preserve user progress. I integrated WebMCP through the document.modelContext API, allowing AI agents to understand and interact with the application through structured tools. The project includes 11 WebMCP tools that can search for nonprofits, retrieve organization details, maintain a shortlist, compare opportunities, prepare and review applications, assess handoff readiness, update transition tasks, add shared notes, and summarize transition progress. My goal was not simply to let an agent click buttons. I wanted the agent to become a useful collaborator. An AI agent can organize information, identify gaps, prepare drafts, and recommend next steps, while people remain responsible for consequential decisions. Submitting an application and approving a final leadership handoff are intentionally human-only actions. This makes the agent helpful without allowing it to make irreversible decisions on someone’s behalf. One of the greatest challenges was determining how much authority an agent should have. Leadership succession affects real organizations and the communities they serve, so completely automating the process would be irresponsible. I addressed this challenge by separating assistance from approval. Agents can help people understand their options and prepare their work, but final decisions stay with the founder and successor. Another challenge was designing the application for two users with very different needs. Successors need a simple way to discover and evaluate opportunities, while founders need tools for documentation, readiness assessment, and transition planning. I created role-specific experiences for each person while connecting them through a shared transition workspace. I also added visible activity tracking so users can understand which actions were completed by a human and which were assisted by an agent. Building Nonprofit Pass taught me that an agent-native application requires much more than exposing existing interface controls to AI. It requires carefully deciding what context agents receive, which actions they may perform, how their contributions are communicated, and where human judgment must remain mandatory. I also learned that succession is not a single moment when one leader replaces another. It is an ongoing process involving trust, documentation, training, shared memory, and accountability. In the future, I hope to connect Nonprofit Pass with real nonprofit opportunities and verified organizational accounts. The platform could eventually include document integrations, mentor verification, founder-successor introductions, transition health monitoring, and long-term impact tracking. My goal is to ensure that a student’s graduation does not have to mean the end of an organization. Young founders create projects that address meaningful needs, and those projects deserve the opportunity to continue. By helping founders, successors, and AI agents work together, Nonprofit Pass creates a path for a nonprofit’s mission and impact to outlive its original founder.
