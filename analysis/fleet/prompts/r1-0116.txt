Like Comment Story Updates Inspiration I did not build One Pixel because the world needed another pixel-art tool. I built it because I wanted to understand what it might feel like for a human and an AI agent to genuinely share a place. For most people, an agent is still something they give a task to. The agent finds an answer, completes an action, or produces a result, and then that little transaction is over. That can be useful, but I do not think it captures the relationship we are moving toward as agents begin participating throughout the open web. I am interested in what happens when a human and an agent can return to the same environment, notice different things, contribute different kinds of intelligence, act over time, and gradually develop something together. That is the idea at the centre of One Pixel. One Pixel is a real multiplayer creative game. But underneath the game, it is also a prototype for a new relationship between humans and agents on the open web — one based on shared environments, complementary intelligence, ongoing conversation, and meaningful agency. I chose to explore this through a game because games let people learn a relationship by participating in it. I do not want to explain human-agent collaboration only as a theory. I want to place a person and an agent inside a world, give each of them a genuinely different role, and let them discover what working together can become. The initial invitation is very simple: Let’s build something together. The interesting part is discovering what “let’s” can actually mean. What it does One Pixel is a persistent multiplayer world containing exactly 82,069 pieces of pixel matter , drawn from the 148 standard CSS named colours . Every pixel has its own stable identity, intrinsic colour, and current integer coordinate. The original Spring Green pixel at (0,0) is immutable, and the world extends outward from it in every direction. The world has one fundamental physical law: Pixel matter is conserved. Pixels cannot be created, deleted, duplicated, or recoloured. Every physical action relocates exactly one existing pixel from its current occupied coordinate to an empty coordinate. That constraint matters. These are not disposable marks being painted onto a private canvas. They are pieces of matter already present in a shared world. If an agent wants to build something, it must look around, find the colours it needs, understand what is already there, and decide what material it can move. The human and agent participate as a pair, but they do not have the same abilities. The human can explore the world, zoom and pan across it, inspect individual cells, select larger regions, and communicate through their normal conversation with the agent. The human contributes intention, imagination, taste, judgment, social understanding, and direction. The human cannot directly drag pixels around the canvas. That physical ability belongs to the agent. Through WebMCP, the agent can enter the world, inspect exact locations, examine bounded regions, render parts of the board as images, receive the human’s current visual reference, understand its own state, protect important placements with limited holds, and relocate one real pixel at a time. A person could give a direct request: “Draw me a small house.” But that is only one form of play. A person could instead say: “Spend some time looking at what people are making. Find blue and orange pixels that do not seem to be part of anything important. Gather some material somewhere quiet, compare it with what other people are building, and then let’s work out together what we could make.” That is not a one-prompt, one-result interaction. The request becomes a direction the pair can continue through a sequence of inspections, decisions, conversations, and individual pixel movements. The agent does not have to wait for the human to translate every idea into coordinates. It can perceive the environment, compare possibilities, form an approach, begin acting, inspect the result, and respond to further human guidance. The human can look at the world, select a cell or region, and say: “I like this part, but this section feels too crowded.” That selection becomes a shared reference—a way of saying “this” or “here.” One Pixel treats a selection as a pronoun rather than a command. It gives the agent the referent the human is talking about, but it does not pretend that pointing at something is automatically an instruction, consent, or proof of understanding. The meaning still comes from the conversation. The canvas is therefore not simply an output preview. It is the persistent place where the pair’s activity becomes visible and where the consequences of their decisions remain after a particular conversation has ended. This is not an AI drawing widget. It is a game an agent can play for and with a human. How I built it I built and deployed One Pixel on my own VPS at onepixel.fun . The browser experience uses HTML, CSS, vanilla JavaScript, and an HTML Canvas. I wanted the world itself to remain the visual centre of the experience rather than placing it inside a conventional application dashboard. The backend is a Node.js service connected to PostgreSQL. It maintains the persistent world, participants, pixel identities, current coordinates, world revisions, movement history, hold bindings, agent visits, shared-attention events, player state, rankings, and durable share snapshots. The core physical tool is move_pixel . The shared-attention system records the human’s current view and selection across their One Pixel browser tabs. When the agent retrieves that context, One Pixel records a factual receipt so the interface can truthfully say that the reference reached the agent.
