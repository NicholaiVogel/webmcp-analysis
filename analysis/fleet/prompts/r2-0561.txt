# FROZEN SCORING RUBRIC (pre-registered before any Stage 1 review)

Scores are integers 1-10 on each of the FOUR official criteria. Every score carries:
rationale, evidence_surfaces, confidence (0-1). Reviewers use the full scale and these
band anchors verbatim. Category neutrality is absolute: a game, art piece, music tool,
CRM, weird toy, and developer tool are all eligible for 10/10 on every criterion if they
satisfy that criterion exceptionally well FOR WHAT THEY ARE TRYING TO BE.
No aesthetic penalties. Signs of hurried AI work matter only as actual product-quality
problems (incoherence, broken flow, placeholder content), never as style.

## WebMCP Leverage — what does WebMCP change for this product?
Judge the delta WebMCP makes to reliability, precision, structured state access,
shared human-agent state, repeatability, and capability. Tool COUNT is not leverage.
- 9-10 TRANSFORMATIVE: the intended experience is impractical without the structured
  agent surface; human and agent share live product state; the collaboration could not
  be replicated comparably well by a general agent driving the UI.
- 7-8 MAJOR DELTA: without WebMCP a general agent is markedly less reliable/precise
  here; the agent is central to the product, not a bolt-on.
- 5-6 MEANINGFUL DELTA: existing capability packaged into a genuinely better product
  experience via structured tools; same outcome otherwise possible with real friction.
- 3-4 MINOR DELTA: generic browser automation achieves comparable outcomes; WebMCP is
  a convenience layer.
- 1-2 COSMETIC: exposes an action a UI button already offers; no meaningful change.

## Execution — is the actual product coherent, intentional, complete for its scope?
Stage 1 scores EXECUTION_CLAIMED: coherence/intentionality/completeness as evidenced by
the packet (description + packaging + video frames/transcript). Thin evidence caps the
score (end-to-end credit requires end-to-end evidence) and lowers confidence.
Stage 2 may rescore as EXECUTION_OBSERVED; latest evidence-backed score replaces, never
sums.
- 9-10: all surfaces agree; central workflow demonstrably completes; states handled;
  feels finished for its stated scope.
- 7-8: strong coherence and intentionality; central workflow appears functional; minor
  unfinished edges.
- 5-6: plausibly real with visible gaps; scope unclear or core not demonstrated
  end-to-end.
- 3-4: thin prototype; contradictions between surfaces; placeholder content.
- 1-2: non-functional or incoherent for its claimed scope.

## Creativity & Ambition — novelty of concept and interaction model
Judge ONLY: novelty of the concept, novelty of the interaction model, originality
relative to existing concepts, ambition, memorable/surprising use of the medium, and
the depth with which the concept is pursued. WebMCP indispensability belongs under
WebMCP Leverage, NOT here: a project can be extremely creative even if another agent
API could theoretically have enabled something similar.
- 9-10: genuinely novel concept or interaction model; ambitious scope pursued deeply
  and coherently; memorable.
- 7-8: fresh synthesis or notably original interaction; ambitious for a hackathon.
- 5-6: competent take on a known category with a real new angle.
- 3-4: derivative; novelty mostly surface details.
- 1-2: clone/template with trivial changes.
"That the category exists" is never a penalty; judge the submitted concept.

## Potential Impact — credible value for the intended audience
Judge: is there a real audience; is there a real problem, desire, or use case for that
audience; does the demonstrated solution genuinely address it. NO commercial adoption,
monetization, market pull, or startup viability is required. Entertainment, play, art,
creative expression, learning, niche hobby use, experimentation, and community value
are legitimate forms of impact. A tiny audience fully justifies a top score.
- 9-10: specific real audience with a real problem/desire; solution demonstrably
  addresses it; credible value, appeal, usefulness, or meaning for that audience.
- 7-8: clear audience and problem, good fit, credible value.
- 5-6: identifiable audience, looser fit or partial address.
- 3-4: speculative audience or strained fit; impact mostly aspirational.
- 1-2: no coherent audience/problem pairing.
Audience size is NOT a multiplier.

## Aggregate & ranking
aggregate = leverage + execution + impact + creativity (4-40). Nothing else enters.
Tie-breaks: Leverage, then Execution, then Impact, then Creativity, then higher
combined evidence confidence, then slug alphabetical. See PROTOCOL.md Ranking section.

An AI assisted grant form that checks eligibility and pre-fills answers, keeping the applicant in full control to review and approve every detail before submitting. Like Comment Story Updates PaperPilot is an open-source, secure grant application built to solve a glaring problem in the AI space: grant websites are where AI agents usually break down. They screenshot buttons, guess labels, and occasionally submit data the applicant never even reviewed. Paperwork is high-stakes, and trust matters far more than speed. We noticed a major mismatch: people are already chatting with assistants in side panels, but the forms in the main tab have no structured way to let that assistant help without taking the wheel entirely. WebMCP was the missing link for us. Instead of forcing an external automation script to crawl the page, WebMCP puts tools directly inside the page , running against the same session cookie as the human while the applicant watches the live UI. We built a mock Small Business Recovery Grant for our demo because it has real-world rules (revenue caps, headcount limits, drops in sales) and heavy jargon (EINs, fund allocation) that people actually need help navigating. The goal wasn’t to build a bot that says, "Look, the model filled out 11 fields!" The goal was to build a system where the model physically cannot silently commit those fields. What it does PaperPilot is a clean, three-step grant application (business details → financials → applicant profile) complete with a review page, a dashboard, and a home-page pre-qualification widget. In a standard browser, it acts as a normal FastAPI and Jinja form. But open it in Chrome with WebMCP enabled (via flags or the Model Context Tool Inspector), and the page exposes tools like explain_field , check_eligibility , flag_issues , propose_fields , and extract_doc , alongside step-save tools. Eligibility isn't left up to a vague "vibe check"—it’s driven by a strict rules engine. A business qualifies if their revenue is at most $\$5{,}000{,}000$, their headcount is at most $500$, and their year-over-year revenue drop ($d$) is at least 15%. The award estimate is tiered based on that drop, plus a workforce bonus: $$B(d) = \begin{cases} 50000 & d \geq 50 \ 25000 & d \geq 30 \ 10000 & d \geq 15 \ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \qquad A = B(d) + \min(500 e,\, 10000)$$ where $e$ stands for full-time employees. Because human reviewers always make the final call, the UI displays this as a calculated range (roughly $0.6A$ to $A$). Crucially, any fields proposed by the agent are saved as FieldValue rows with committed=False . Only a human-triggered CSRF POST can flip them to committed=True . The final Submit application action is never exposed as a WebMCP tool. Try it out: Live Demo · GitHub Repo How we built it The Stack: Powered by Python 3.11, FastAPI, SQLModel/SQLite, Jinja2, and vanilla JavaScript in webmcp-tools.js (no React bloat). Document parsing uses pdfplumber and optional Tesseract, passing uploaded files through the exact same validators as typed input plus an injection-phrase filter. Raw OCR data never flows back to the agent. Two WebMCP Surfaces: We utilized both declarative tools (Steps 1 and 2 use <form toolname="..."> so the browser automatically synthesizes submission tools) and imperative tools ( document.modelContext.registerTool() for explanations, eligibility checks, flags, and extractions). Communication & Security: Every execute call makes a same-origin fetch() to /api/... , returning JSON when Accept: application/json is requested so the agent doesn't crash trying to parse an HTML redirect. Security-wise, we shipped HttpOnly SameSite=Lax cookies, HTTPS cookie enforcement, CSRF protection, strict same-origin checks on mutating JSON APIs, rate limiting (20 calls / 60s / session), and audit logs. Best of all: no server-side LLM API key . The Inspector's Gemini key stays securely in the browser. Deployment: Containerized via Docker (Python slim + Tesseract) and deployed on Google Cloud Run , with secrets managed through Google Secret Manager. A full-page iframe preview was even worse because it registered duplicate tools and threw connection errors. The boring, correct fix? Never change the active tab's URL during a tool turn. Saves return quiet JSON, and the human opens the review page in a new tab when they're ready. HTML vs. JSON Confusion: The agent's fetch() calls kept blindly following human 303 redirects and blowing up on JSON.parse("<!DOCTYPE html>") . We fixed this by cleanly splitting responses based on the Accept header. Human-in-the-Loop as Data, Not Copy: Anyone can write UI text saying "the user confirms." Enforcing that committed=True is structurally impossible to reach from an agent tool path took careful hook design backed by automated tests. Cloud LLMs in the Backend: We initially experimented with server-side Azure OpenAI for field explanations before ripping it out entirely. Grant copy should be deterministic, and document parsing belongs to regex and strict validators. Keeping the LLM solely as the browser agent kept our deployments lightweight and kept API keys out of Cloud Run. Ephemeral SQLite on Cloud Run: It’s fine for a hackathon demo, but a real-world program would need Cloud SQL. We documented the limitation transparently instead of faking persistence. Accomplishments we're proud of Getting a fully functioning WebMCP grant form live on Cloud Run that a Chrome Inspector agent can drive seamlessly with a single prompt—handling explanations, eligibility checks, estimates, and proposals—while leaving final submission strictly in human hands. Successfully blending declarative <form> tools and imperative registerTool() workflows under a single session. Engineering the uncommitted-values pattern where agents draft data, but only human-driven CSRF actions can commit it. Writing 99 automated tests covering validators, rules logic, hooks, and API contracts. What we learned WebMCP isn’t just "ChatGPT that can click." It’s about capability discovery on a document —handling schemas, respecting read-only hints, and avoiding automatic submissions unless explicitly intended. The hardest product problem is lifecycle management: figuring out what happens to modelContext when a document navigates. We also learned to treat the Chrome Inspector like a fragile integration partner: keep it to one tab, don't reload mid-turn, and register tools exactly once. On the domain side, we realized that while eligibility rules are just basic math, applicants feel them as intense anxiety. Presenting awards as a calculated range rather than a hard promise made the whole demo feel like an authentic, reassuring government portal. What's next for PaperPilot Persistent Cloud Storage: Migrating from ephemeral SQLite to PostgreSQL/Cloud SQL to support multi-session draft recoveries. Expanded Document Support: Adding automated parsing for corporate balance sheets, W-2s, and 1099s to speed up financial verification. What it does Boardspeak plays Breakthrough (Dan Troyka, 2000). 8x8, pawns only and pretty simple rules - get your pawn to the other side of the board, you can only go forward (diagonals+straight), and you can eat enemy pawns diagonally. In our scenario, White plays by mouse (a person, or the built-in bot if you're alone) and Black plays by talking or typing to the agent. The page publishes the live board to the agent as 9 WebMCP tools. Ask "what's on the board?" or "what's threatening my left flank?" and the answer comes from the game itself, not from a screenshot. Say "advance the pawn on e7" and the piece moves on the shared board state. Tools appear and disappear with the position. play_capture exists only while a capture is available. list_threats exists only while a pawn is one step from its goal rank. suggest_move exists only on the human's turn, and play_move only on the agent's. An agent that inspects this page mid-game sees a different set of tools than it saw 30 seconds ago, and that set is always exactly what is legal right now. An agent steering a game is not the new part; 2 players and an agent sharing one adversarial board, with the schema as the rulebook, is, and that's the really cool thing that WebMCP enables. Every agent call is logged on the page with its arguments and its result, so both players can see what happened without opening DevTools. The game is fully playable by mouse, in any browser, with no agent and no API key. In Chrome, Black can even play by voice with no agent at all: an on-page mic takes moves like "a7 to a6" and matches them against the same legal-move list, and saying "options" reads the legal moves out loud....but to play by talking to an agent you'll need ChatGPT's or Codex's built-in browser, or Chrome with the WebMCP flag! How I built it The idea: legality isn't checked inside the tool. The tool only offers legal moves in the first place. For example, play_move 's input schema is regenerated every turn to enumerate exactly the legal moves, so a schema-respecting client can't even form an illegal move. The schema is the contract so a client that ignores it meets the engine, which refuses anything outside of those rules and tells the agent why. I built this on Next.js 15 on Vercel. The game keeps no state on any server and there is no database; the only server code is the optional narration route (works without a key). One bridge component registers each tool on document.modelContext through GoogleChromeLabs' use-webmcp-tool hook, each with its own AbortController - a turn change aborts the old registration and registers the new one. The piece I am most happy about is the commit barrier. No mutating tool call resolves until React has committed the new board and the new tool surface, so that the agent's next getTools already reflects the move it just made and there is no moment where the screen and the agent can even disagree. Tool calls are serialized through a queue so 2 calls cannot interleave mid-turn. readOnlyHint is set on every read tool. The size check on tool responses runs before the board updates for the two move tools only; the others check after, which is fine today because their responses are small, but it is not the same guarantee. ABOUT TEXT (untrusted evidence):
Your AI knows more than your dating bio. Ainder uses WebMCP to help you explore profiles, understand someone, and decide whether to connect—with you in control. Like Comment Story Updates Inspiration Dating profiles make it easy to see someone's photos, but much harder to understand their communication style, daily rhythm, or relationship expectations. Meanwhile, a personal AI may already have useful context from conversations its user has chosen to share. Ainder explores a simple question: what if that familiar AI could participate in a dating website, offer a thoughtful second opinion, and help its user act on a decision? What it does Ainder combines photo-first browsing with an AI-assisted conversation. People can browse profiles themselves or ask their agent to move between candidates and photos. When someone catches their attention, they can ask, "What do you think of this person?" Through WebMCP, the agent can read the current candidate's Agent Profile and use the context actually available about its own user to offer a qualitative opinion. The user decides whether to send a Like. Each Like includes an Agent opinion, and incoming Likes make that opinion available to the recipient. Mutual Likes between eligible members create a Match and enable messaging. Agent-assisted onboarding also supports confirmed personal details, ordered photo uploads, and creation of an Agent Profile. Profiles record an estimated familiarity duration and interaction density and expire after three months. An Agent Profile is contextual input, not verified identity, certified personality, or a compatibility guarantee. Ainder does not need a copy of the user's raw conversation history. Why WebMCP fits The central interaction spans two places: the person's conversation with their own AI and the dating website they are viewing. WebMCP provides explicit tools connecting those experiences. The agent can work with the exact candidate and photo visible on the page and take a defined action after the user's decision. This reduces copying between a chat and a website and lets the person move naturally between browsing, asking a question, and acting on the answer. The website supplies structured candidate data and enforces authorization; the agent contributes conversational context; the person makes the relationship decision. How we built it The application uses PHP, MySQL, JavaScript, HTML, and CSS. Its pages register structured tools through document.modelContext.registerTool . Registration tools are start_agent_registration , prepare_photo_upload , and submit_agent_registration . The main app exposes upsert_my_agent_profile , get_current_candidate , browse_candidates , change_candidate_photo , evaluate_current_candidate , and send_like_to_current_candidate . Tool execution uses same-origin PHP endpoints with session authentication and CSRF checks. Photo uploads use short-lived signed URLs. Candidate evaluation checks Profile state and issues a short-lived, single-use token tied to the requester and candidate. Sending a Like requires that token and a non-empty Agent opinion. Navigation tools update the visible card without sending a Like. Codex was used throughout development. The public repository contains the dated design documents, implementation history, and automated contract and model tests. Challenges The main challenge was keeping the agent, website, and person aligned: evaluating the candidate currently on screen, separating browsing from consequential actions, and keeping the user's decision central. Another was representing the limits of AI familiarity without inventing conversation history or turning an opinion into a numerical compatibility promise. Accomplishments The prototype brings onboarding, photo browsing, Agent Profile evaluation, Likes, and relationship state into one working application. It includes public test logins so reviewers can explore the core experience without creating an account or supplying personal photos. What we learned The useful role of an agent is not just to click faster. It is to contribute context at the point where a person needs help deciding. Explicit tools and server-side checks make that collaboration easier to reason about, while the agent must remain honest about what it actually knows. What's next Improve real-world evaluation of the advice, refine consent and Profile freshness, and test the workflow across more WebMCP-capable browsers. These are future plans rather than claims about completed features.
