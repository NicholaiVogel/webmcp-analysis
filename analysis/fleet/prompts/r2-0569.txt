=== RUBRIC (frozen) ===
# FROZEN SCORING RUBRIC (pre-registered before any Stage 1 review)

Scores are integers 1-10 on each of the FOUR official criteria. Every score carries:
rationale, evidence_surfaces, confidence (0-1). Reviewers use the full scale and these
band anchors verbatim. Category neutrality is absolute: a game, art piece, music tool,
CRM, weird toy, and developer tool are all eligible for 10/10 on every criterion if they
satisfy that criterion exceptionally well FOR WHAT THEY ARE TRYING TO BE.
No aesthetic penalties. Signs of hurried AI work matter only as actual product-quality
problems (incoherence, broken flow, placeholder content), never as style.

## WebMCP Leverage — what does WebMCP change for this product?
Judge the delta WebMCP makes to reliability, precision, structured state access,
shared human-agent state, repeatability, and capability. Tool COUNT is not leverage.
- 9-10 TRANSFORMATIVE: the intended experience is impractical without the structured
  agent surface; human and agent share live product state; the collaboration could not
  be replicated comparably well by a general agent driving the UI.
- 7-8 MAJOR DELTA: without WebMCP a general agent is markedly less reliable/precise
  here; the agent is central to the product, not a bolt-on.
- 5-6 MEANINGFUL DELTA: existing capability packaged into a genuinely better product
  experience via structured tools; same outcome otherwise possible with real friction.
- 3-4 MINOR DELTA: generic browser automation achieves comparable outcomes; WebMCP is
  a convenience layer.
- 1-2 COSMETIC: exposes an action a UI button already offers; no meaningful change.

## Execution — is the actual product coherent, intentional, complete for its scope?
Stage 1 scores EXECUTION_CLAIMED: coherence/intentionality/completeness as evidenced by
the packet (description + packaging + video frames/transcript). Thin evidence caps the
score (end-to-end credit requires end-to-end evidence) and lowers confidence.
Stage 2 may rescore as EXECUTION_OBSERVED; latest evidence-backed score replaces, never
sums.
- 9-10: all surfaces agree; central workflow demonstrably completes; states handled;
  feels finished for its stated scope.
- 7-8: strong coherence and intentionality; central workflow appears functional; minor
  unfinished edges.
- 5-6: plausibly real with visible gaps; scope unclear or core not demonstrated
  end-to-end.
- 3-4: thin prototype; contradictions between surfaces; placeholder content.
- 1-2: non-functional or incoherent for its claimed scope.

## Creativity & Ambition — novelty of concept and interaction model
Judge ONLY: novelty of the concept, novelty of the interaction model, originality
relative to existing concepts, ambition, memorable/surprising use of the medium, and
the depth with which the concept is pursued. WebMCP indispensability belongs under
WebMCP Leverage, NOT here: a project can be extremely creative even if another agent
API could theoretically have enabled something similar.
- 9-10: genuinely novel concept or interaction model; ambitious scope pursued deeply
  and coherently; memorable.
- 7-8: fresh synthesis or notably original interaction; ambitious for a hackathon.
- 5-6: competent take on a known category with a real new angle.
- 3-4: derivative; novelty mostly surface details.
- 1-2: clone/template with trivial changes.
"That the category exists" is never a penalty; judge the submitted concept.

## Potential Impact — credible value for the intended audience
Judge: is there a real audience; is there a real problem, desire, or use case for that
audience; does the demonstrated solution genuinely address it. NO commercial adoption,
monetization, market pull, or startup viability is required. Entertainment, play, art,
creative expression, learning, niche hobby use, experimentation, and community value
are legitimate forms of impact. A tiny audience fully justifies a top score.
- 9-10: specific real audience with a real problem/desire; solution demonstrably
  addresses it; credible value, appeal, usefulness, or meaning for that audience.
- 7-8: clear audience and problem, good fit, credible value.
- 5-6: identifiable audience, looser fit or partial address.
- 3-4: speculative audience or strained fit; impact mostly aspirational.
- 1-2: no coherent audience/problem pairing.
Audience size is NOT a multiplier.

## Aggregate & ranking
aggregate = leverage + execution + impact + creativity (4-40). Nothing else enters.
Tie-breaks: Leverage, then Execution, then Impact, then Creativity, then higher
combined evidence confidence, then slug alphabetical. See PROTOCOL.md Ranking section.

Inspiration LifeLinks began with a simple idea I heard about: a service that provided QR labels people could attach to belongings so they could locate things later or associate useful information—such as instructions or tutorial videos—with a physical object. That was useful, but it led me to a much bigger question: what if those records were not merely pages for people to open, but part of a persistent context layer that an AI agent could understand and help maintain? General-purpose AI already knows an extraordinary amount about the world. What it does not naturally know is your world : what you own, where you put it, what you prefer, what worked last time, what you are planning, and what has changed. Products such as ChatGPT Projects demonstrate the value of retaining context, but that context can still be fairly open-ended. I wanted something more structured without making it rigid: organized enough for an agent to retrieve an exact item, relationship, location, event, or routine, but dynamic and personal enough to evolve alongside somebody’s real life. Several personal use cases shaped the product. My brother is an avid camper, skier, and cyclist in Denver. He and his wife have a small basement filled with carefully packed storage totes, but they still struggle to remember which tote contains a particular piece of equipment. That inspired the physical hierarchy and QR features. An AI should not merely know that they own a tent—it should be able to tell them that the tent is inside a particular tote in a particular part of the basement. Not every object needs its own QR code; one label on the nearest container can provide the physical bridge to everything recorded inside it. I also own a 3D printer and have many rolls of filament in different colors and materials. I used LifeLinks to create an actual filament library so an agent can help me compare a future project with the supplies and owner-reported quantities I already have. When I first explained LifeLinks to my wife, her immediate reaction was that she could use it for her makeup and beauty collection, as well as nonphysical information such as routines. That made me smile because I had already imagined the same thing. Product names, brands, shades, preferences, and experiences can become useful context for future conversations without requiring a QR code at all. The larger goal is to help people—including people who rarely use AI agents—experience how useful AI can become when it has an organized, properly labeled, persistent context layer around the things they are actually doing. What it does LifeLinks is a personal context system that connects everyday information with authorized AI agents. My Life Links Physical places and objects can be represented as nested Life Links. A storage wall can contain totes, a tote can contain camping equipment, and an individual item can carry its own notes, attachments, history, and recorded location. Collections Collections organize the same records by purpose rather than location. Camping gear can be divided into sections such as Shelter, Sleep Systems, Camp Kitchen, and Cycling Kit without changing where any of those items are physically stored. This distinction is important: “Where is it?” and “What is it for?” are different questions. Optional QR labels Users can generate and print QR codes for places, containers, or individual records. Scanning one opens the corresponding LifeLinks record. QR codes are a useful bridge between the physical and digital worlds, but they are not required. Many LifeLinks use cases—including routines, project information, calendars, and personal preferences—do not need QR codes at all. Search and attachments Search covers Life Links, Collections, current Routines, recorded Routine history, authorized Calendar events, and supported attachment text. Users can also keep private notes, photos, and documents with the records they describe, making LifeLinks useful for hobbies and project work rather than just inventory. Routines Routines can contain flexible activities or ordered steps, instructions, planned targets, revisions, and completion history. This makes it possible to plan what should happen while preserving what actually happened. Calendar The Calendar combines native LifeLinks events with selected Google Calendar and Microsoft Outlook calendars. Calendar visibility and agent permissions remain under the user’s control. Together, Routines and Calendar allow an agent to understand not only what someone owns, but also what they intend to do, when they plan to do it, and what progress they have recorded. Connected agents LifeLinks supports two distinct agent experiences: Browser WebMCP exposes authorized tools from an eligible, signed-in LifeLinks page. Remote MCP allows compatible agents to use authorized LifeLinks tools after account linking, even when the website is closed. Connected agents can perform supported searches, create and edit information, organize Collections, work with Routines and Calendars, move records, and prepare supported deletions within the user’s permissions and confirmation boundaries. How we built it LifeLinks existed before this challenge as a narrower human-facing QR and record application. During the WebMCP Challenge, I substantially expanded it into a structured human-and-agent workspace with recursive Life Links, purpose-based Collections, Routines, Calendar integration, broader search, richer attachments, browser WebMCP tools, and a separately authorized remote MCP server. The responsive web application is built with React, Vite, and TypeScript. A Node.js and Express API manages the application workflows, while PostgreSQL provides durable storage. The system also uses OpenAPI, OAuth 2.0, the Google Calendar API, Microsoft Graph, QR generation and scanning, and a Docker-based Railway deployment. The human interface and connected-agent tools reuse the same underlying identities, permissions, queries, and commands. An agent does not write into a separate demonstration database or generate a temporary chat summary. An authorized change is a real application change that appears in LifeLinks and remains available later. The browser WebMCP and remote MCP connections are deliberately separate. Browser WebMCP belongs to the active webpage, while remote MCP uses an OAuth-linked connection designed to work without depending on an open page or browser cookies.
