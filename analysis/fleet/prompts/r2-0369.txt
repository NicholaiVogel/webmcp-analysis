# FROZEN SCORING RUBRIC (pre-registered before any Stage 1 review)

Scores are integers 1-10 on each of the FOUR official criteria. Every score carries:
rationale, evidence_surfaces, confidence (0-1). Reviewers use the full scale and these
band anchors verbatim. Category neutrality is absolute: a game, art piece, music tool,
CRM, weird toy, and developer tool are all eligible for 10/10 on every criterion if they
satisfy that criterion exceptionally well FOR WHAT THEY ARE TRYING TO BE.
No aesthetic penalties. Signs of hurried AI work matter only as actual product-quality
problems (incoherence, broken flow, placeholder content), never as style.

## WebMCP Leverage — what does WebMCP change for this product?
Judge the delta WebMCP makes to reliability, precision, structured state access,
shared human-agent state, repeatability, and capability. Tool COUNT is not leverage.
- 9-10 TRANSFORMATIVE: the intended experience is impractical without the structured
  agent surface; human and agent share live product state; the collaboration could not
  be replicated comparably well by a general agent driving the UI.
- 7-8 MAJOR DELTA: without WebMCP a general agent is markedly less reliable/precise
  here; the agent is central to the product, not a bolt-on.
- 5-6 MEANINGFUL DELTA: existing capability packaged into a genuinely better product
  experience via structured tools; same outcome otherwise possible with real friction.
- 3-4 MINOR DELTA: generic browser automation achieves comparable outcomes; WebMCP is
  a convenience layer.
- 1-2 COSMETIC: exposes an action a UI button already offers; no meaningful change.

## Execution — is the actual product coherent, intentional, complete for its scope?
Stage 1 scores EXECUTION_CLAIMED: coherence/intentionality/completeness as evidenced by
the packet (description + packaging + video frames/transcript). Thin evidence caps the
score (end-to-end credit requires end-to-end evidence) and lowers confidence.
Stage 2 may rescore as EXECUTION_OBSERVED; latest evidence-backed score replaces, never
sums.
- 9-10: all surfaces agree; central workflow demonstrably completes; states handled;
  feels finished for its stated scope.
- 7-8: strong coherence and intentionality; central workflow appears functional; minor
  unfinished edges.
- 5-6: plausibly real with visible gaps; scope unclear or core not demonstrated
  end-to-end.
- 3-4: thin prototype; contradictions between surfaces; placeholder content.
- 1-2: non-functional or incoherent for its claimed scope.

## Creativity & Ambition — novelty of concept and interaction model
Judge ONLY: novelty of the concept, novelty of the interaction model, originality
relative to existing concepts, ambition, memorable/surprising use of the medium, and
the depth with which the concept is pursued. WebMCP indispensability belongs under
WebMCP Leverage, NOT here: a project can be extremely creative even if another agent
API could theoretically have enabled something similar.
- 9-10: genuinely novel concept or interaction model; ambitious scope pursued deeply
  and coherently; memorable.
- 7-8: fresh synthesis or notably original interaction; ambitious for a hackathon.
- 5-6: competent take on a known category with a real new angle.
- 3-4: derivative; novelty mostly surface details.
- 1-2: clone/template with trivial changes.
"That the category exists" is never a penalty; judge the submitted concept.

## Potential Impact — credible value for the intended audience
Judge: is there a real audience; is there a real problem, desire, or use case for that
audience; does the demonstrated solution genuinely address it. NO commercial adoption,
monetization, market pull, or startup viability is required. Entertainment, play, art,
creative expression, learning, niche hobby use, experimentation, and community value
are legitimate forms of impact. A tiny audience fully justifies a top score.
- 9-10: specific real audience with a real problem/desire; solution demonstrably
  addresses it; credible value, appeal, usefulness, or meaning for that audience.
- 7-8: clear audience and problem, good fit, credible value.
- 5-6: identifiable audience, looser fit or partial address.
- 3-4: speculative audience or strained fit; impact mostly aspirational.
- 1-2: no coherent audience/problem pairing.
Audience size is NOT a multiplier.

## Aggregate & ranking
aggregate = leverage + execution + impact + creativity (4-40). Nothing else enters.
Tie-breaks: Leverage, then Execution, then Impact, then Creativity, then higher
combined evidence confidence, then slug alphabetical. See PROTOCOL.md Ranking section.

Less prep. Clearer follow-up. With WebMCP, agents draft home exercise plans and explain browser-local motion results—while therapists keep control of every prescription. Like Comment Story Updates Inspiration Home exercise care continues long after a patient leaves the clinic. Yet finding suitable exercise materials, assembling a program, explaining it, and understanding what happened between visits all take time. Our starting point was Crosspoint's existing exercise library and home-exercise product concepts. We saw an opportunity to connect two recurring tasks: preparing a clear program before the patient leaves, and making sense of their practice afterward. CoachPoint asks a practical question: What if an agent could handle more of the preparation and follow-up work, while the therapist kept every prescription decision? What it does CoachPoint connects a therapist, a patient, and a browser agent through one home-exercise workflow. The therapist creates the plan. They select a client and describe their goals, available time, equipment, and proposed exercises in plain language. Through WebMCP, the agent uses the curated movement library and its safety notes to assemble a visible, editable draft. The therapist adjusts dosage and order, then confirms the prescription in the interface. There is no tool that lets the agent confirm it. The patient practices and checks in. They open the confirmed program and choose when to start the camera. For the supported half-squat exercise, browser-local pose processing counts repetitions and provides immediate visual feedback, with optional audio cues. After the set, the patient reports perceived effort and pain. The agent can then explain the saved result and offer an optional focus for the next set. The patient accepts or dismisses it; the prescribed exercise and dosage remain unchanged. The therapist reviews the follow-up. Back in the client workspace, the therapist can see recorded completion, repetitions, effort, pain, and camera-derived observations, then ask for a concise pre-visit summary grounded in those same records. Why WebMCP fits this problem The useful context is already inside the browser: the current client workspace, the editable draft, and movement results derived on the patient's device. WebMCP gives the agent a structured way to work with that context through tools exposed by the current page. It can create something the therapist can immediately inspect, or retrieve a saved result without receiving camera footage. A server-side integration would need an additional path to access our unsynchronized browser-local state. WebMCP lets us expose selected data and validated actions where that state already lives. The browser is both the shared workspace and the source of the movement observations. How we built it We built the application with Next.js, React, TypeScript, and Tailwind CSS, and deployed it on Vercel. MediaPipe pose estimation runs locally with a self-hosted model and WebAssembly runtime. The human interface and WebMCP tools reuse the same domain operations for catalog search, case validation, dosage limits, time estimation, and draft creation. This keeps agent actions subject to the same checks as manual actions. Tools register through document.modelContext.registerTool() and belong to the current route. An AbortController retires them when that route is left. Confirmed prescriptions are preserved as versioned snapshots, and patient results are validated before they become available for review. Representative tools include draft_program , review_completed_set , stage_next_set_focus , and get_adherence_summary . The pre-existing library and design foundation are distinguished from the new prescription workflow, WebMCP integration, local motion processing, and feedback loop in our challenge-work documentation . Challenges we ran into Finding the right moment for the agent. We initially explored live monitoring and frequent spoken feedback. The interaction felt poorly paced. We separated immediate browser feedback from post-set reasoning: counting and short cues happen locally, while the agent interprets a saved result when the user asks. Making assistance feel useful, not procedural. Early drafting involved too many separate tool calls and overly detailed prompts. We consolidated the common drafting path into one operation for an explicitly requested new draft. Existing drafts remain protected from silent replacement. Keeping state and responsibility clear. Route changes, cancelled calls, interrupted camera sessions, and repeated actions could otherwise leave the interface and agent describing different situations. We added lifecycle cleanup, versioned persistence, conflict checks for coaching suggestions, and explicit human controls. We also kept detected repetitions separate from self-reported effort: eight repetitions and an effort rating of eight are different facts. Accomplishments we're proud of We built and rehearsed the complete loop: an agent-generated draft, a therapist's change from ten to eight squat repetitions, a completed camera-tracked set, an explicit effort-and-pain check-in, an accepted coaching focus, and a therapist-facing summary. Automated tests cover domain rules, tool contracts, cancellation, persistence, and motion counting. Browser testing checks that tool results and the visible interface agree. The manual workflow remains usable without an agent. What we learned The most useful unit of agent work was a reviewable draft or a completed-set explanation, not a stream of individual clicks or camera frames. We also learned that boundaries belong in the product itself. Therapist confirmation, patient acceptance, limited data exposure, and clear descriptions of camera estimates matter more than a disclaimer added at the end. What's next for CoachPoint The current build is a synthetic demonstration with three demo clients and fifteen catalog exercises. Camera-based counting currently supports the half squat; other exercises use camera-independent workflows. Data is stored in the current browser, so patient links are not yet a cross-device sharing system. The recorded demo uses OBS replay for repeatability. Camera observations are two-dimensional estimates, not clinical conclusions, and the product has not been clinically validated. Next, we want to validate additional exercise-specific motion rules, test diverse real-camera conditions with clinicians, and build secure, consent-based cross-device access. We also want to measure preparation time and follow-up usefulness in practice before claiming productivity or clinical gains. Less preparation work. More informed conversations. Clinical decisions stay human. Turn browser-agent actions into visible, source-linked handoffs your team can verify and continue. Like Comment Story Updates Inspiration For product and engineering teams using multiple AI assistants, the expensive step is often not generating an answer—it is moving a decision or finding to the next person or agent without losing its source, owner, or next action. Copying a summary into another chat strips away that provenance and makes the handoff hard to verify. Gatherwire explores a simple answer: give a person and a browser agent the same visible workspace, then make a handoff a durable, source-linked product object instead of another disposable chat message. What it does Gatherwire is a public WebMCP collaboration workspace. A person can use it like a compact team room. From that same top-level page, a compatible browser agent discovers seven structured site tools: gatherwire_list_spaces gatherwire_read_messages gatherwire_search_messages gatherwire_list_project_agents gatherwire_publish_handoff gatherwire_post_message gatherwire_create_space The main demo is a five-call mission. The agent lists the spaces, finds a seeded handoff note, reads its source room, finds the available demo project agent, and publishes a handoff to Atlas in the product room using the source message ID as evidence. The result appears immediately in the human interface: a visible capsule with a summary, one next action, the receiving agent, linked evidence, source room, task ID, and correlation ID. A separate receipt panel records the tool call, and clicking the evidence returns to the exact source message. The page marks the mission 5/5 , so evaluators can verify progress without opening a separate transcript. Gatherwire's completed challenge boundary is the handoff layer, not an external agent runtime. Atlas is synthetic and no external agent is started; this keeps discovery, shared state, provenance, and safe write behavior directly testable without implying a production bridge exists. Why WebMCP This experience depends on the page and visitor session the person already has open. A traditional remote API can expose backend functions, but it does not automatically give an agent the exact workspace, on-page progress, or visible receipts that the person sees. WebMCP lets the site declare a narrow capability layer directly: The browser agent does not have to guess where to click or scrape the interface. Human actions and agent calls use the same operations and the same HTTP API. Agent writes refresh the visible product state instead of disappearing into an isolated tool transcript. Inputs, side effects, and boundaries are explicit and inspectable. Without a page-native tool layer, this workflow typically means brittle UI automation or a separately installed integration, plus manual copying between chats. Gatherwire exposes the workflow from the page itself and turns a found message into a traceable collaboration artifact in one bounded flow. How we built it The page registers seven imperative tools with document.modelContext.registerTool(...) . Each definition has a focused description, a bounded JSON Schema, read/write annotations, and JSON-safe output. We intentionally avoided a single all-powerful “operate the workspace” tool. The human UI and WebMCP handlers call the same API. A dependency-free Node.js service serves the application and manages an isolated sample workspace for each visitor. Session IDs are opaque and host-only; unknown caller-supplied IDs are rotated instead of adopted. The handoff endpoint accepts only the synthetic agent returned by the demo. Every evidence ID must exist in the selected source space. Successful writes return stable task and correlation IDs. A caller-supplied request ID makes a retry idempotent; reusing the same ID with different content is rejected. All user-controlled content is length-bounded and rendered as text, never HTML. The live app runs on Google Cloud Compute Engine behind a dedicated Cloudflare Tunnel at gatherwire.vistua.de . The repository includes the complete source, deployment files, MIT license, verification record, and 15 automated tests covering session isolation, evidence validation, idempotency, security headers, tool contracts, and registration. Challenges The hardest question was not how to register a tool. It was how much authority a public demo should honestly give it. A useful handoff needs enough context to be actionable, but an unconstrained “run an agent” endpoint would be unsafe and misleading. We separated discovery, reading, searching, posting, room creation, agent listing, and handoff publication. We allowlisted the destination, required source-local evidence, and kept execution outside the challenge demo. The second challenge was legibility. A successful tool call is not enough if the person cannot tell what happened. That led to the five-step mission, visible receipts, evidence links, stable identifiers, and the handoff capsule itself. Accomplishments Seven native WebMCP tools are discoverable and executable from the live page. The complete mission was run in ChatGPT's in-app browser using native WebMCP. The final handoff was read back from the target room with matching task and correlation IDs. Repeating the same write request returned the existing capsule instead of creating a duplicate. Fifteen automated tests pass with zero failures, errors, or skips. Every visitor gets an isolated workspace; no account or credential is required. No customer data, private repository, production conversation, or external agent participates in the demo. What we learned WebMCP makes tool design part of product design. A good tool is not only callable; it has a truthful name, a narrow contract, understandable side effects, useful output, and visible feedback. We also learned that provenance can be a collaboration primitive. A summary is easy to lose or distrust. A source-linked artifact with evidence and stable IDs can survive the chat that created it and support the next person or agent. Challenge-period work The broader idea of Slack-like collaboration between people and project-bound agents, plus private experiments with backend orchestration, existed before this challenge. Gatherwire itself is a new standalone WebMCP app created during the submission period. Define a domain once, then give people and browser agents the same safe, structured application. Like Comment Story Updates Inspiration Most web applications are built for people to click and for agents to guess. Agents scrape labels and infer controls from fragile DOMs. s-m-r-t asks a different question: what if an application's real domain model, permissions, workflows, and interface also defined a deliberate browser-native tool surface? Job hunting made that problem concrete. Finding opportunities, checking sources, triaging listings, recording notes, and preparing application packets are repetitive and stressful. They benefit from agent help, while final approval and employer submission should remain unmistakably human decisions. What it does s-m-r-t is the entry. It is a TypeScript framework for building operational applications around one typed domain model. Decorated business objects generate aligned persistence, REST, CLI, MCP, WebMCP, browser-data, and AI surfaces. WebMCP is not a separate demo API pasted onto an application: it is a governed browser projection of the same model and application contracts. For the challenge, s-m-r-t gained: canonical WebMCP definitions for deliberately API-exposed models; authenticated execution through the existing REST boundary; explicit exposure policies, effect classification, tool budgets, and namespaces; lifecycle-safe registration through document.modelContext ; mounted form-control and data-surface adapters, giving agents and people the same UI state; staged form edits requiring human review; bounded queries and durable actions with stable identity and confirmation boundaries; declarative view intents and layered playbooks that make the agent surface statically knowable; local, self-hosted, and managed-cloud runtime profiles preserving the same tools and policies. Iolaus is the demonstration application built with s-m-r-t. It is a local-first, single-user employment-search app using SQLite. A browser agent inspects source health, browses and investigates fictional job listings, records a triage decision and note, advances the queue, opens a shortlist, and inspects an application packet. Those actions update the same state the person sees. There is intentionally no WebMCP tool for final approval or employer submission. Why WebMCP is essential Without WebMCP, the agent needs brittle DOM automation or a separate privileged integration. With s-m-r-t, each page advertises bounded tools through document.modelContext . Those tools carry effect metadata, use authenticated application routes, respect field and exposure policies, and disappear with the page lifecycle. Mounted view tools change the same state the person sees, while background queries inspect data without disturbing the view. The collaboration is legible: the agent performs structured work, the person sees its results, and sensitive transitions remain behind explicit human review. The implementation is reusable across application domains instead of being tied to one job board or agent harness. How we built it s-m-r-t is a pnpm TypeScript monorepo. Its scanner reads @smrt() declarations and emits manifests, schemas, routes, clients, and canonical tool descriptors. @happyvertical/smrt-web validates and registers generated and bespoke browser tools. @happyvertical/smrt-svelte binds those contracts to mounted forms and data surfaces. Its application runtime composes SQLite/local, PostgreSQL/self-hosted, and managed-cloud profiles without changing domain objects or WebMCP policy. The execution path fails closed. The decorator controls model-operation exposure; authenticated REST remains the authorization boundary; undeclared effects are classified as destructive; sensitive fields are omitted; stale tools refuse execution; and staged edits cannot self-confirm. Integration fixtures exercise generated definitions, authentication failures, registration lifecycle, policy changes, mounted UI tools, and cross-profile parity. Iolaus consumes published s-m-r-t packages and provides an isolated demo profile. pnpm demo:prepare builds and migrates private SQLite state, seeds visibly fictional opportunities and crawl provenance, starts a loopback-only server, and opens a one-time owner handoff. pnpm demo:smoke runs an authenticated browser proof and verifies both the available capabilities and the absence of approval/submission tools. Challenges we ran into The hardest problem was preserving one security and lifecycle model across generated model tools, page-mounted controls, custom component tools, and multiple runtime profiles. A capability policy is only useful if every registration path passes through it. We also had to separate discovery from authorization, exclude sensitive candidate fields, and prevent remounts or policy changes from leaving stale tools behind. The local-first install introduced another systems challenge: safe owner bootstrap, idempotent SQLite setup, private on-device state, deterministic fixtures, and recovery commands all had to work without weakening the deployed application model. Accomplishments that we're proud of WebMCP is a first-class generated framework surface, not a hand-built endpoint for one demo. The same declarations drive persistence, APIs, MCP, WebMCP, clients, and knowledge artifacts. Human-visible controls and agent commands converge on shared registries and state. Effect policy and human approval boundaries are explicit and testable. Iolaus installs locally and proves a useful workflow without transmitting an application. Cross-profile fixtures verify that infrastructure choices do not silently change the agent surface. What we learned WebMCP works best as an application architecture concern, not a thin automation layer. Browser tools should derive from the same source of truth as data and authorization. A strong human-agent experience also needs bounded inputs, visible shared state, effect metadata, lifecycle cleanup, deterministic testing, and carefully chosen places where the agent must stop. What's next for s-m-r-t We will continue hardening conformance across generated, mounted, and declared agent surfaces; improve route-aware capability discovery; and make the one-prompt local installation pattern reusable for other s-m-r-t applications. Iolaus will remain a real-world proving ground while preserving private local data and human control of external actions.
