# FROZEN SCORING RUBRIC (pre-registered before any Stage 1 review)

Scores are integers 1-10 on each of the FOUR official criteria. Every score carries:
rationale, evidence_surfaces, confidence (0-1). Reviewers use the full scale and these
band anchors verbatim. Category neutrality is absolute: a game, art piece, music tool,
CRM, weird toy, and developer tool are all eligible for 10/10 on every criterion if they
satisfy that criterion exceptionally well FOR WHAT THEY ARE TRYING TO BE.
No aesthetic penalties. Signs of hurried AI work matter only as actual product-quality
problems (incoherence, broken flow, placeholder content), never as style.

## WebMCP Leverage — what does WebMCP change for this product?
Judge the delta WebMCP makes to reliability, precision, structured state access,
shared human-agent state, repeatability, and capability. Tool COUNT is not leverage.
- 9-10 TRANSFORMATIVE: the intended experience is impractical without the structured
  agent surface; human and agent share live product state; the collaboration could not
  be replicated comparably well by a general agent driving the UI.
- 7-8 MAJOR DELTA: without WebMCP a general agent is markedly less reliable/precise
  here; the agent is central to the product, not a bolt-on.
- 5-6 MEANINGFUL DELTA: existing capability packaged into a genuinely better product
  experience via structured tools; same outcome otherwise possible with real friction.
- 3-4 MINOR DELTA: generic browser automation achieves comparable outcomes; WebMCP is
  a convenience layer.
- 1-2 COSMETIC: exposes an action a UI button already offers; no meaningful change.

## Execution — is the actual product coherent, intentional, complete for its scope?
Stage 1 scores EXECUTION_CLAIMED: coherence/intentionality/completeness as evidenced by
the packet (description + packaging + video frames/transcript). Thin evidence caps the
score (end-to-end credit requires end-to-end evidence) and lowers confidence.
Stage 2 may rescore as EXECUTION_OBSERVED; latest evidence-backed score replaces, never
sums.
- 9-10: all surfaces agree; central workflow demonstrably completes; states handled;
  feels finished for its stated scope.
- 7-8: strong coherence and intentionality; central workflow appears functional; minor
  unfinished edges.
- 5-6: plausibly real with visible gaps; scope unclear or core not demonstrated
  end-to-end.
- 3-4: thin prototype; contradictions between surfaces; placeholder content.
- 1-2: non-functional or incoherent for its claimed scope.

## Creativity & Ambition — novelty of concept and interaction model
Judge ONLY: novelty of the concept, novelty of the interaction model, originality
relative to existing concepts, ambition, memorable/surprising use of the medium, and
the depth with which the concept is pursued. WebMCP indispensability belongs under
WebMCP Leverage, NOT here: a project can be extremely creative even if another agent
API could theoretically have enabled something similar.
- 9-10: genuinely novel concept or interaction model; ambitious scope pursued deeply
  and coherently; memorable.
- 7-8: fresh synthesis or notably original interaction; ambitious for a hackathon.
- 5-6: competent take on a known category with a real new angle.
- 3-4: derivative; novelty mostly surface details.
- 1-2: clone/template with trivial changes.
"That the category exists" is never a penalty; judge the submitted concept.

## Potential Impact — credible value for the intended audience
Judge: is there a real audience; is there a real problem, desire, or use case for that
audience; does the demonstrated solution genuinely address it. NO commercial adoption,
monetization, market pull, or startup viability is required. Entertainment, play, art,
creative expression, learning, niche hobby use, experimentation, and community value
are legitimate forms of impact. A tiny audience fully justifies a top score.
- 9-10: specific real audience with a real problem/desire; solution demonstrably
  addresses it; credible value, appeal, usefulness, or meaning for that audience.
- 7-8: clear audience and problem, good fit, credible value.
- 5-6: identifiable audience, looser fit or partial address.
- 3-4: speculative audience or strained fit; impact mostly aspirational.
- 1-2: no coherent audience/problem pairing.
Audience size is NOT a multiplier.

## Aggregate & ranking
aggregate = leverage + execution + impact + creativity (4-40). Nothing else enters.
Tie-breaks: Leverage, then Execution, then Impact, then Creativity, then higher
combined evidence confidence, then slug alphabetical. See PROTOCOL.md Ranking section.

A storefront whose WebMCP tools hand the agent a question instead of a guess — and that reads a whole sentence: budget, refusals and all. Like Comment Story Updates Why this use case is a strong fit for WebMCP Every storefront in this challenge will expose the same shape of tool: get-products , filter-products , show-products . The WebMCP spec's own e-commerce example does exactly that. All of them assume the store's job is to answer . For most of a shopping conversation that assumption is wrong. Ask this catalog for "a belt" and 97 products match; the top-ranked one beats the tenth by 5%. Ranking them is a coin flip dressed as a recommendation. One more fact — what it is made of — clears forty of them on average. The store knows this; it has the distribution in front of it. But a tool that can only return products has no way to say so, so it returns its coin flip and lets the agent present a guess as an answer. WebMCP is the first place a website can express its own uncertainty as a first-class outcome. search_products returns one of two shapes: a ranked list, or a single clarifying question with options and counts. The spec lists "fully autonomous workflows" under its Non-Goals and says the human interface stays primary; a tool that declines to guess is the most direct expression of that intent we could build. It puts the person back in the loop at the exact moment the machine cannot decide — not as a permission prompt bolted on afterwards, but because the site genuinely does not know and says so. How it creates a better user experience The store listens the way an agent talks. A search box gets "leather belt". An agent relaying a person gets "I'm looking for a leather belt, nothing with a snap, not over $50". Fed to a keyword matcher, that sentence requires "looking" to appear in a title, reads "snap" as a requirement , and never sees the budget. Counterask parses budgets, refusals ("not leather", "no laces", "nothing from Nike"), orderings ("cheapest", "best rated") and stated attributes out of the sentence itself. Measured on 800 such sentences written from real product records, refusals inverted into requirements went from 100% to 0%, budgets broken in the top ten from 31% to 0%, and Hit@10 from 0.79 to 0.99 — under phrasings the parser was never tuned on as well. On the build that ships, the two benchmarks read Hit@10 0.998 (two-word shopper) and 0.993 (agent-relayed sentences), with every listening check at zero. It never asks what it was told. Anything stated is a constraint, not a question. "No preference" is remembered, so the same question cannot come straight back. The agent can pass whatever it already knows from the conversation — attributes, exclusions, budget, facets the shopper does not mind — structured, and structured input wins over the parse. It says what it heard, and what still differs. Every result carries an understood block echoing exactly how the request was read, so the agent can check before trusting the answer and the person sees the same thing as chips. When the store does answer, it names the attributes that still separate the products shown — "eleven buckle, one pull-on" — so the agent can summarise rather than read out a list. "No results" is never the last word. When nothing survives every requirement, the store lifts each one in turn, re-counts, and reports which is doing the damage. The agent gets relax ; the person gets buttons. What people and agents can do together that was difficult before The person supplies the one thing the store cannot compute: which of two equally good attributes they actually care about. The store supplies the one thing the person cannot see: that this is the moment their answer matters, which attribute it is, and what choosing each option would leave. The agent carries the question across, in the person's own words, and carries the answer back. Before WebMCP this loop did not exist. An agent driving a site through its DOM sees a grid, not a decision. A site behind a REST API cannot register a tool that appears only while a question is open and vanishes when it is answered — here answer_question does exactly that, so the tool list itself tells the agent what the page is waiting for. And a person and an agent clicking or calling land in the same function: one state machine, two surfaces, nothing that can drift between what a human sees and what an agent is told. None of this is about menswear. The parser reads whatever attribute vocabulary the catalog builder emits; the stopping policy reasons about evidence and pool size, not clothing; the tools describe a store, not a category. Swap the catalog and the same tools ask the right question about laptops, groceries or hotel rooms — and the "no results" recovery, the understood echo and the tool-that-appears-when-needed come with them. Concretely, in one session: "find me a wallet that's not leather, under $30" → the store returns 30 candidates and one question ( which category — wallets, card cases, money clips? ) → the agent asks the person → the person answers → the store answers with what still differs → the agent curates the grid with show_products so the person sees the pick, not a transcript → "why that one?" → explain_ranking , deterministic, quotable. Every tool carries a title . The visit — search, open question, cart — is remembered across reloads. checkout — a declarative <form toolname="checkout"> with toolparamdescription on its fields and without toolautosubmit : an agent can fill in the name and address, the browser focuses the button, and only the person places the order. The submit handler answers an agent-invoked submission through SubmitEvent.respondWith() . Results are MCP-shaped on the wire ( content text plus structuredContent ), so a client following either convention reads the same thing. Like Comment Story Updates Showcase Why FigureFoundry Is a Strong Fit for WebMCP Scientific figure editors are exactly the kind of dense, form-heavy applications where agents currently struggle. Agents are often forced to navigate these interfaces by clicking through an unfamiliar DOM, while researchers spend excessive amounts of time manually performing tasks that an agent could draft in seconds. FigureFoundry addresses this directly. It registers seven document.modelContext tools: inspect_dataset_catalog inspect_dataset_fields inspect_figure_workspace analyze_dataset analyze_group_comparison propose_figure_revision apply_figure_revision This allows an agent to inspect the actual dataset schema and current figure state rather than guessing at the interface structure. The agent can then propose a structured revision that FigureFoundry itself validates. How It Creates a Better User Experience A researcher can request something as simple as: "Compare body mass by species, colored by island." Instead of blindly interacting with the editor, the agent inspects the bound dataset's actual fields, runs a deterministic analysis, and stages a typed Figure Spec diff against the relevant panel. Nothing immediately changes on the canvas. Every proposal is presented as a preview, and applying it requires explicit user confirmation showing both the target panel and the revision being applied. The result is an agent that assists with the tedious work without taking scientific control away from the researcher. What's Newly Possible Previously, turning a request such as "compare these groups" into a correct, publication-ready panel required manual field mapping, chart-type selection, and statistical setup. With WebMCP, the agent can perform the data inspection and analysis steps first. The researcher remains responsible for the scientific judgment: approving, discarding, or editing the proposed revision. Every applied change is also recorded in an append-only provenance ledger, including the revision it was based on. This keeps the figure's history reproducible even when an agent contributes to its construction. How We Implemented It The tools are schema-bound to the active dataset, meaning an agent cannot propose a field that does not actually exist. apply_figure_revision uses optimistic concurrency control through basedOnRevision and rejects proposals that are stale, unapproved, or target an invalid panel. This makes the agent integration operational rather than cosmetic: the model is given structured access to the application's actual state, while the application remains responsible for validation and state changes. Why FigureFoundry There is no doubt that FigureFoundry is a straightforward concept. That is precisely why I chose to build it. Its value is not in being a flashy novelty application. The efficiency implications are the point. Scientific figure editing contains exactly the kind of repetitive, structured interaction where WebMCP can have a meaningful impact: the agent can understand the data and application state, prepare the work, and explain what it intends to change, while the researcher retains control over the final scientific decision. That is where I believe WebMCP has the greatest potential: not replacing the user, but removing the friction between what the user wants to accomplish and the interface they currently have to operate manually. Like 1 Comment Story Updates Dynamic paths Home page Flight Recorder for tool calls Decisions, assumptions, and goals Testing with WebMCP tools Chrome extension Tutorial mode Inspiration AI agents are becoming increasingly capable of taking actions across the web, but users do not always get a clear picture of the consequences before those actions happen. A decision like buying a car can affect several competing goals at once, yet most financial tools reduce that complexity to a single static calculation. I built Counterfactual around a simple question: what if an agent could show you several possible futures before asking you to commit to one? Instead of immediately executing an action, Counterfactual gives the agent and user a shared space to explore alternatives, revise assumptions, understand tradeoffs, and decide together which future—if any—should become real. What it does Counterfactual is a WebMCP-powered decision simulator and permission layer for the agentic web. Through WebMCP, an AI agent can discover structured tools exposed by Counterfactual, read the user's financial assumptions, create alternative scenarios, simulate their outcomes, and compare them. Counterfactual turns those scenarios into explorable futures with dynamic visualizations that show how each decision affects the user's finances and protected goals. Users can change an assumption at any time and immediately see every future recalculate. A built-in WebMCP Flight Recorder makes the agent's behavior visible by recording each tool it discovers and invokes, along with its arguments, risk level, origin, status, and results. Rather than receiving only the agent's final recommendation, users can inspect the actual sequence of actions that produced it. Most importantly, Counterfactual distinguishes between exploring a future and making it real . Read-only and simulation tools can run freely, but when an agent attempts a consequential action, Counterfactual pauses execution and requires explicit human approval. The result is a simple principle: agents can explore autonomously, but humans retain control over commitment. How I built it I built Counterfactual with Next.js, TypeScript, Tailwind CSS, and client-side state . Its scenario engine uses deterministic calculations so the agent's conclusions and the visual interface always operate from the same source of truth. Counterfactual exposes structured WebMCP tools through document.modelContext.registerTool() . These include tools for reading financial state, defining a decision, protecting goals, creating scenarios, comparing outcomes, simulating purchases, and committing to a selected scenario. Each tool has a natural-language description, JSON Schema input definition, validation, and metadata describing its behavior and risk. To support browsers that do not yet implement WebMCP natively, Counterfactual uses the @mcp-b/webmcp-polyfill package. A thin compatibility layer detects the available context—native, polyfilled, legacy, or fallback—and registers the same tool surface against it. Every invocation, whether initiated manually or by an external agent, passes through the same execution and logging pipeline. Calls are timestamped and risk-tagged before appearing in the Flight Recorder. Consequential actions go through an additional policy and approval layer before they can modify committed state. The approval is tied to that specific proposed action, preventing an agent from silently turning a simulation into a real commitment. I also tested the WebMCP integration end-to-end using Google's webmcp-evals CLI. That testing uncovered an issue that was invisible through Counterfactual's own UI but appeared when a tool was invoked through the actual WebMCP path. Fixing it helped ensure the project works through the protocol itself rather than only through a scripted demo. Challenges I ran into The biggest challenge was building against an experimental standard whose API and browser support are still evolving. Counterfactual needed to target the current document.modelContext API while remaining usable in browsers and agents relying on earlier or unsupported implementations. Another challenge was maintaining a single source of truth across the agent, editable controls, scenario calculations, visual branches, and Flight Recorder. A tool call could not simply generate an answer—it had to operate on the same live state the user was seeing and immediately update the rest of the experience. The final challenge was designing meaningful human oversight without making the agent feel unnecessarily constrained. Requiring approval for every tool call would defeat much of the value of an agent, while allowing every action to execute automatically would remove meaningful user control. Counterfactual therefore treats risk as a gradient: agents can read, simulate, fork, and compare independently, while actions that create real commitments stop at the point where human judgment matters most. Accomplishments that I'm proud of I'm proud that Counterfactual uses WebMCP as a core interaction model , rather than adding it as a superficial integration. The agent does not simply fill out a form or operate a scripted demo—it collaborates with the user inside a shared decision environment. I also built a complete flow from tool discovery → simulation → comparison → recommendation → approval . Users can see what the agent is doing, inspect how it reached its recommendation, modify the assumptions themselves, explore alternative futures, and ultimately reject or approve the proposed action. Most importantly, Counterfactual turns an abstract AI safety problem into something tangible: consequential agent actions become forkable futures that people can inspect before making them real. What I learned I learned that WebMCP is more than a reliable way for agents to interact with websites. It can enable entirely new forms of human-agent collaboration . Structured tools allow an agent and an interface to share the same capabilities and application state without relying on fragile DOM interpretation or browser automation. I also learned that confirmation alone is not enough to provide meaningful user control. Before approving an action, people need to understand what they are approving, which goals may be affected, what alternatives exist, and whether the action can be reversed. Building Counterfactual showed me how structured tool schemas, deterministic application logic, risk classification, and visual design can work together to make agent behavior more transparent, understandable, and trustworthy. Why my use case is a strong fit for WebMCP Many agent experiences focus on reading the web: searching, summarizing, and answering questions. Counterfactual focuses on the harder problem— what happens when an agent can act on something that matters? That is where WebMCP becomes especially valuable. Counterfactual's decision process maps naturally onto a set of well-defined tools such as get_financial_state , define_decision , set_protected_goal , fork_scenario , compare_scenarios , and commit_scenario . Those tools also span a genuine risk gradient. Reading financial state is fundamentally different from simulating a hypothetical decision, and both are fundamentally different from committing to one. Counterfactual uses that distinction to determine when an agent can proceed independently and when control must return to the user. Instead of bolting permissions onto a chat interface after the fact, the application's capabilities, schemas, risk levels, and guardrails are part of the same structured tool environment the agent discovers and uses.
