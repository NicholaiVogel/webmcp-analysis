# FROZEN SCORING RUBRIC (pre-registered before any Stage 1 review)

Scores are integers 1-10 on each of the FOUR official criteria. Every score carries:
rationale, evidence_surfaces, confidence (0-1). Reviewers use the full scale and these
band anchors verbatim. Category neutrality is absolute: a game, art piece, music tool,
CRM, weird toy, and developer tool are all eligible for 10/10 on every criterion if they
satisfy that criterion exceptionally well FOR WHAT THEY ARE TRYING TO BE.
No aesthetic penalties. Signs of hurried AI work matter only as actual product-quality
problems (incoherence, broken flow, placeholder content), never as style.

## WebMCP Leverage — what does WebMCP change for this product?
Judge the delta WebMCP makes to reliability, precision, structured state access,
shared human-agent state, repeatability, and capability. Tool COUNT is not leverage.
- 9-10 TRANSFORMATIVE: the intended experience is impractical without the structured
  agent surface; human and agent share live product state; the collaboration could not
  be replicated comparably well by a general agent driving the UI.
- 7-8 MAJOR DELTA: without WebMCP a general agent is markedly less reliable/precise
  here; the agent is central to the product, not a bolt-on.
- 5-6 MEANINGFUL DELTA: existing capability packaged into a genuinely better product
  experience via structured tools; same outcome otherwise possible with real friction.
- 3-4 MINOR DELTA: generic browser automation achieves comparable outcomes; WebMCP is
  a convenience layer.
- 1-2 COSMETIC: exposes an action a UI button already offers; no meaningful change.

## Execution — is the actual product coherent, intentional, complete for its scope?
Stage 1 scores EXECUTION_CLAIMED: coherence/intentionality/completeness as evidenced by
the packet (description + packaging + video frames/transcript). Thin evidence caps the
score (end-to-end credit requires end-to-end evidence) and lowers confidence.
Stage 2 may rescore as EXECUTION_OBSERVED; latest evidence-backed score replaces, never
sums.
- 9-10: all surfaces agree; central workflow demonstrably completes; states handled;
  feels finished for its stated scope.
- 7-8: strong coherence and intentionality; central workflow appears functional; minor
  unfinished edges.
- 5-6: plausibly real with visible gaps; scope unclear or core not demonstrated
  end-to-end.
- 3-4: thin prototype; contradictions between surfaces; placeholder content.
- 1-2: non-functional or incoherent for its claimed scope.

## Creativity & Ambition — novelty of concept and interaction model
Judge ONLY: novelty of the concept, novelty of the interaction model, originality
relative to existing concepts, ambition, memorable/surprising use of the medium, and
the depth with which the concept is pursued. WebMCP indispensability belongs under
WebMCP Leverage, NOT here: a project can be extremely creative even if another agent
API could theoretically have enabled something similar.
- 9-10: genuinely novel concept or interaction model; ambitious scope pursued deeply
  and coherently; memorable.
- 7-8: fresh synthesis or notably original interaction; ambitious for a hackathon.
- 5-6: competent take on a known category with a real new angle.
- 3-4: derivative; novelty mostly surface details.
- 1-2: clone/template with trivial changes.
"That the category exists" is never a penalty; judge the submitted concept.

## Potential Impact — credible value for the intended audience
Judge: is there a real audience; is there a real problem, desire, or use case for that
audience; does the demonstrated solution genuinely address it. NO commercial adoption,
monetization, market pull, or startup viability is required. Entertainment, play, art,
creative expression, learning, niche hobby use, experimentation, and community value
are legitimate forms of impact. A tiny audience fully justifies a top score.
- 9-10: specific real audience with a real problem/desire; solution demonstrably
  addresses it; credible value, appeal, usefulness, or meaning for that audience.
- 7-8: clear audience and problem, good fit, credible value.
- 5-6: identifiable audience, looser fit or partial address.
- 3-4: speculative audience or strained fit; impact mostly aspirational.
- 1-2: no coherent audience/problem pairing.
Audience size is NOT a multiplier.

## Aggregate & ranking
aggregate = leverage + execution + impact + creativity (4-40). Nothing else enters.
Tie-breaks: Leverage, then Execution, then Impact, then Creativity, then higher
combined evidence confidence, then slug alphabetical. See PROTOCOL.md Ranking section.

What it does Each demo walks you through a conventional user interface, and then cordons off a bounded portion of it as WebMCP to redesign (the Live Canvas) The Storefront demo lets the agent curate the search results with a toolbox of approved card presets and badges. How we built it The architecture north star is "agent composition, application execution". The application exposes its UI primitives through WebMCP. The agent supplies a composition, and then the live canvas renders that composition using application-owned components. What we learned WebMCP shows great potential to break interfaces out of the "ai chatbot jail". What's next for Live Canvas The most likely next step is to select an existing open-source component library and use it to build the first end-to-end WebMCP UI framework. That would turn the Live Canvas from just a demo pattern into a production-hardened implementation. When AI agents arrived, they could write code but lacked spatial awareness—unable to check if a door would swing into an oven or optimize sheet cuts on a table saw. We built Cabinet Maker Xtreme to bridge that gap: a browser-based studio where woodworkers and AI agents work within the exact same 3D spatial model to turn kitchen designs into machine-ready cut lists. What it does Parametric 3D Studio: Place, move, and snap standard cabinetry with live clearance badges and elevation snap locks. NKBA & Physical Clash Validation: Detects door swing collisions, drawer binds, and kitchen work triangle compliance ($12\text{ft} \le \text{Triangle} \le 26\text{ft}$) in real time. 2D Sheet Nesting: Guillotine bin-packs panels onto $48'' \times 96''$ plywood sheets, deducting edge-banding thickness ($W_{\text{raw}} = W_{\text{finished}} - 2T_{\text{band}}$) and blade kerf. CNC-Ready DXF & Assembly Guides: Exports layered DXFs ( OUTLINE_CUT , POCKET_DADO , BORE_32MM ) and vector assembly manuals. Agent-Native WebMCP: Exposes 26 tools with strict JSON schemas so AI models can design, inspect, and undo actions with full parity to human mouse clicks. How we built it Integer Sixteenth Geometry: To eliminate floating-point drift across long cabinet runs, all spatial math uses integer sixteenths: $$\text{Sixteenths} = \text{round}(\text{Inches} \times 16)$$ Wall coordinates project into world space via rotation angle $\theta$: $$x_{\text{world}} = x_{\text{start}} + x_{\text{local}} \cos\theta + z_{\text{local}} \sin\theta, \quad z_{\text{world}} = z_{\text{start}} - x_{\text{local}} \sin\theta + z_{\text{local}} \cos\theta$$ Parametric Builder: Compiles carcasses, $32\text{mm}$ system boring, and door styles (Shaker, Flat Slab, Raised Panel) procedurally on the fly. Stack: Built with Next.js, React 19, Three.js, React Three Fiber, and Zustand. Challenges we ran into Planning WebMCP Tool Contracts: We initially treated WebMCP as a secondary API instead of an architectural foundation. Lacking an upfront schema abstraction layer meant hand-wiring 26 tool wrappers, while overlooking transactional parity initially left the agent without undo capabilities. Raycast Self-Obstruction: Transient placement previews hovered directly under the cursor, intercepting mouse rays and freezing movement. We fixed this by setting raycast={() => null} on ghost meshes and adding an active-wall placement plane. 3D Marquee Selection: Projecting world coordinates through the perspective camera into screen space ($[-1, 1]$ NDC) required factoring in local depth offsets, or floating countertops were miscalculated from angled viewpoints. Mobile Touch Collisions: Moving the 3D tool dock to the bottom of mobile viewports caused it to be blocked by the validation panel's DOM overlay. We adjusted z-indexes and resting offsets to preserve $44\text{px}$ touch targets. Accomplishments that we're proud of Zero-Setup Screen-to-Machine: Designing a kitchen in-browser and downloading CNC-ready nested DXF cut sheets in seconds. Full Agent-Human Parity: AI models use the exact same state machine, revision guards, and undo history as human clicks. Pure Client-Side Speed: Real-time parametric rendering, sheet nesting, and BOM generation running at 60 FPS in WebGL without server round-trips. What we learned Agent Affordances Belong Up Front: AI agents require the same affordances as humans (especially error recovery and undo stacks); treating agent tools as an afterthought creates technical debt. Decouple Visual Detail from Domain Data: 3D aesthetic details (like multi-piece Shaker door stiles) must be rendered in the view layer without cluttering the canonical carcass model used for cut lists and CAD sheets. What's next for Cabinet Maker Xtreme Direct G-code post-processors for Shapeoko, ShopBot, and industrial CNC routers. Live supplier catalog APIs (Blum, Salice) for automated hardware procurement. Multi-agent collaboration for automated commercial project estimation and shop scheduling. Like Comment Story Updates Nivra: result of changes Nivra: issue found Nivra: HLD view Nivra: proposal solution Nivra: final cut Nivra: LLD view for Checkout Inspiration Software architecture is usually spread across diagrams, documentation, tickets, and conversations. Humans can understand the visual context, while AI agents often receive only screenshots or incomplete text descriptions. We wanted to explore a different model: what if the architect and the agent could work inside the same structured architecture workspace? That idea became Nivra. What it does Nivra is a shared visual architecture reasoning workspace for humans and AI agents. The human works with an interactive architecture map. Through browser-native WebMCP tools, an agent can read the same underlying model, inspect components and dependencies, navigate between high-level and low-level views, record findings, create proposals, and validate architecture against explicit policies. The demo begins with a Commerce Platform where Checkout appears to be independently deployable. Deeper inspection reveals hidden runtime coupling: Basket Adapter directly shares Product Store state across a deployment boundary without an explicit contract. The agent surfaces the evidence, but the architectural decision remains with the human. The architect defines the required policy, and Nivra validates the Current Architecture deterministically. Current fails two of four rules. Nivra then creates a separate remediation proposal that replaces the shared-state dependency with an explicit Checkout Snapshot Contract. The Proposal passes all four policies, while Current Architecture remains unchanged. Why WebMCP Nivra is not an AI assistant discussing a screenshot. WebMCP allows the application to expose structured architecture operations directly to an agent inside the same live page and browser session. Nivra exposes seven WebMCP tools for: reading the active architecture; inspecting elements and relationships; opening and focusing architecture views; recording architectural findings; defining deterministic constraints; creating patch-based proposals; validating Current or Proposal architecture. This makes agent actions inspectable, repeatable, and visible to the human through the Agent Activity timeline. How we built it Nivra is built with React, TypeScript, Vite, React Flow, Zustand, and browser-native WebMCP. The architecture domain model is kept independent from the UI. React Flow renders the visual workspace, while Zustand manages interaction and persistence. WebMCP tools call the same validated workspace actions used by the human-facing interface. Current Architecture is treated as immutable. Proposals are represented as patch-derived alternatives rather than destructive edits. Validation is deterministic and runs against the same policy rules for both Current and Proposal. The project also includes automated unit tests and a repeatable browser-level WebMCP workflow that exercises the complete scenario multiple times from a canonical reset state. Challenges we faced One of the hardest challenges was making the agent experience meaningful without removing human control. The agent needed enough capability to inspect evidence and propose changes, while the architect still had to own policy and approval decisions. We also had to ensure that: repeated agent calls remained idempotent; Current Architecture could never be accidentally overwritten; high-level and low-level views represented the same model; validation results were deterministic; agent operations were visible and understandable; the complete product story remained clear in a demo shorter than three minutes. Working with an experimental browser API also required careful feature detection and graceful behavior in browsers where WebMCP is unavailable. Accomplishments that we're proud of We built a complete human-agent workflow rather than a technical proof of concept. Nivra can move from architecture discovery to evidence, human policy, remediation proposal, deterministic validation, implementation planning, and a saved verified architecture branch—all inside one coherent workspace. The original Current Architecture remains available throughout the entire process. What we learned The most important lesson was that agent-native applications need more than a chat interface. Agents become more useful when applications expose precise domain operations, stable identifiers, validated inputs, and observable outcomes. We also learned that a strong human-agent experience is not about maximizing autonomy. It is about giving each participant the right responsibility: the agent discovers and structures evidence, while the human owns intent and architectural decisions. What's next for Nivra Future versions could add architecture imports, collaborative multi-user workspaces, durable server-side storage, real delivery-system integrations, and connections to source control, ADRs, and work-management platforms. The long-term goal is to make architecture a living shared context for engineering teams and their agents. Having come from a competitive running background back in high school, I recognise the importance of having a bespoke training plan and a coach that truly understands your needs and circumstances. \n\n Typical training apps do a basic Q&A to take a low-res "snapshot" of your current fitness levels, then spit out a workout structure that assumes you progress the same way as everyone else. It's no wonder people often get injured from following these rigid regimes! Also, subscription costs add up quickly, and I didn't want to stack yet another $20+ monthly subscription on top of all the ones I already have (like ChatGPT whoop whoop!). \n\n As someone who's keenly interested in tinkering and building with agents, I got the idea to build an agentic coach to go through this marathon training journey with me. While this definitely would've worked adequately with other primitives like Google Sheets, I realised WebMCP could shift the paradigm and enable me to a beautiful, interactive workspace that both my coach agent and I can collaborate on together. \n\n Why this is a strong fit for WebMCP \n\n Good coaching is more than just generic advice generation. A coach needs access to the runner’s current training plan, recent workouts, personal constraints and preferences; the runner needs to understand why the coach proposes a certain training regime (i.e. provenance). Therefore, a bespoke training regime should be transparent and collaborative. \n\n WebMCP enables the web app itself to be the shared interface between runner and agent. The agent can read canonical context and propose tweaks through purpose-built tools, while the runner can see the same evidence and review those changes in the workspace. Neither side has to rely on invisible state and/or context held only within a conversation. \n\n How this creates a better training experience \n\n Conversation remains the best way for a runner to describe subjective experiences such as fatigue, discomfort or a workout that was given up on. The workspace is better suited to displaying calendars, evidence and before-and-after comparisons. Your Last Coach lets each interface do what it does best. \n\n A fresh coach agent can retrieve a structured Coaching Briefing, inspect the relevant workout(s) and preserve the runner’s feedback in their own words. Its recommendations then appear as an interactive review inside the training calendar rather than as a wall of text in chat. The runner can compare the options and explicitly approve one before anything changes. \n\n Taken together, these make for a more collaborative and grounded coaching experience, where the runner can have full confidence that they are receiving a training plan that's truly personalised for them. \n\n How I implemented WebMCP \n\n I built Your Last Coach as a client-side React and TypeScript application with one authoritative workspace state shared by both the visible interface and the WebMCP tools. This prevents the agent and runner from looking at separate versions of the training plan. \n\n The page exposes six tools covering the coaching journey: \n\n \n Retrieving the Coaching Briefing \n Reading the workout plan and any completed workout results \n Recording the runner’s feedback \n Opening a window for the runner to review plan changes \n Retrieving the runner’s decision. \n \n\n A structured interaction contract helps a fresh agent discover the intended workflow without requiring a custom prompt or skill. \n\n Recommendations are validated by the application and presented as two ranked options in a native review window. The plan is updated atomically when the runner presses “Adapt my plan”. The new plan version and an adaptation receipt are then available to both the runner and agent. \n\n Challenges I ran into \n\n As WebMCP was completely new to me at the start of this project, the main challenge was in learning about how it worked, and brainstorming how I could build my product around this emerging framework. This also forced me to consider the various trade-offs between using WebMCP versus other methods (e.g. for providing my agent with the necessary context so a fresh session can hit the ground running). \n\n Other related challenges were around figuring out the limitations of WebMCP, particularly where it's supported at the moment, since it's still a new concept. For example, I stumbled a little when working in Codex Cloud sessions, as I did not realise that WebMCP wasn't supported in the cloud browser at the time of working on this. \n\n Miscellaneous challenges would be, of course, managing the scope of this submission despite my lofty goals for the final product. I wanted to showcase the key features of WebMCP, without creating an overly convoluted demo product. Time was also a limiting factor here, as I had to compress most of the work into a weekend. For the challenges I faced throughout this experience, I'm pretty proud of the outcome and of myself :) \n\n What's next for Your Last Coach \n\n This demo currently contains synthetic data, so the obvious next step would be to incorporate real health and workout data from my fitness tracker using the COROS MCP. This will require architectural considerations around automating data egress/ingress, storage, security, etc. \n\n In terms of features, I will likely expand the idea of "monitoring topics" as valuable slowly changing context. An early example visible in the demo app today is a topic on an ongoing shin injury. These are important for coaches to know about, but also requires careful planning around mutability, since the state of these topics changes over time, and we would not want our agent coach to plan around topics that are no longer relevant, but still are useful to keep in mind down the line. \n\n Longer-term extensions could be around running-adjacent parts of training, such as nutrition and strength exercises.
