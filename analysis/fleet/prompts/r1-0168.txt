# FROZEN SCORING RUBRIC (pre-registered before any Stage 1 review)

Scores are integers 1-10 on each of the FOUR official criteria. Every score carries:
rationale, evidence_surfaces, confidence (0-1). Reviewers use the full scale and these
band anchors verbatim. Category neutrality is absolute: a game, art piece, music tool,
CRM, weird toy, and developer tool are all eligible for 10/10 on every criterion if they
satisfy that criterion exceptionally well FOR WHAT THEY ARE TRYING TO BE.
No aesthetic penalties. Signs of hurried AI work matter only as actual product-quality
problems (incoherence, broken flow, placeholder content), never as style.

## WebMCP Leverage — what does WebMCP change for this product?
Judge the delta WebMCP makes to reliability, precision, structured state access,
shared human-agent state, repeatability, and capability. Tool COUNT is not leverage.
- 9-10 TRANSFORMATIVE: the intended experience is impractical without the structured
  agent surface; human and agent share live product state; the collaboration could not
  be replicated comparably well by a general agent driving the UI.
- 7-8 MAJOR DELTA: without WebMCP a general agent is markedly less reliable/precise
  here; the agent is central to the product, not a bolt-on.
- 5-6 MEANINGFUL DELTA: existing capability packaged into a genuinely better product
  experience via structured tools; same outcome otherwise possible with real friction.
- 3-4 MINOR DELTA: generic browser automation achieves comparable outcomes; WebMCP is
  a convenience layer.
- 1-2 COSMETIC: exposes an action a UI button already offers; no meaningful change.

## Execution — is the actual product coherent, intentional, complete for its scope?
Stage 1 scores EXECUTION_CLAIMED: coherence/intentionality/completeness as evidenced by
the packet (description + packaging + video frames/transcript). Thin evidence caps the
score (end-to-end credit requires end-to-end evidence) and lowers confidence.
Stage 2 may rescore as EXECUTION_OBSERVED; latest evidence-backed score replaces, never
sums.
- 9-10: all surfaces agree; central workflow demonstrably completes; states handled;
  feels finished for its stated scope.
- 7-8: strong coherence and intentionality; central workflow appears functional; minor
  unfinished edges.
- 5-6: plausibly real with visible gaps; scope unclear or core not demonstrated
  end-to-end.
- 3-4: thin prototype; contradictions between surfaces; placeholder content.
- 1-2: non-functional or incoherent for its claimed scope.

## Creativity & Ambition — novelty of concept and interaction model
Judge ONLY: novelty of the concept, novelty of the interaction model, originality
relative to existing concepts, ambition, memorable/surprising use of the medium, and
the depth with which the concept is pursued. WebMCP indispensability belongs under
WebMCP Leverage, NOT here: a project can be extremely creative even if another agent
API could theoretically have enabled something similar.
- 9-10: genuinely novel concept or interaction model; ambitious scope pursued deeply
  and coherently; memorable.
- 7-8: fresh synthesis or notably original interaction; ambitious for a hackathon.
- 5-6: competent take on a known category with a real new angle.
- 3-4: derivative; novelty mostly surface details.
- 1-2: clone/template with trivial changes.
"That the category exists" is never a penalty; judge the submitted concept.

## Potential Impact — credible value for the intended audience
Judge: is there a real audience; is there a real problem, desire, or use case for that
audience; does the demonstrated solution genuinely address it. NO commercial adoption,
monetization, market pull, or startup viability is required. Entertainment, play, art,
creative expression, learning, niche hobby use, experimentation, and community value
are legitimate forms of impact. A tiny audience fully justifies a top score.
- 9-10: specific real audience with a real problem/desire; solution demonstrably
  addresses it; credible value, appeal, usefulness, or meaning for that audience.
- 7-8: clear audience and problem, good fit, credible value.
- 5-6: identifiable audience, looser fit or partial address.
- 3-4: speculative audience or strained fit; impact mostly aspirational.
- 1-2: no coherent audience/problem pairing.
Audience size is NOT a multiplier.

## Aggregate & ranking
aggregate = leverage + execution + impact + creativity (4-40). Nothing else enters.
Tie-breaks: Leverage, then Execution, then Impact, then Creativity, then higher
combined evidence confidence, then slug alphabetical. See PROTOCOL.md Ranking section.

Knoww is the prediction market layer for the internet. It turns research and live market odds into a shared human-agent workspace for taking trades and preparing decisions with people in final control Like Comment Story Updates What Knoww does Knoww turns live prediction markets into a shared research workspace where people and agents can explore market information together. A person can ask an agent to find relevant events, narrow the visible market list, open a valid result, compare related markets, inspect the selected order book, change the chart range, and prepare the trade interface for review. Every step happens on the same page the person is viewing. The result remains visible, understandable, and easy to correct. Knoww gives the agent structured access to market discovery and analysis without giving it control of a wallet, a signature, or trade submission. The problem Prediction market research involves more than opening a result page. A single event may contain several related markets, outcomes, probabilities, charts, and order books. The useful context also depends on the filters, selected market, and chart range currently visible on the page. A browser agent can attempt to reconstruct this workflow through page inspection and clicks, but it must infer the meaning of interface elements and continuously track changing page state. That process can be fragile. WebMCP lets Knoww expose the meaning behind the interface directly. Instead of guessing which card, control, or outcome represents the user's intent, the agent receives narrow and typed operations connected to the current page. Why WebMCP is the right fit A separate market data integration could return probabilities and market information, but it would not naturally share the interface state the person is currently inspecting. WebMCP allows the person and the agent to work with the same filters, selected market, order book, and chart range. When the agent changes something, the person sees the result immediately through the normal Knoww interface. This creates one continuous research workflow. The agent can discover relevant events, narrow the market list, open a valid result, compare related markets, inspect the selected order book, adjust the chart range, and prepare the trade interface for review. Every action remains visible on the page. The person can verify the result, correct the agent, or stop the workflow at any point. What I built I added 12 WebMCP tools across the market discovery page and individual event pages. On /markets , get_markets_page_context reads the current page state. search_events searches public events. set_market_filters changes only supported visible filters. load_more_markets advances the visible results. open_event can open only an event already returned by Knoww. On an event page, get_current_event_context reads the active event and current selection. find_markets_on_page finds relevant markets within the event. compare_markets returns a focused comparison. get_selected_order_book reads the currently selected order book. select_market_view changes the visible market. set_chart_range changes the visible chart period. prepare_trade prepares the trade interface for the person to review. The available tools change with the page context. The market discovery page exposes discovery and navigation capabilities. An event page exposes comparison, order book, chart, and trade preparation capabilities. This keeps the tool set focused while supporting a complete workflow from market discovery to decision preparation. Example experience A person can ask: "Find Bitcoin related prediction markets, narrow the list, open the most relevant event, compare its markets, inspect the selected order book, and prepare the trade view for my review." The agent can complete this workflow through structured WebMCP tools while the person watches the same page change and remains free to correct any step. This is more reliable than asking the agent to infer the entire workflow from buttons, cards, and page layout. Human control and safety The WebMCP tools cannot connect a wallet, sign a transaction, or submit a trade. prepare_trade only prepares the visible trade interface. The person must inspect the values and complete any action with financial consequences. Every tool uses a typed and bounded input schema. Search limits, filter values, pagination, and navigation targets are constrained. open_event accepts only events already returned by Knoww. Tool results are marked as untrusted public content. These boundaries give the agent enough capability to provide meaningful assistance while preserving human control. Technical implementation Knoww uses Next.js, React, and TypeScript, and is deployed with Cloudflare Workers. It reads public Polymarket market data. I connected the WebMCP handlers to Knoww's existing React state and page actions instead of recreating the market logic in a separate integration. This means the person and the agent work with the same filters, selected market, order book, and chart range. When a tool changes the page state, the normal Knoww interface reflects that change. Tool registration runs through top level page JavaScript so the tools can be discovered by ChatGPT's in app browser. I also added registration cleanup so a normal React unmount does not incorrectly report a registration failure. Testing I tested the production /markets page in ChatGPT's in app browser. ChatGPT discovered all five tools registered on that page. I successfully executed get_markets_page_context and used search_events to find Bitcoin related events on the live production site. The remaining seven tools are registered only on event pages, where their event specific context is available. Challenges and lessons The difficult part was not exposing more control. It was deciding exactly which control the agent should receive. The WebMCP tools had to stay synchronized with React state while maintaining clear boundaries. Search limits, filter values, pagination, navigation targets, and available page actions all needed to be constrained. The page lifecycle also required careful handling so route changes and normal React cleanup did not appear as tool registration failures. The most important lesson was that WebMCP is strongest when it reveals the semantics of an existing interface without bypassing that interface or its permission model. Knoww now gives an agent enough structure to help someone reason about live prediction markets, while stopping exactly where financial authority should remain with the person. I am a strong believer in the paradigm of "generative UI", and the agent dynamically presenting just the information and the functionality the user needs - when he or she needs it. My main realization from the last year of experimentation with MCP and adjacant tech is that agentic user interfaces are not only more efficient, they are also more fun to use. In July 2025 I read about MCP-B on Hacker News and the idea that web apps could expose MCP tools directly to an agent running in the browser. Since I was deep into MCP at that time, I was really excited by this and started exploring the spec. Being in the Microsoft space, I did experiments with MCP-B tools running inside a Power App , and when MCP-B was accompanied by the WebMCP proposal and Chrome shipped its first preview, I tried that too in various demos - for example here . When I heard about the WebMCP hackathon I got really stoked, since I had been wanting to try WebMCP more. I also already had a good demo scenario - at the conference AgentCon Stockholm earlier this year I gave a talk on agentic applications and used a PC part picker to demonstrate how to use AG-UI to share state between an agent and an app (still live here ). It was a good example of an agentic application, but the website had to ship the AI-assistant and foot the inference bill. WebMCP inverts that, which is an interesting idea. The user brings the agent, and the site hands it tools. So we (Andreas Adner and my friends Rafsan Huseynov (in the US) and David Lorenze (in Spain)) expanded upon this idea and built RigBuilder, a PC Builder where an agent can help you out, using WebMCP. What it does RigBuilder is an agent-native PC part picker. It is also a compatibility and constraint solver , making sure that all parts fit together and are within your budget. Your agent (ChatGPT's desktop browser, or Chrome 149+ with an agent) drives the build through 14 WebMCP tools and the agent gets access to validation and a gets a state revision back on every call. For example, ask for "a quiet, compact 1440p gaming PC under $1500" and the agent helps you assemble it. Swap the case by hand and say "fix whatever broke" and the agent reads your change from the shared state. Ask to get under $1400 and the page's optimizer computes the swap plan for the agent to present, and so on and so forth. Ask to see it, and render_build tool produces a brand-free impression of your parts using an OpenAI imagegen model (expect mixed results, it's AI after all ;-) There is a also a second user group - admins. An admin can curate the part catalogue in an WebMCP-enabled admin experience, where he for example can get help from his agent to research a newly launched part on the web and add it to the catalogue with the help of the agent. And RigBuilder is also a complete manual part picker, if you don't have an agent. :-) How we built it React/Vite/TypeScript with Zustand, and a single Cloudflare Worker: D1 for the catalog, R2 for renders and part cards, KV for share links, a Durable Object for the daily render quota, a Rate-Limiting binding for bursts, Turnstile for the render gate, Cloudflare Access to gate access to the admin side. The WebMCP surface was the priority, so it got the most design effort. Every tool returns the same envelope: validated build, conflicts, revision, so the agent never has to guess what changed. Shared state is versioned: a write based on stale knowledge is rejected even if the agent never learned about revisions, because the guard defaults to the revision the agent last saw. Tool descriptions carry untrustedContentHint where web-sourced text enters. The render_build tool sends only part ids and enums; the Worker rebuilds the prompt and cache hash itself and never trusts a client prompt or image. The catalog is 448 hand-authored parts, 82 verified against manufacturer spec pages with recorded sources; every tool result says which is which. Challenges The main challenge was deciding what the WebMCP tools should actually do. We did not want to expose a simple "remote-control" layer that simply mapped tools to normal user interactions - buttons and clicks. The tools needed to give the agent capabilities beyond what a user could accomplish: validating an entire build, identifying conflicts, optimizing against a budget, and returning a complete, structured view of the resulting state. Finding the right level of abstraction was a challenge. Each tool had to be powerful enough to add real value, but focused enough for an agent to understand when and how to use it. Shared state created another WebMCP-specific problem: both the human and the agent can modify the build. The agent might plan its next action from a state that the user has already changed manually. We solved this with versioned state and revision guards that reject stale writes, while still allowing agents to use the tools without explicitly managing revision numbers on every call. From my experiments with AG-UI I have realized that shared state between agent and app is a major unlock when it comes to agentic applications - and WebMCP is really good fit here. Finally, we had to treat the agent as capable but not inherently trusted. Tool inputs are validated by the application, web-sourced catalog text is marked as untrusted, and sensitive operations are reconstructed and checked on the server. WebMCP makes an application dramatically more accessible to agents, but it comes with some new and specific challenges when it comes to security. What we learned WebMCP changes who and what you design for. When the agent becomes the interface, tool descriptions form the foundation of the interaction model. Designing a good agent experience requires the same care as designing a visual one; that care simply moves into the tools, their contracts, and the state they share with the page. This is a pretty interesting shift. It's also an interesting challenge that we can't really make that many assumptions with regard to the agent that is consuming the WebMCP tools. We also learned that the entire “add an AI assistant” architecture can disappear/morph. RigBuilder has no embedded model, inference bill, or chat widget. The user brings the agent, while the application contributes its tools, domain knowledge, and interface. There is no assistant bolted onto the side anymore - also a major shift. What's next Lots of stuff to do. More verified parts, exact cards for the whole catalog, live pricing, and the part I care most about - bringing the same pattern back to the business applications I work with in my day job. Design a home gym with an AI agent. Edit one shared room through WebMCP while the app checks space, access, training goals, and budget. Like Comment Story Updates Inspiration I first tried Codex Modeling Studio and was fascinated by the idea of designing something together with an AI agent in a shared visual workspace. It made me want to explore the same kind of human-agent collaboration in a domain I care about personally. Strength training is my hobby, so I started wondering whether the same approach could help design a home gym around real spatial, training, and budget constraints. That question became Home Gym Creator. What it does Home Gym Creator helps people design a home gym that fits their actual room, training goals, and budget. Users can model the room in 2D or 3D, add furniture, doors, windows, and unavailable areas, then choose equipment from a catalog of 24 fictional products. An AI agent can read and modify the same project through WebMCP. It can configure the room, search the catalog, add and arrange equipment, react to manual changes, and validate the finished layout. Every agent action is immediately visible in the editor and uses the same undo and redo history as manual edits. The application validates the layout by checking collisions, room boundaries, exercise clearance, access paths, ceiling height, wall mounting, training-goal coverage, and total cost. The agent respects these validation results and uses them to improve the design together with the user. How I built it I built Home Gym Creator with Next.js, TypeScript, Zustand, Zod, and React Three Fiber. Manual actions and WebMCP tools use the same project commands, state, validation, and undo history. I used Codex and Cursor throughout development for planning, implementation, debugging, and testing. The app runs entirely in the browser and is deployed on Vercel. Challenges I ran into At first, the agent placed equipment too close to furniture or blocked paths from the door to the wardrobe and bed. I introduced collision detection, equipment and furniture use zones, and access-path validation to model these practical constraints. I did not want placement tools to contain rigid rules that removed the agent’s freedom. Instead, I added a tool that suggests suitable positions before placement and another that validates the complete layout afterward. Modeling realistic combinations was also challenging: for example, a wall-mounted pull-up bar can remain usable above a low object, and people can still walk underneath it. Representing these height-aware rules required careful iteration. Accomplishments that I'm proud of I am proud that the agent can use deterministic tools to check its own work and respect the user's room, budget, and training requirements. Selecting equipment often requires combining several products: for example, a strength setup for squats and bench press may need a rack, bench, barbell, and weight plates. The agent can choose that combination within the available budget, arrange it inside the room, and verify that it fits without collisions or blocked access paths. Making this complete workflow work reliably was one of my biggest accomplishments in the project. What I learned I learned about the current limitations of AI-generated 3D assets. My first attempt was to generate 50 equipment models at once, but it did not produce usable results. Generating one model at a time and improving it iteratively worked much better. I also learned how to guide an agent without adding rigid instructions to every tool. Instead, I gave it dedicated suggestion and validation tools, allowing it to explore solutions, check its own work, and improve the layout based on deterministic feedback. What's next for Home Gym Creator If I continue developing Home Gym Creator, I would add protective floor-mat planning, wall and floor customization, and more equipment options with additional 3D models. I would also expand the concept beyond home gyms toward commercial facilities, supporting multiple rooms and floors, changing rooms, and more complex equipment layouts.
