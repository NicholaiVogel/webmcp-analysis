# FROZEN SCORING RUBRIC (pre-registered before any Stage 1 review)

Scores are integers 1-10 on each of the FOUR official criteria. Every score carries:
rationale, evidence_surfaces, confidence (0-1). Reviewers use the full scale and these
band anchors verbatim. Category neutrality is absolute: a game, art piece, music tool,
CRM, weird toy, and developer tool are all eligible for 10/10 on every criterion if they
satisfy that criterion exceptionally well FOR WHAT THEY ARE TRYING TO BE.
No aesthetic penalties. Signs of hurried AI work matter only as actual product-quality
problems (incoherence, broken flow, placeholder content), never as style.

## WebMCP Leverage — what does WebMCP change for this product?
Judge the delta WebMCP makes to reliability, precision, structured state access,
shared human-agent state, repeatability, and capability. Tool COUNT is not leverage.
- 9-10 TRANSFORMATIVE: the intended experience is impractical without the structured
  agent surface; human and agent share live product state; the collaboration could not
  be replicated comparably well by a general agent driving the UI.
- 7-8 MAJOR DELTA: without WebMCP a general agent is markedly less reliable/precise
  here; the agent is central to the product, not a bolt-on.
- 5-6 MEANINGFUL DELTA: existing capability packaged into a genuinely better product
  experience via structured tools; same outcome otherwise possible with real friction.
- 3-4 MINOR DELTA: generic browser automation achieves comparable outcomes; WebMCP is
  a convenience layer.
- 1-2 COSMETIC: exposes an action a UI button already offers; no meaningful change.

## Execution — is the actual product coherent, intentional, complete for its scope?
Stage 1 scores EXECUTION_CLAIMED: coherence/intentionality/completeness as evidenced by
the packet (description + packaging + video frames/transcript). Thin evidence caps the
score (end-to-end credit requires end-to-end evidence) and lowers confidence.
Stage 2 may rescore as EXECUTION_OBSERVED; latest evidence-backed score replaces, never
sums.
- 9-10: all surfaces agree; central workflow demonstrably completes; states handled;
  feels finished for its stated scope.
- 7-8: strong coherence and intentionality; central workflow appears functional; minor
  unfinished edges.
- 5-6: plausibly real with visible gaps; scope unclear or core not demonstrated
  end-to-end.
- 3-4: thin prototype; contradictions between surfaces; placeholder content.
- 1-2: non-functional or incoherent for its claimed scope.

## Creativity & Ambition — novelty of concept and interaction model
Judge ONLY: novelty of the concept, novelty of the interaction model, originality
relative to existing concepts, ambition, memorable/surprising use of the medium, and
the depth with which the concept is pursued. WebMCP indispensability belongs under
WebMCP Leverage, NOT here: a project can be extremely creative even if another agent
API could theoretically have enabled something similar.
- 9-10: genuinely novel concept or interaction model; ambitious scope pursued deeply
  and coherently; memorable.
- 7-8: fresh synthesis or notably original interaction; ambitious for a hackathon.
- 5-6: competent take on a known category with a real new angle.
- 3-4: derivative; novelty mostly surface details.
- 1-2: clone/template with trivial changes.
"That the category exists" is never a penalty; judge the submitted concept.

## Potential Impact — credible value for the intended audience
Judge: is there a real audience; is there a real problem, desire, or use case for that
audience; does the demonstrated solution genuinely address it. NO commercial adoption,
monetization, market pull, or startup viability is required. Entertainment, play, art,
creative expression, learning, niche hobby use, experimentation, and community value
are legitimate forms of impact. A tiny audience fully justifies a top score.
- 9-10: specific real audience with a real problem/desire; solution demonstrably
  addresses it; credible value, appeal, usefulness, or meaning for that audience.
- 7-8: clear audience and problem, good fit, credible value.
- 5-6: identifiable audience, looser fit or partial address.
- 3-4: speculative audience or strained fit; impact mostly aspirational.
- 1-2: no coherent audience/problem pairing.
Audience size is NOT a multiplier.

## Aggregate & ranking
aggregate = leverage + execution + impact + creativity (4-40). Nothing else enters.
Tie-breaks: Leverage, then Execution, then Impact, then Creativity, then higher
combined evidence confidence, then slug alphabetical. See PROTOCOL.md Ranking section.

Approve-duplicate and reject-duplicate would be meaningless as buttons, but as site tools they give the agent a safe write path with a human approval gate, on the page you are both looking at. Nothing goes live without a yes, and everything rolls back. How i built it WordPress has an Abilities API, and an open-source bridge ( code-atlantic/webmcp-abilities , GPL) that maps abilities to WebMCP tools. Respira registers its production tool catalog as abilities, the bridge exposes them to the page, and a REST layer executes them with WordPress's own authentication and per-tool permission callbacks. The patched, working bridge is open source at respira-press/webmcp-for-wordpress . Challenges i ran into The bridge had never actually worked in a real agent browser, and finding out why became the project. Four bugs, each masking the next: Discovery ran as an anonymous visitor. The tools fetch sent cookies without WordPress's REST nonce: 401, zero tools registered, on every default-configured site. Execution died at WordPress core's door. The bridge put its CSRF token in the header core reserves for its own nonce, so core rejected every call before the handler ran. ChatGPT's modelContext is a frozen object implementing only registerTool . The bridge's batch provideContext call threw silently and nothing registered. Ability names contain a slash that stock Apache rejects when URL-encoded , so execution 404ed on every Apache host. i debugged blind inside ChatGPT's browser with an on-page diagnostic badge: a screenshot from inside the agent browser was the only ground truth. Then one more wall: 296 registered tools tripped ChatGPT's per-page limit and disabled WebMCP for the document entirely. The fix was editorial, not technical: a curated, page-scoped set of ~30 tools. Accomplishments i am proud of As far as i can tell, this is the first WordPress site with working WebMCP site tools in ChatGPT . Every fix shipped to production the same day in Respira for WordPress v8.7.1, so paying customers run this code today. The bridge fixes are reported upstream with a standing PR offer, and the field data from this fleet now feeds WordPress core's agent identity proposal . What i learned The gap between "implements the spec" and "works in a real agent browser" is where all the work lives. And agent browsers impose an attention budget: fewer, better-scoped tools beat an exhaustive catalog. What's next Anonymous visitor tools (public read-only search and product Q&A), per-role tool sets, and landing the bridge fixes upstream so every WordPress site gets a working WebMCP path. Like Comment Story Updates Inspiration Andreas: For the past year I have been interested in one particular question - what does an application look like when its primary user is an agent? And how can humans and agents collaborate? Since the start of last year, I have explored the MCP spec at depth and how it can be used by agents, and chronicled my adventures at my blog ( https://nullpointer.se ). I've been especially interested in "agentic UI", and what the future of applications and UI will look like, as we enter the agentic era. I have spent a lot of time exploring protocols such as AG-UI, A2UI and MCP Apps to try to understand what lies in the future for agentic applications and user interfaces. I am a strong believer in the paradigm of "generative UI", and the agent dynamically presenting just the information and the functionality the user needs - when he or she needs it. My main realization from the last year of experimentation with MCP and adjacant tech is that agentic user interfaces are not only more efficient, they are also more fun to use. In July 2025 I read about MCP-B on Hacker News and the idea that web apps could expose MCP tools directly to an agent running in the browser. Since I was deep into MCP at that time, I was really excited by this and started exploring the spec. Being in the Microsoft space, I did experiments with MCP-B tools running inside a Power App , and when MCP-B was accompanied by the WebMCP proposal and Chrome shipped its first preview, I tried that too in various demos - for example here . When I heard about the WebMCP hackathon I got really stoked, since I had been wanting to try WebMCP more. I also already had a good demo scenario - at the conference AgentCon Stockholm earlier this year I gave a talk on agentic applications and used a PC part picker to demonstrate how to use AG-UI to share state between an agent and an app (still live here ). It was a good example of an agentic application, but the website had to ship the AI-assistant and foot the inference bill. WebMCP inverts that, which is an interesting idea. The user brings the agent, and the site hands it tools. So we (Andreas Adner and my friends Rafsan Huseynov (in the US) and David Lorenze (in Spain)) expanded upon this idea and built RigBuilder, a PC Builder where an agent can help you out, using WebMCP. What it does RigBuilder is an agent-native PC part picker. It is also a compatibility and constraint solver , making sure that all parts fit together and are within your budget. Your agent (ChatGPT's desktop browser, or Chrome 149+ with an agent) drives the build through 14 WebMCP tools and the agent gets access to validation and a gets a state revision back on every call. For example, ask for "a quiet, compact 1440p gaming PC under $1500" and the agent helps you assemble it. Swap the case by hand and say "fix whatever broke" and the agent reads your change from the shared state. Ask to get under $1400 and the page's optimizer computes the swap plan for the agent to present, and so on and so forth. Ask to see it, and render_build tool produces a brand-free impression of your parts using an OpenAI imagegen model (expect mixed results, it's AI after all ;-) There is a also a second user group - admins. An admin can curate the part catalogue in an WebMCP-enabled admin experience, where he for example can get help from his agent to research a newly launched part on the web and add it to the catalogue with the help of the agent. And RigBuilder is also a complete manual part picker, if you don't have an agent. :-) How we built it React/Vite/TypeScript with Zustand, and a single Cloudflare Worker: D1 for the catalog, R2 for renders and part cards, KV for share links, a Durable Object for the daily render quota, a Rate-Limiting binding for bursts, Turnstile for the render gate, Cloudflare Access to gate access to the admin side. The WebMCP surface was the priority, so it got the most design effort. Every tool returns the same envelope: validated build, conflicts, revision, so the agent never has to guess what changed. Shared state is versioned: a write based on stale knowledge is rejected even if the agent never learned about revisions, because the guard defaults to the revision the agent last saw. Tool descriptions carry untrustedContentHint where web-sourced text enters. The render_build tool sends only part ids and enums; the Worker rebuilds the prompt and cache hash itself and never trusts a client prompt or image. The catalog is 448 hand-authored parts, 82 verified against manufacturer spec pages with recorded sources; every tool result says which is which. Challenges The main challenge was deciding what the WebMCP tools should actually do. We did not want to expose a simple "remote-control" layer that simply mapped tools to normal user interactions - buttons and clicks. The tools needed to give the agent capabilities beyond what a user could accomplish: validating an entire build, identifying conflicts, optimizing against a budget, and returning a complete, structured view of the resulting state. Finding the right level of abstraction was a challenge. Each tool had to be powerful enough to add real value, but focused enough for an agent to understand when and how to use it. Shared state created another WebMCP-specific problem: both the human and the agent can modify the build. The agent might plan its next action from a state that the user has already changed manually. We solved this with versioned state and revision guards that reject stale writes, while still allowing agents to use the tools without explicitly managing revision numbers on every call. From my experiments with AG-UI I have realized that shared state between agent and app is a major unlock when it comes to agentic applications - and WebMCP is really good fit here. Finally, we had to treat the agent as capable but not inherently trusted. Tool inputs are validated by the application, web-sourced catalog text is marked as untrusted, and sensitive operations are reconstructed and checked on the server. WebMCP makes an application dramatically more accessible to agents, but it comes with some new and specific challenges when it comes to security. What we learned WebMCP changes who and what you design for. When the agent becomes the interface, tool descriptions form the foundation of the interaction model. Designing a good agent experience requires the same care as designing a visual one; that care simply moves into the tools, their contracts, and the state they share with the page. This is a pretty interesting shift. It's also an interesting challenge that we can't really make that many assumptions with regard to the agent that is consuming the WebMCP tools. We also learned that the entire “add an AI assistant” architecture can disappear/morph. RigBuilder has no embedded model, inference bill, or chat widget. The user brings the agent, while the application contributes its tools, domain knowledge, and interface. There is no assistant bolted onto the side anymore - also a major shift. What's next Lots of stuff to do. More verified parts, exact cards for the whole catalog, live pricing, and the part I care most about - bringing the same pattern back to the business applications I work with in my day job.
