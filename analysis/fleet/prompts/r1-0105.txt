# FROZEN SCORING RUBRIC (pre-registered before any Stage 1 review)

Scores are integers 1-10 on each of the FOUR official criteria. Every score carries:
rationale, evidence_surfaces, confidence (0-1). Reviewers use the full scale and these
band anchors verbatim. Category neutrality is absolute: a game, art piece, music tool,
CRM, weird toy, and developer tool are all eligible for 10/10 on every criterion if they
satisfy that criterion exceptionally well FOR WHAT THEY ARE TRYING TO BE.
No aesthetic penalties. Signs of hurried AI work matter only as actual product-quality
problems (incoherence, broken flow, placeholder content), never as style.

## WebMCP Leverage — what does WebMCP change for this product?
Judge the delta WebMCP makes to reliability, precision, structured state access,
shared human-agent state, repeatability, and capability. Tool COUNT is not leverage.
- 9-10 TRANSFORMATIVE: the intended experience is impractical without the structured
  agent surface; human and agent share live product state; the collaboration could not
  be replicated comparably well by a general agent driving the UI.
- 7-8 MAJOR DELTA: without WebMCP a general agent is markedly less reliable/precise
  here; the agent is central to the product, not a bolt-on.
- 5-6 MEANINGFUL DELTA: existing capability packaged into a genuinely better product
  experience via structured tools; same outcome otherwise possible with real friction.
- 3-4 MINOR DELTA: generic browser automation achieves comparable outcomes; WebMCP is
  a convenience layer.
- 1-2 COSMETIC: exposes an action a UI button already offers; no meaningful change.

## Execution — is the actual product coherent, intentional, complete for its scope?
Stage 1 scores EXECUTION_CLAIMED: coherence/intentionality/completeness as evidenced by
the packet (description + packaging + video frames/transcript). Thin evidence caps the
score (end-to-end credit requires end-to-end evidence) and lowers confidence.
Stage 2 may rescore as EXECUTION_OBSERVED; latest evidence-backed score replaces, never
sums.
- 9-10: all surfaces agree; central workflow demonstrably completes; states handled;
  feels finished for its stated scope.
- 7-8: strong coherence and intentionality; central workflow appears functional; minor
  unfinished edges.
- 5-6: plausibly real with visible gaps; scope unclear or core not demonstrated
  end-to-end.
- 3-4: thin prototype; contradictions between surfaces; placeholder content.
- 1-2: non-functional or incoherent for its claimed scope.

## Creativity & Ambition — novelty of concept and interaction model
Judge ONLY: novelty of the concept, novelty of the interaction model, originality
relative to existing concepts, ambition, memorable/surprising use of the medium, and
the depth with which the concept is pursued. WebMCP indispensability belongs under
WebMCP Leverage, NOT here: a project can be extremely creative even if another agent
API could theoretically have enabled something similar.
- 9-10: genuinely novel concept or interaction model; ambitious scope pursued deeply
  and coherently; memorable.
- 7-8: fresh synthesis or notably original interaction; ambitious for a hackathon.
- 5-6: competent take on a known category with a real new angle.
- 3-4: derivative; novelty mostly surface details.
- 1-2: clone/template with trivial changes.
"That the category exists" is never a penalty; judge the submitted concept.

## Potential Impact — credible value for the intended audience
Judge: is there a real audience; is there a real problem, desire, or use case for that
audience; does the demonstrated solution genuinely address it. NO commercial adoption,
monetization, market pull, or startup viability is required. Entertainment, play, art,
creative expression, learning, niche hobby use, experimentation, and community value
are legitimate forms of impact. A tiny audience fully justifies a top score.
- 9-10: specific real audience with a real problem/desire; solution demonstrably
  addresses it; credible value, appeal, usefulness, or meaning for that audience.
- 7-8: clear audience and problem, good fit, credible value.
- 5-6: identifiable audience, looser fit or partial address.
- 3-4: speculative audience or strained fit; impact mostly aspirational.
- 1-2: no coherent audience/problem pairing.
Audience size is NOT a multiplier.

## Aggregate & ranking
aggregate = leverage + execution + impact + creativity (4-40). Nothing else enters.
Tie-breaks: Leverage, then Execution, then Impact, then Creativity, then higher
combined evidence confidence, then slug alphabetical. See PROTOCOL.md Ranking section.

Like Comment Story Updates Inspiration A friend of mine was organizing a crypto event, and after we got the venue, we were standing in a large empty hall trying to figure out where everything should go. Where should the rooms or different areas be? Where should the furniture go? How much space do we actually have? Most of the planning was happening in our heads and through conversations. That made me think about how we plan physical spaces today. We have AI agents that can reason about complex problems, but they usually cannot directly work with the structured space we are looking at. I wanted to build something where a person could brainstorm with an agent, see ideas take shape directly on the canvas, and have both the human and the agent work on the same space at the same time. That idea became Taxis. What it does Taxis is a spatial planning canvas for designing physical spaces. You can use it to plan a house, office, event, restaurant, exhibition, warehouse, studio, or other physical environments. The key idea is that both the human and the AI agent can work on the same plan. A person can manually create, move, resize, rotate, duplicate, and delete rooms and objects while an agent can perform these same types of spatial operations through WebMCP. The human does not have to wait for the agent to finish before making changes. Both can contribute to the same evolving plan. For example, while an agent is arranging rooms, a person can add doors, adjust objects, or make other changes themselves. We demonstrated this by giving the agent a plot of land and asking it to create a practical bungalow layout with rooms, living spaces, a gaming room, and a garage. The result can then be inspected and edited directly on the canvas. How we built it Taxis is a browser-based spatial planning application built with TypeScript and Vite. The application represents rooms and objects as structured spatial data, including their dimensions, positions, rotations, and other properties. We expose the application's spatial operations as WebMCP tools using document.modelContext.registerTool() . These tools allow an agent to interact directly with the application's structured state, including creating, moving, resizing, rotating, duplicating, and deleting spatial elements. This means the agent is not generating an image of a floor plan. It is directly manipulating the actual objects inside the application. WebMCP provides the interface that allows an external agent to work with the same application state that the human is editing. Challenges we ran into One of the more difficult parts was making resizing and reshaping reliable. At one point, when resizing certain objects, the pointer interaction could incorrectly switch between resize and reshape behavior, causing the cursor to loop between the two interaction modes. We had to refine the interaction logic so that the application could correctly distinguish between resizing an object and reshaping it. Another challenge was making the spatial operations reliable when they are being performed through agent tools. The application has to maintain a consistent structured state while rooms and objects are being created and modified. We also had to make the WebMCP tools clear enough for an agent to understand what operations were available and how they affected the spatial canvas. Accomplishments that we're proud of We built a working spatial planning application where a human and an AI agent can interact with the same structured canvas. We are particularly proud of the fact that the agent is not simply generating a static floor plan. It can directly manipulate the objects inside the application while the human can continue editing the same space. We demonstrated this by using an agent to plan a bungalow on a 20m × 30m plot of land, including bedrooms, living spaces, a gaming room, and a garage. The same underlying system is not restricted to houses. The spatial canvas and its agent tools can be applied to many different types of physical spaces. What we learned We learned that the most interesting part of agent integration is not simply asking an AI to generate something. Giving an agent access to the structured state and actions of an application changes what the agent can actually do. WebMCP allowed us to expose meaningful spatial operations so that an agent could work directly inside the same environment as the human. We also learned that human and agent collaboration does not have to be a handoff where one finishes and the other takes over. Both can contribute to the same evolving workspace. The human can make subjective decisions and adjustments while the agent handles spatial operations and repetitive work. What's next for Taxis The current version is a foundation for a broader spatial planning system. Next, we want to improve the spatial reasoning capabilities of the agent, including better constraint checking, collision detection, capacity planning, and more intelligent arrangements. We also want to expand the types of spaces and objects that can be represented, making Taxis useful for everything from homes and offices to retail spaces, warehouses, exhibitions, studios, and other physical environments. The long-term goal is to make Taxis a general-purpose spatial workspace where people and agents can plan, experiment, and build together. Like Comment Story Updates The problem nobody has budget for Every operations team lives inside sites that will never ship an API for them. Monitoring in Grafana. Customer commitments in Cal.com. Payer portals, vendor consoles, admin dashboards. The person holding the session can see everything and change anything, but their agent can do nothing except screenshot the screen and guess where to click. WebMCP fixes this for sites that adopt it. Today that is roughly none of them. We measured three real open-source products, 522k lines of code, 429 places where a tool obviously belongs, and zero WebMCP registrations. So we asked a different question. What if the user adopts WebMCP for the site? The reframe WebMCP turns the login you already have into an API. A signed-in tab already carries every credential and permission the human has. If that tab registers tools with document.modelContext.registerTool , the agent gets a typed, narrow, inspectable interface to the site, and the vendor never has to ship a line of code. Adoption inverts. It no longer waits on ten thousand vendors. It happens one user, one tab, one reviewed pack at a time. What Agentready Network does Agentready Network is a local-first registry and runtime of reviewed action packs . A pack describes what a site can do, on exactly which origin, with which risk labels. The runtime turns that pack into live WebMCP tools inside the user's own signed-in session, then wraps every call in the safety the vendor never provided: Exact origins. A pack binds to one host and a reviewed fingerprint. Drift fails closed. Declarative, never executable. Manifests are JSON. The registry cannot ship code into your browser. Bounded, untrusted output. Results are allowlist-projected, byte-capped, and labeled contentTrust: "untrusted" . Tool output is content, not instruction. Human authority on consequences. Reads flow. Writes need a preview. Consequential actions need one exact, single-use approval bound to origin, tool, and normalized arguments. Change an argument and the grant is void. Verified closure. After a consequential action an inspector reads authoritative state and issues a receipt with no cookies, secrets, or raw bodies. Unknown outcome is reported as in_doubt , never as success. One concrete job, end to end The demo is not a feature tour. It is one incident. An alert fires in Grafana. Customer bookings in Cal.com are about to be affected. Neither site has WebMCP. The agent discovers the reviewed Grafana and Cal.com packs, installs them for this origin, and reads authoritative state. It never receives a credential. The agent prepares the exact continuity action and states what will change and how it will be inspected. The incident is still investigating . The human grants that exact action once, from a control the agent cannot reach. The agent executes once. The inspector verifies and issues one receipt. The incident is resolved . We poison the fingerprint. The pack refuses to run. We export the recorder draft. Every secret is gone before it can be published. Agents investigate. Humans authorize. Outcomes are verified. Why WebMCP, specifically Screenshot-and-click pays the full cost on every step: a screenshot, a vision pass, a click that misses when the viewport shifts. We measured it. These are single-trial measurements from our audit work, not a benchmark suite, but the shape is unmistakable. The cost moves from per-action to per-app, and it never comes back.
