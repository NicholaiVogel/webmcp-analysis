# FROZEN SCORING RUBRIC (pre-registered before any Stage 1 review)

Scores are integers 1-10 on each of the FOUR official criteria. Every score carries:
rationale, evidence_surfaces, confidence (0-1). Reviewers use the full scale and these
band anchors verbatim. Category neutrality is absolute: a game, art piece, music tool,
CRM, weird toy, and developer tool are all eligible for 10/10 on every criterion if they

No aesthetic penalties. Signs of hurried AI work matter only as actual product-quality
problems (incoherence, broken flow, placeholder content), never as style.

## WebMCP Leverage — what does WebMCP change for this product?
Judge the delta WebMCP makes to reliability, precision, structured state access,
shared human-agent state, repeatability, and capability. Tool COUNT is not leverage.
- 9-10 TRANSFORMATIVE: the intended experience is impractical without the structured
  agent surface; human and agent share live product state; the collaboration could not
  be replicated comparably well by a general agent driving the UI.
- 7-8 MAJOR DELTA: without WebMCP a general agent is markedly less reliable/precise
  here; the agent is central to the product, not a bolt-on.
- 5-6 MEANINGFUL DELTA: existing capability packaged into a genuinely better product
  experience via structured tools; same outcome otherwise possible with real friction.
- 3-4 MINOR DELTA: generic browser automation achieves comparable outcomes; WebMCP is
  a convenience layer.
- 1-2 COSMETIC: exposes an action a UI button already offers; no meaningful change.

## Execution — is the actual product coherent, intentional, complete for its scope?
Stage 1 scores EXECUTION_CLAIMED: coherence/intentionality/completeness as evidenced by
the packet (description + packaging + video frames/transcript). Thin evidence caps the
score (end-to-end credit requires end-to-end evidence) and lowers confidence.
Stage 2 may rescore as EXECUTION_OBSERVED; latest evidence-backed score replaces, never
sums.
- 9-10: all surfaces agree; central workflow demonstrably completes; states handled;
  feels finished for its stated scope.
- 7-8: strong coherence and intentionality; central workflow appears functional; minor
  unfinished edges.
- 5-6: plausibly real with visible gaps; scope unclear or core not demonstrated
  end-to-end.
- 3-4: thin prototype; contradictions between surfaces; placeholder content.
- 1-2: non-functional or incoherent for its claimed scope.

## Creativity & Ambition — novelty of concept and interaction model
Judge ONLY: novelty of the concept, novelty of the interaction model, originality
relative to existing concepts, ambition, memorable/surprising use of the medium, and
the depth with which the concept is pursued. WebMCP indispensability belongs under
WebMCP Leverage, NOT here: a project can be extremely creative even if another agent
API could theoretically have enabled something similar.
- 9-10: genuinely novel concept or interaction model; ambitious scope pursued deeply
  and coherently; memorable.
- 7-8: fresh synthesis or notably original interaction; ambitious for a hackathon.
- 5-6: competent take on a known category with a real new angle.
- 3-4: derivative; novelty mostly surface details.
- 1-2: clone/template with trivial changes.
"That the category exists" is never a penalty; judge the submitted concept.

## Potential Impact — credible value for the intended audience
Judge: is there a real audience; is there a real problem, desire, or use case for that
audience; does the demonstrated solution genuinely address it. NO commercial adoption,
monetization, market pull, or startup viability is required. Entertainment, play, art,
creative expression, learning, niche hobby use, experimentation, and community value
are legitimate forms of impact. A tiny audience fully justifies a top score.
- 9-10: specific real audience with a real problem/desire; solution demonstrably
  addresses it; credible value, appeal, usefulness, or meaning for that audience.
- 7-8: clear audience and problem, good fit, credible value.
- 5-6: identifiable audience, looser fit or partial address.
- 3-4: speculative audience or strained fit; impact mostly aspirational.
- 1-2: no coherent audience/problem pairing.
Audience size is NOT a multiplier.

## Aggregate & ranking
aggregate = leverage + execution + impact + creativity (4-40). Nothing else enters.
Tie-breaks: Leverage, then Execution, then Impact, then Creativity, then higher
combined evidence confidence, then slug alphabetical. See PROTOCOL.md Ranking section.

Don't wait for the web to write tools for agents. Let agents write them, and make that safe to allow. Like Comment Story Updates SAW Bridge Inspiration I kept reading the same sentence in every WebMCP write-up: give agents tools, not DOM. I agreed with it completely. Then I opened a handful of sites I use every day with WebMCP enabled in Chrome, and every single one of them handed the agent an empty document.modelContext . That's the whole problem, right there. The standard is good. The tools don't exist yet, because somebody has to sit down and write them for every site, one at a time. So agents are back to guessing from screenshots on 99% of the web while we wait. I got stuck on a question: why does registerTool exist as a runtime API at all? If tools were meant to be a fixed list, you'd declare them once at load. But WebMCP lets you register at any moment, and it ships a toolchange event so listeners find out when the surface changes. Most people use that for SPA routing. I wanted to see what happens if you take it literally. What if the agent registers the tools? What it does SAW-Bridge gives a page three meta-tools instead of a catalogue: saw_inspect_page_scope — look at the page and report what it actually exposes saw_suggest_and_register_tool — design a schema and register a real, lasting WebMCP tool saw_execute_code_mode — write code and run it once, for one-off work The demo is a dashboard of world happiness and AI readiness data. It has a filter chip for happiness above 7.5. It deliberately has no chip for 7.4, and no tool for it either. So I asked ChatGPT's in-app browser for 7.4. It listed the page's tools, called saw_inspect_page_scope , found the site's own filterByMinHappiness function, and then it stopped and asked me before touching anything. I said yes. The table dropped from twelve rows to four — Finland 7.82, Denmark 7.64, Iceland 7.56, Netherlands 7.42. All above 7.4, and the chart re-rendered through the app's own code path, not a simulated click on something that looked clickable. The part I didn't expect is that the agent chose between the two strategies. For a one-off threshold it wrote code and ran it once, which is the cheaper call. Ask for something reusable and it takes the other path and registers a lasting tool — and then Chrome's own DevTools WebMCP panel lists that tool and will invoke it with your parameters, because by that point it's an ordinary WebMCP tool. It isn't picking between one blessed tool and scraping the screen. It's deciding how permanent its new capability should be. Every mutating call goes through an approval dialog first, showing the exact tool and the exact arguments. There's a live audit drawer so you can watch what was discovered, assembled, approved and refused, and why. There are two delivery surfaces sharing one engine: a single-file SDK a site drops in, and a Chrome extension that injects the same thing into sites that ship nothing. How we built it The core is plain WebMCP. Tools register through document.modelContext.registerTool() . Consumers discover with getTools() and invoke with executeTool() . Registration takes an AbortSignal , so aborting genuinely unregisters. Assembled tools get untrustedContentHint: true ; read-only ones get readOnlyHint: true . Two pieces took actual thinking. Finding what a page exposes. The interesting stuff on a page is buried among roughly a thousand standard browser globals, and a hand-written denylist rots the moment Chrome ships a new API. What worked: create a hidden iframe, read Object.getOwnPropertyNames off its contentWindow to get a reference set of standard globals from the browser itself , then diff the real window against it. Whatever's left is the page's own surface, and it stays correct as browsers change. Then rank candidates before truncating — an object with callable methods outranks a bare object, which outranks a loose function. I learned that one the hard way, see below. Compiling what the agent writes. This lands on the same insight as Cloudflare's Code Mode : models handle capability better when they can write code against an API than when they pick from a long list of tool definitions. I want to be precise about the relationship — I did not use @cloudflare/codemode . Cloudflare runs model-written code in a server-side Worker isolate. This runs it in the page's own realm, and that difference is the reason the project exists. The currently applied filter, the selected row, the chart instance holding the data — none of that exists outside the page. A sandbox elsewhere can't touch what the user is actually looking at without replicating the whole session. The token math lands in the same place by a different road. Code Mode collapses N tool definitions into one code tool. Here there's no catalogue to collapse: three meta-tools, and concrete tools show up only when a task calls for one. That's progressive disclosure, which MCP's own client guidance recommends when definitions start eating context — except the tools being disclosed don't have to exist yet. The whole thing threw on the last line, so the three meta-tools never registered — but the polyfill and the global had already been assigned, so the page looked healthy and every button failed silently. I only caught it by building a jsdom harness that clicked the actual buttons on the actual HTML. My REPL couldn't run its own demo. The code path wrapped snippets in return (...) , which only accepts a single expression. My own demo passed a statement body. It threw a SyntaxError every time. Now it tries the expression form, falls back to a statement body, and compiles async so snippets can await . executeTool doesn't take a tool name. The spec says it takes a RegisteredTool object from getTools() and resolves with the stringified result. I'd written it to accept a string and return a plain object. Against my own polyfill it worked perfectly. In a browser with native WebMCP it would have rejected on the first call — the exact environment judges test in. That was a genuinely unnerving thing to find. It worked from file:// and broke as soon as I deployed. WebMCP needs an origin-keyed agent cluster, which means an Origin-Agent-Cluster: ?1 response header. file:// is exempt, and localhost is a secure context but is not origin-keyed, so this fails only after hosting, with a SecurityError and no hint. Both runtimes now check at startup and name what's missing instead of letting you guess. The security dialog could be hijacked by the thing it was guarding against. This one bothered me most. When an agent authors tools, tool metadata becomes untrusted input, and I'd built the approval dialog with innerHTML . A payload like x</pre><img src=y onerror=...> renders live markup inside the security gate and can click its own Approve button. It turns research and live market odds into a shared human-agent workspace for taking trades and preparing decisions with people in final control Like Comment Story Updates What Knoww does Knoww turns live prediction markets into a shared research workspace where people and agents can explore market information together. Knoww gives the agent structured access to market discovery and analysis without giving it control of a wallet, a signature, or trade submission. The problem Prediction market research involves more than opening a result page. A single event may contain several related markets, outcomes, probabilities, charts, and order books. The useful context also depends on the filters, selected market, and chart range currently visible on the page. A browser agent can attempt to reconstruct this workflow through page inspection and clicks, but it must infer the meaning of interface elements and continuously track changing page state. That process can be fragile. WebMCP lets Knoww expose the meaning behind the interface directly. Instead of guessing which card, control, or outcome represents the user's intent, the agent receives narrow and typed operations connected to the current page. Why WebMCP is the right fit A separate market data integration could return probabilities and market information, but it would not naturally share the interface state the person is currently inspecting. WebMCP allows the person and the agent to work with the same filters, selected market, order book, and chart range. When the agent changes something, the person sees the result immediately through the normal Knoww interface. This creates one continuous research workflow. The agent can discover relevant events, narrow the market list, open a valid result, compare related markets, inspect the selected order book, adjust the chart range, and prepare the trade interface for review. Every action remains visible on the page. The person can verify the result, correct the agent, or stop the workflow at any point. What I built I added 12 WebMCP tools across the market discovery page and individual event pages. On /markets , get_markets_page_context reads the current page state. search_events searches public events. set_market_filters changes only supported visible filters. load_more_markets advances the visible results. open_event can open only an event already returned by Knoww. On an event page, get_current_event_context reads the active event and current selection. find_markets_on_page finds relevant markets within the event. compare_markets returns a focused comparison. get_selected_order_book reads the currently selected order book. select_market_view changes the visible market. set_chart_range changes the visible chart period. prepare_trade prepares the trade interface for the person to review. The available tools change with the page context. The market discovery page exposes discovery and navigation capabilities. An event page exposes comparison, order book, chart, and trade preparation capabilities. This keeps the tool set focused while supporting a complete workflow from market discovery to decision preparation. Example experience A person can ask: "Find Bitcoin related prediction markets, narrow the list, open the most relevant event, compare its markets, inspect the selected order book, and prepare the trade view for my review." The agent can complete this workflow through structured WebMCP tools while the person watches the same page change and remains free to correct any step. This is more reliable than asking the agent to infer the entire workflow from buttons, cards, and page layout. Human control and safety The WebMCP tools cannot connect a wallet, sign a transaction, or submit a trade. prepare_trade only prepares the visible trade interface. The person must inspect the values and complete any action with financial consequences. Every tool uses a typed and bounded input schema. Search limits, filter values, pagination, and navigation targets are constrained. open_event accepts only events already returned by Knoww. Tool results are marked as untrusted public content. These boundaries give the agent enough capability to provide meaningful assistance while preserving human control. Technical implementation Knoww uses Next.js, React, and TypeScript, and is deployed with Cloudflare Workers. It reads public Polymarket market data. I connected the WebMCP handlers to Knoww's existing React state and page actions instead of recreating the market logic in a separate integration. This means the person and the agent work with the same filters, selected market, order book, and chart range. When a tool changes the page state, the normal Knoww interface reflects that change. Tool registration runs through top level page JavaScript so the tools can be discovered by ChatGPT's in app browser. I also added registration cleanup so a normal React unmount does not incorrectly report a registration failure. Testing I tested the production /markets page in ChatGPT's in app browser. ChatGPT discovered all five tools registered on that page. I successfully executed get_markets_page_context and used search_events to find Bitcoin related events on the live production site. It was deciding exactly which control the agent should receive. The WebMCP tools had to stay synchronized with React state while maintaining clear boundaries. Search limits, filter values, pagination, navigation targets, and available page actions all needed to be constrained. The page lifecycle also required careful handling so route changes and normal React cleanup did not appear as tool registration failures. The most important lesson was that WebMCP is strongest when it reveals the semantics of an existing interface without bypassing that interface or its permission model. Knoww now gives an agent enough structure to help someone reason about live prediction markets, while stopping exactly where financial authority should remain with the person. ABOUT TEXT (untrusted evidence):
A human and an AI drawing on the same canvas — and the page can say no. Like Comment Story Updates Inspiration Most WebMCP demos hand an agent tools that read the page. I wanted to hand it tools that touch something a person is touching at the same time, and see what breaks. Everything broke immediately, and in an interesting way. In the first experiment a human drew seven strokes across the agent's work-in-progress. The agent never noticed. Not a bug — WebMCP is pull-only. A page cannot speak to its agent. It has no notify , no event, no way up the pipe. It can only answer when it is called. So the question became: with only that one channel, how much control can a page take back? What it does The page gives an agent nine drawing tools. Ask it for a lion; it draws one. Then pick up the mouse and draw two wheels underneath it while it works. 19:18:40 You Drew a line (9 points) ← you barge in mid-batch … You × 7 more 19:18:48 AI ⛔ Blocked canvas_batch — canvas changed by human 19:18:48 AI Inspected the updated canvas 19:19:02 AI Updated the plan — A cute smiling male lion car, using the completed red user-drawn wheels and connecting them to the mane-body with a simple axle. 19:19:12 AI Drew 39 shapes in one batch Its half-finished mane stops where it stopped, because the rest of that batch was dropped . It cannot draw again until it has looked, and then said what your wheels changed about the plan — and what it is giving up because of them. In this run it said, unprompted: "Both red wheels now have hubs, inner rims, and tread marks. I'll keep those wheel details untouched and add only the axle connection." Then it drew the axle. Not a lion that happens to still have wheels beside it — a lion joined to your wheels by a bar it decided to add. About sixty seconds, start to finish, with nothing scripted anywhere. Every one of those decisions came out of the agent reading tool output. The drawing is deliberately crude. An image model would draw a better lion. The log is the deliverable. How I built it First, a word on what this is not . WebMCP already has a human-in-the-loop hook — requestUserInteraction , which lets a tool pause and ask the person to confirm. That is agent-initiated : the agent decides to ask, the person waits in front of a dialog, and says yes or no to something already drafted. It is consent. This is the opposite shape. It is human-initiated : the person acts whenever they like, without asking, and the agent finds out afterwards that it has been overruled. It is pre-emption. Both belong in a page people and agents share, and the spec currently offers only the first. Someone who is making something does not want to approve the agent's plan — they want to reach in and change it, and have the agent catch up. Then four rules, each one arrived at by watching the previous version fail. 1 — Refuse, don't stop. A page cannot interrupt an agent. But an agent calls a tool every few seconds, so refusing the next call feels, to the person watching, like an instant stop. Every writing tool calls guard() first. 2 — If you cannot push, answer when you are pulled. Everything the page needs to say rides back on the tool the agent already calls. canvas_describe always appends what the human did since the last check. It is the only channel WebMCP offers, so the page uses it as one. 3 — Seeing is not accepting. The first version asked , in prose, for the agent to re-plan around the interruption. It obliged — narrated the change beautifully, then carried on drawing its original picture. So looking stopped being enough: canvas_describe now hands over to a second gate, and only canvas_replan(observed, new_plan, abandoning) opens it. All three fields are required. abandoning is the load-bearing one — a plan you have given nothing up for is the old plan wearing a new sentence. 4 — The human's marks are never painted over. Told to incorporate the wheels, the agent laid its mane straight over them. Nothing was deleted and the counts were honest, but half the wheels vanished from the picture, and the picture is what people read. Paint order is now fixed: whatever the agent draws goes underneath. Challenges I ran into Requests land; prohibitions do not. "Check the canvas before you draw" was ignored, however it was worded. Refusing the call was not. Every rule here is enforced by a code path, not by asking nicely. Batching fixed the speed and broke the premise. A realistic lion took six minutes — the agent called canvas_stroke with three points every four seconds. Adding canvas_batch cut it to one call . The next tester's report was one sentence: "It drew everything at once, so I had no time to barge in." Painting the batch slowly to look interruptible would have been a lie — the picture was already decided. So a batch is now accepted and laid down one shape at a time over a few seconds, and if the human draws during that, the rest is dropped and never happens. Batching gained an honest price: bigger is faster, and bigger loses more. The agent answers in the conversation's language. Asked in Japanese, it wrote its new plan in Japanese, onto an English canvas. Fixed by asking for English in three places — the tool description, each parameter's description, and the return value read immediately before the call. One place was not enough. Accomplishments I'm proud of The measured finding that surprised me most has nothing to do with drawing: A tool's description changes how an agent batches its work. With a neutral description, the agent drew a spiral as 2 points per call, ~40 calls, over a minute. After adding "Pass every point in one call. Each call costs a few seconds, so the number of calls is the wait the user sees" — 121 points in a single call, in 4 seconds. Same tool, same model, 15× fewer round trips, from one paragraph of prose. What I learned That a page under WebMCP is not a passive surface. It is the only party in the room that knows what the person just did, and the return value of a tool is a real communication channel — one the agent reads carefully and acts on. Almost all the design work turned out to be writing. What's next The mechanism is now a standalone file, barge-in.js , that knows nothing about drawing. It knows only who touched the page last, and it is four lines to add to a page: const bi = BargeIn.create({ describeTool: 'doc_read', replanTool: 'doc_replan' }); editor.addEventListener('input', () => { bi.note('you', 'edited a paragraph'); // look() reports this bi.humanActed(); // this refuses the agent }); function doc_insert(p) { bi.guard(); /* … */ } // guard every write function doc_read() { return Object.assign({ text: editor.value }, bi.look()); } The first application already runs, as a second page in the same repo: examples/doc.html , a document a person and an agent write at the same time — six tools, the same barge-in.js behind them, about 250 lines of page on top. Rule 4 changes shape there, and that is the interesting part. On the canvas it means the agent's paint goes underneath. In a document it means a paragraph you have typed in is yours : ⛔ That paragraph belongs to the user — they typed in it. It can never be rewritten or deleted. Write around it: use doc_insert to add a paragraph before or after, and leave theirs exactly as it is. The agent has to compose around you — and on the first live attempt it went further than the rule required: "You replaced paragraph 2 during the edit, so it now belongs to you and must remain unchanged. It restructured the document around it : the one paragraph it was forbidden to touch became the thing the other two were rewritten to support. Have you ever wanted to play a collaborative video game but your friends aren't available? Meet Storyforge — a fantasy rpg game where humans and AI agent (using WebMCP) collaborate on fantasy quests. Like Comment Story Updates Inspiration Most game companions feel like scripted followers, while AI assistants usually live in a separate chat window. We wanted to explore what happens when an agent becomes a genuine party member—one that understands the battlefield, remembers the journey, offers useful advice, and acts through the same rules as the player. Storyforge grew from the idea that AI should not replace the player or secretly rewrite the game. It should make the adventure feel more social, strategic, and alive. What it does Storyforge is a fantasy action RPG in which the player becomes the Last Heir and travels across five corrupted realms to recover the Eclipse Seals. The player fights enemies, completes realm-specific objectives, collects weapons, recruits companions, and confronts multi-phase bosses. Each companion has a distinct personality, ability, tactical role, trust level, and independent motivation. Through WebMCP, an external agent can: Inspect the live battlefield and fellowship state Understand health, enemies, inventory, objectives, pressure mechanics, and boss phases Explain the next valid quest action Recommend companion abilities and battle strategies Issue reversible tactical commands Propose coordinated party plans for the player to approve Agent actions appear in a visible activity feed, keeping the collaboration understandable and accountable. How we built it We built Storyforge with React, TypeScript, Vite, Zustand, and a deterministic TypeScript game engine. The WorldEngine is the canonical source of truth for combat, quests, inventory, companions, relationships, and campaign progression. Both the React interface and WebMCP tools operate on this same state. The game registers typed, JSON-schema-based WebMCP tools through document.modelContext or navigator.modelContext . Read tools expose purpose-built views of the battlefield and fellowship. Action tools pass validated commands into the engine rather than manipulating the interface or inventing results. Consequential agent actions create visible proposals that the player can accept or reject. Activity is persisted locally, and the project includes automated tests for combat, quest progression, companion behavior, and WebMCP integration. Challenges we ran into The hardest challenge was making agents useful without allowing them to bypass the game. Early versions made companions feel cosmetic because the player could complete most objectives alone. We redesigned the campaign around companion-specific abilities, recruitment trials, trust, pressure mechanics, coordinated attacks, and independent initiatives. We also had to keep agent reasoning grounded. Instead of asking an agent to interpret pixels, we exposed structured battlefield state and enforced every action through the game engine. Another challenge was browser compatibility. WebMCP, browser speech synthesis, microphone access, and deployment environments expose different capabilities. We added feature detection, captions, typed-command fallbacks, visible status indicators, and a Chrome Origin Trial token. Finally, presenting a large game world alongside combat information, party controls, dialogue, recommendations, and agent activity required several UI redesigns to prevent overlapping panels. Accomplishments that we're proud of We are proud that WebMCP is part of the actual gameplay rather than a decorative chatbot. The agent sees the same canonical state that drives combat and can affect the encounter through real, constrained tools. At the same time, the player retains authority over movement, major plans, and consequential changes. We are also proud of the fellowship system. Companions persist across realms, fight alongside the player, develop trust, unlock combinations, offer contextual advice, and sometimes leave to pursue opportunities based on their own priorities. The result is a complete five-stage campaign with distinct environments, collectible equipment, recruitment challenges, escalating realm mechanics, multi-phase bosses, persistent progression, remappable controls, and a visible human-agent collaboration history. What we learned We learned that believable agentic gameplay depends more on constraints and consequences than unlimited freedom. Agents feel intelligent when they receive accurate context, understand what is currently possible, and make decisions that visibly affect the world. Giving an agent unrestricted control would have made the game less coherent and less enjoyable. We also learned that transparency improves trust. Showing tool calls, recommendations, proposals, and outcomes makes the agent feel like a teammate whose reasoning can be understood—not an invisible system changing the game behind the scenes. Most importantly, AI companions become compelling when they have specialized capabilities, incomplete information, relationships, and motivations of their own. What's next for Storyforge Next, we want to deepen the social and strategic side of the fellowship: Dynamic voice generation with a distinct voice for every companion and boss Longer companion memory and relationships shaped by player decisions Procedurally generated quests grounded in persistent world rules More companion disagreements, personal missions, and conflicting priorities Drop-in cooperative play between human and agent-controlled party members Additional realms, weapons, enemies, and branching endings Improved tactical planning with environmental interactions and party formations Shareable campaign histories that preserve every important human-agent decision Our long-term goal is for every Storyforge adventure to feel like playing with a group of friends who understand the world, surprise you, and help create a story none of you could have produced alone.
