=== RUBRIC (frozen) ===
# FROZEN SCORING RUBRIC (pre-registered before any Stage 1 review)

Scores are integers 1-10 on each of the FOUR official criteria. Every score carries:
rationale, evidence_surfaces, confidence (0-1). Reviewers use the full scale and these
band anchors verbatim. Category neutrality is absolute: a game, art piece, music tool,
CRM, weird toy, and developer tool are all eligible for 10/10 on every criterion if they
satisfy that criterion exceptionally well FOR WHAT THEY ARE TRYING TO BE.
No aesthetic penalties. Signs of hurried AI work matter only as actual product-quality
problems (incoherence, broken flow, placeholder content), never as style.

## WebMCP Leverage — what does WebMCP change for this product?
Judge the delta WebMCP makes to reliability, precision, structured state access,
shared human-agent state, repeatability, and capability. Tool COUNT is not leverage.
- 9-10 TRANSFORMATIVE: the intended experience is impractical without the structured
  agent surface; human and agent share live product state; the collaboration could not
  be replicated comparably well by a general agent driving the UI.
- 7-8 MAJOR DELTA: without WebMCP a general agent is markedly less reliable/precise
  here; the agent is central to the product, not a bolt-on.
- 5-6 MEANINGFUL DELTA: existing capability packaged into a genuinely better product
  experience via structured tools; same outcome otherwise possible with real friction.
- 3-4 MINOR DELTA: generic browser automation achieves comparable outcomes; WebMCP is
  a convenience layer.
- 1-2 COSMETIC: exposes an action a UI button already offers; no meaningful change.

## Execution — is the actual product coherent, intentional, complete for its scope?
Stage 1 scores EXECUTION_CLAIMED: coherence/intentionality/completeness as evidenced by
the packet (description + packaging + video frames/transcript). Thin evidence caps the
score (end-to-end credit requires end-to-end evidence) and lowers confidence.
Stage 2 may rescore as EXECUTION_OBSERVED; latest evidence-backed score replaces, never
sums.
- 9-10: all surfaces agree; central workflow demonstrably completes; states handled;
  feels finished for its stated scope.
- 7-8: strong coherence and intentionality; central workflow appears functional; minor
  unfinished edges.
- 5-6: plausibly real with visible gaps; scope unclear or core not demonstrated
  end-to-end.
- 3-4: thin prototype; contradictions between surfaces; placeholder content.
- 1-2: non-functional or incoherent for its claimed scope.

## Creativity & Ambition — novelty of concept and interaction model
Judge ONLY: novelty of the concept, novelty of the interaction model, originality
relative to existing concepts, ambition, memorable/surprising use of the medium, and
the depth with which the concept is pursued. WebMCP indispensability belongs under
WebMCP Leverage, NOT here: a project can be extremely creative even if another agent
API could theoretically have enabled something similar.
- 9-10: genuinely novel concept or interaction model; ambitious scope pursued deeply
  and coherently; memorable.
- 7-8: fresh synthesis or notably original interaction; ambitious for a hackathon.
- 5-6: competent take on a known category with a real new angle.
- 3-4: derivative; novelty mostly surface details.
- 1-2: clone/template with trivial changes.
"That the category exists" is never a penalty; judge the submitted concept.

## Potential Impact — credible value for the intended audience
Judge: is there a real audience; is there a real problem, desire, or use case for that
audience; does the demonstrated solution genuinely address it. NO commercial adoption,
monetization, market pull, or startup viability is required. Entertainment, play, art,
creative expression, learning, niche hobby use, experimentation, and community value
are legitimate forms of impact. A tiny audience fully justifies a top score.
- 9-10: specific real audience with a real problem/desire; solution demonstrably
  addresses it; credible value, appeal, usefulness, or meaning for that audience.
- 7-8: clear audience and problem, good fit, credible value.
- 5-6: identifiable audience, looser fit or partial address.
- 3-4: speculative audience or strained fit; impact mostly aspirational.
- 1-2: no coherent audience/problem pairing.
Audience size is NOT a multiplier.

## Aggregate & ranking
aggregate = leverage + execution + impact + creativity (4-40). Nothing else enters.
Tie-breaks: Leverage, then Execution, then Impact, then Creativity, then higher
combined evidence confidence, then slug alphabetical. See PROTOCOL.md Ranking section.

Like Comment Story Updates Inspiration Southern Horticulture is a garden center in St. Augustine, Florida, half a mile from salt spray. The staff spend most of the day answering one question in different clothes: "will this plant live in my yard?" The honest answer depends on light, salt exposure, irrigation, whether a deer eats the leaves, and how much the person actually wants to water. A website cannot ask all of that, so every plant catalog on the internet shows the same grid to everyone and lets the shopper guess. Agents changed what is possible. A shopper will happily tell an assistant about the dog and the sprinkler that broke last year, because it is a conversation rather than a form with nineteen fields. WebMCP was the missing half: a way for that conversation to reach into the page the shopper is looking at, instead of ending in a wall of text. What it does The Living Garden Center is a plant catalog that rebuilds itself around one specific yard or purpose. You tell your agent what the space is really like. It calls describe_garden, and the page next to you recomposes: a verified pet friendly section rises to the top because a dog shares the yard, salt-tolerant privacy plants group together, and other plants with no verified toxicity record move into a section that says plainly that a missing record is not evidence of safety. Every plant carries a verdict from a deterministic evaluator, from excellent down to insufficient data, and every verdict is explained. Tap a plant and you get why it landed there: the strengths, the compromises, and the care routine it actually asks for, on dials rather than in adjectives. Some plants get an honest no, with a "what would need to change" answer that names the single facet that would flip the verdict. The agent and the shopper share one live page and one context. Say the salt is worse than you first thought, and the sections reshuffle in front of you. Ask why bougainvillea is a compromise, and the agent answers from the same evaluation the page is showing. Add plants to a saved list, then take a link with you. The catalog never invents stock. Availability language comes from one recorded statement, and the tools say when we have no record rather than guessing. How we built it The core is a pure, deterministic evaluator. A GardenContext carries site conditions, household, and preferences, along with an assumption ledger that marks every value as known, derived, assumed, or unknown. Evaluating a plant against that context produces a fit with strengths, compromises, blockers, and unknowns. A rule table composes sections from those fits, and a page manifest renders through a fixed component registry. No language model writes copy at runtime, which is why the page recomposes instantly and says the same thing twice. WebMCP sits on top as a thin layer. Registration checks for the API and registers every tool once at load: document.modelContext.registerTool({ name: "describe_garden", description: "Interpret the shopper's words into structured garden conditions", inputSchema: { /* ... / }, execute: async (input) => { / ... */ } }); Eighteen tools in the public app, eleven read and seven write, each with explicit safety annotations so the browser can show which ones make changes and which three are destructive. Writes go through the same validation the human form uses. Context updates take a revision number and fail loudly on conflict, so the agent and the shopper cannot silently overwrite each other. Two details made it robust. The tools return a share URL that encodes the yard, so an agent working in a browser you cannot see can hand you a link that opens the same personalized page anywhere. And an agent activity panel logs every tool call beside the page, so a human can see exactly what the agent did and undo it. The app is Next.js 16 on Vercel. The repo ships a 60-plant sample catalog and reads a fuller catalog from an environment variable, so the demo is complete without publishing our whole inventory. Challenges we ran into The hardest problem was not the code. It was discovering where site tools actually run. Our first test failed because ChatGPT used its remote agent browser, so the tools executed in a browser we could not see and the visible tab never changed. Then Chrome reported no tools at all, which turned out to be the WebMCP flag being off. Then a regular ChatGPT chat said it could use the page and could not. Site tools live in the desktop app's built in browser under only a Local ChatGPT Work session, on GPT-5.6 Sol or Terra. Nothing on our page could fix any of that, so we made the page say it: a status chip that reads either "Site tools ready: 18 tools this agent can call" or a one line explanation of what to change. Chrome 151 has no way to unregister a tool, and registering a duplicate name is rejected. So tools register exactly once and read live state at execute time rather than closing over it. The subtler challenge was honesty. It is easy to build a recommender that sounds confident. We had to write an explicit contract: an unknown never becomes a negative, a missing toxicity record is never reported as safe, and no tool ever states a live stock count. That constraint produced the most distinctive part of the product. Accomplishments that we're proud of The page genuinely rebuilds itself around a conversation, and it does so from a deterministic evaluator rather than a generated layout, so it is fast, repeatable, and auditable. The honest no. Most catalogs cannot afford to say "we would skip this one for your yard." Ours does, with the reason and the condition that would change it. A garden center that tells you not to buy something is the one you come back to. The evidence gap. We surface the count of plants with no verified pet safety record and keep them visibly uncertain instead of quietly filtering them out or, worse, implying they are fine. Reaching parity between the human and the agent. Same page, same context object, same validation, revision checked so neither one clobbers the other, and a visible log of what the agent touched. And it works. In the ChatGPT desktop app under a local Work session, the arrow turns blue and the catalog reorganizes while you watch. What we learned Tools should carry judgment, not just data. A search_plants that returns rows makes the agent guess. A tool that returns a verdict plus the reasons behind it lets the agent explain, and the explanation matches what the human sees on screen. Annotations matter more than they look. Before we marked the write tools explicitly, the browser listed sixteen read and zero write tools, and a client has no good way to reason about a tool it cannot classify. Shared state needs concurrency control. Two actors editing one context is a distributed systems problem in miniature, and revision checking with an explicit conflict result solved it in about thirty lines. The failure mode nobody warns you about is invisibility. A page that offers tools looks identical to a page that does not when the browser lacks the API. Telling the user which one they are looking at was one of the highest value changes we made all day. What's next for Garden Center Web-MCP Real availability. We have a specification for inventory evidence flowing from the point of sale into a per variation availability state, which would let the agent answer "ten Podocarpus in seven gallon and two fifteen gallon Ligustrum, can you hold them" with a number instead of a hedge. The honesty contract already routes every availability sentence through one function, so this lands without touching a single tool. Filling the unknowns.
