## WebMCP Leverage — what does WebMCP change for this product?
Judge the delta WebMCP makes to reliability, precision, structured state access,
shared human-agent state, repeatability, and capability. Tool COUNT is not leverage.
- 9-10 TRANSFORMATIVE: the intended experience is impractical without the structured
  agent surface; human and agent share live product state; the collaboration could not
  be replicated comparably well by a general agent driving the UI.
- 7-8 MAJOR DELTA: without WebMCP a general agent is markedly less reliable/precise
  here; the agent is central to the product, not a bolt-on.
- 5-6 MEANINGFUL DELTA: existing capability packaged into a genuinely better product
  experience via structured tools; same outcome otherwise possible with real friction.
- 3-4 MINOR DELTA: generic browser automation achieves comparable outcomes; WebMCP is
  a convenience layer.
- 1-2 COSMETIC: exposes an action a UI button already offers; no meaningful change.

## Execution — is the actual product coherent, intentional, complete for its scope?
Stage 1 scores EXECUTION_CLAIMED: coherence/intentionality/completeness as evidenced by
the packet (description + packaging + video frames/transcript). Thin evidence caps the
score (end-to-end credit requires end-to-end evidence) and lowers confidence.
Stage 2 may rescore as EXECUTION_OBSERVED; latest evidence-backed score replaces, never
sums.
- 9-10: all surfaces agree; central workflow demonstrably completes; states handled;
  feels finished for its stated scope.
- 7-8: strong coherence and intentionality; central workflow appears functional; minor
  unfinished edges.
- 5-6: plausibly real with visible gaps; scope unclear or core not demonstrated
  end-to-end.
- 3-4: thin prototype; contradictions between surfaces; placeholder content.
- 1-2: non-functional or incoherent for its claimed scope.

## Creativity & Ambition — novelty of concept and interaction model
Judge ONLY: novelty of the concept, novelty of the interaction model, originality
relative to existing concepts, ambition, memorable/surprising use of the medium, and
the depth with which the concept is pursued. WebMCP indispensability belongs under
WebMCP Leverage, NOT here: a project can be extremely creative even if another agent
API could theoretically have enabled something similar.
- 9-10: genuinely novel concept or interaction model; ambitious scope pursued deeply
  and coherently; memorable.
- 7-8: fresh synthesis or notably original interaction; ambitious for a hackathon.
- 5-6: competent take on a known category with a real new angle.
- 3-4: derivative; novelty mostly surface details.
- 1-2: clone/template with trivial changes.
"That the category exists" is never a penalty; judge the submitted concept.

## Potential Impact — credible value for the intended audience
Judge: is there a real audience; is there a real problem, desire, or use case for that
audience; does the demonstrated solution genuinely address it. NO commercial adoption,
monetization, market pull, or startup viability is required. Entertainment, play, art,
creative expression, learning, niche hobby use, experimentation, and community value
are legitimate forms of impact. A tiny audience fully justifies a top score.
- 9-10: specific real audience with a real problem/desire; solution demonstrably

- 7-8: clear audience and problem, good fit, credible value.
- 5-6: identifiable audience, looser fit or partial address.
- 3-4: speculative audience or strained fit; impact mostly aspirational.
- 1-2: no coherent audience/problem pairing.
Audience size is NOT a multiplier.

aggregate = leverage + execution + impact + creativity (4-40). Nothing else enters.

An hour of the two of us talking turns into a long evening of cleanup: the false starts, the "hold on, let me say that again", the twenty seconds where one of us is looking up a paper. Every podcast we know that quietly stopped after four episodes stopped for that reason. So the podcast went on hold and we built the tool first. A slightly ridiculous order of operations, but here we are. What it does Werkstern records each person's voice locally and uncompressed. While you're talking, you tag things with a single keypress: retake, error, good point, chapter, source. Those tags are the whole idea. The tedious part of editing gets done while you're still in the conversation, by the person who just heard what went wrong. Afterwards, ChatGPT reads the finished take through WebMCP and suggests specific regions to remove, in seconds. The app lists them for you, you keep or drop each one, and only then does anything get cut. You get an audible preview before you commit and an undo that puts everything back. The original recordings stay untouched, which we verify byte for byte in the test suite. Why we used MCP We started from a constraint. We're recording medical conversations, including half-formed thoughts and things one of us says and then takes back. That audio stays on the machine, so any design that began with uploading the file was ruled out early. The second thing that shaped it was accuracy. In a conversation about medicine the qualifier is often the load-bearing part of the sentence. "This helps in a narrow group of patients" and "this helps" are two different claims, and only one of them is true. We needed to see every proposed cut before it happened. WebMCP fit both of those. The model never receives audio. It receives a deliberately thin description of the take: markers, director signals and pause windows, all in seconds. No names, no device labels, no file paths, no tokens. It reasons over that and hands back numbers, and the app does the actual cutting locally with FFmpeg, sample-accurately. That division of labour is what made the project feel workable. The model handles the part it's good at, which is reading a conversation and having an opinion about what to trim. The app handles the part it's good at, which is being exact and reversible. The approval step in between is where most of our care went. How we built it The recorder came first and took most of the time. Capture. An AudioWorklet per station at 48 kHz, 24-bit mono, with echo cancellation, noise suppression and automatic gain deliberately switched off. Two-second chunks, each carrying a CRC32, buffered in IndexedDB so a Wi-Fi dropout costs you nothing. Node, Fastify and ws on the host machine; the browser side is plain ES modules with no framework. Clock drift. Two laptops have two crystals, and over an hour they disagree. This was the hardest audio problem here. Werkstern writes a 1 kHz stripe digitally into every raw track just after the start and again just before the stop, then locates both afterwards by cross-correlation. For each guest track the correction factor is $$f = \frac{n_{\text{end}} - n_{\text{start}}}{N_{\text{end}} - N_{\text{start}}}$$ where $n$ are the guest's stripe positions and $N$ the host's, in samples. We report it as $(f - 1) \times 10^{6}$ ppm and correct it with asetrate plus SOXR resampling at 28-bit precision. A take is accepted only when the residual offset stays under 240 samples, which at 48 kHz works out to $$\frac{240}{48\,000} = 5\ \text{ms}.$$ Rendering. The soundboard stays out of the microphone signal. Every pad press is stored as an event, and the final stereo mix is rebuilt afterwards from that log plus a content-addressed copy of each sound file. Replace a sound six months later and the old episode still renders exactly as it did. The agent layer sits on top and touches none of that. Eight tools registered in the top-level document through document.modelContext.registerTool : four read-only ones, preview_edit_batch which validates and displays without saving anything, and three writing tools behind the approval dialog. Every schema is closed with additionalProperties: false and bounded lengths, and each tool carries an honest readOnlyHint so the host can tell reading from writing. Cuts are stored as batches of source-time regions with a revision number that only ever goes up. Every write has to state the revision it believes is current, so a command built on stale information gets rejected instead of landing on a take that has moved on. The preview you hear is the complement of what was removed: for regions $R = {[s_i, e_i)}$ over a take of length $T$, it plays $[0, T) \setminus \bigcup_i [s_i, e_i)$, concatenated. Challenges we ran into Approval turned out to be a concurrency problem. Our worst bug had nothing to do with audio. Two approval requests could share the same dialog element: the second one overwrote the text of the first while it was still open, and one click then resolved both promises. You would have read "render a preview?" and, with that same click, authorized a permanent edit. Approvals are serialized now, and a second request arriving while one is open gets refused. Cancelling, losing the connection, leaving the dialog unanswered, and a dialog that fails to open all count as a no. Undo had a hole in it. If you queued a preview render and then hit undo, the render was still holding the old region list and would cheerfully write the audio you had just discarded back to disk. A render now re-reads the take before it starts and gives up if the batch is gone. We reviewed our own work adversarially, and it was uncomfortable. Before calling it finished we ran an independent audit against our own commit. It came back with one critical finding and a handful of serious ones. Then, while fixing those, I introduced a new one: my approval guard could get stuck and silently refuse every write for the rest of the session. It only surfaced because a browser test behaved oddly and I chased it instead of writing it off as flakiness. I'm glad I did. Testing a modal dialog under browser automation was miserable. The automation pane we used never dispatches the <dialog> close event. showModal() opens it, close() sets the return value, and the listener simply never runs. Instead of calling it working on a harness we couldn't trust, we proved the flow two other ways: unit tests with a DOM stub where the event does fire, and an end-to-end test that drives the real FFmpeg binary over a synthetic take, checks the output is exactly 48 kHz stereo 16-bit at the expected length, and confirms the source file's SHA-256 is identical before and after. Being straight about filler words. The first feature everyone asks for is automatic removal of "um" and "äh". We have no transcript and no speech recognition, so we can't do it. get_edit_capabilities says so directly in its output, to the model, in the tool response itself. Leaving that vague would have taken one sentence. and honestly we didnt had time to make a video, so we simply asked ChatGPT to generate one :D What we learn

This demo explores user interfaces that adapt to the humans that browse it, and asks what would the web be if you could B-Y-O-U-I: Bring your own user interface. Like Comment Story Updates Inspiration For as much as marketers have optimized for conversion, they haven't been able to cut through the number one reason that people leave open carts. they're not ready to buy. It had just happened to me while shopping for a microphone: I started at one site, then realized I needed more information to determine which was going to be the best fit. What if the shopping interface could have met me where I was at--researching the options, ranking the matches, explaining its rationale, and then designing a search result layout to fit its conclusions? That's just one glimpse of how web-interfaces of the future will adapt to their users' needs through a Live Canvas. What it does Each demo walks you through a conventional user interface, and then cordons off a bounded portion of it as WebMCP to redesign (the Live Canvas) The Storefront demo lets the agent curate the search results with a toolbox of approved card presets and badges. This constrained design is a good fit for the untrusted nature of the public internet, keeping users safe from XSS attacks. The Dashbord demo scales the idea up by giving you an entirely blank page. It imagine how the future of CRM / ERP dashboards might function. Prompt the agent to onboard you, collaborate on a vision with the agent as you would with your analytics team, and the end result is an entirely personal dashboard of controls, visualizations and reports. The Distribution demo scales the idea outwards by asking whether you distribute a Live Canvas as part of conventional component libraries. It demonstrates what a future Datatable component could be, with the ability to generate bespoke reports and save them to your browser - all without backend engineering or ai-vendor lock-in. How we built it The architecture north star is "agent composition, application execution". The application exposes its UI primitives through WebMCP. The agent supplies a composition, and then the live canvas renders that composition using application-owned components. This solution prevents data from leaking out of the organization's security boundary, and also prevents token bloat. Challenges we ran into and accomplishments that we're proud of There are two large challenges that required creativity: The largest challenge was discoverability. WebMCP is not bidirectional: an agent can invoke tools, but there's no mechanism for the website to initiate a conversation. The past 30 years of the web have trained users for the affordance of buttons and forms. I built a new set of visible prompts and verbs to communicate to surface the new modality which was an interesting accomplishment, but does highlight an apparent shortcoming in the standard. The second challenge is the built-in approval flow of agentic browsers. ChatGPT would auto-deny requests to access the user's simulated library to make recommendations, e.g. In the short term, this is solved with instructional text to ask for permission in advance. However, what WebMCP needs is a built in consent layer where tools are tied to required consent artifacts. What we learned WebMCP shows great potential to break interfaces out of the "ai chatbot jail". What's next for Live Canvas The most likely next step is to select an existing open-source component library and use it to build the first end-to-end WebMCP UI framework. That would turn the Live Canvas from just a demo pattern into a production-hardened implementation.
