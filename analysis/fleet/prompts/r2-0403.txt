# FROZEN SCORING RUBRIC (pre-registered before any Stage 1 review)

Scores are integers 1-10 on each of the FOUR official criteria. Every score carries:
rationale, evidence_surfaces, confidence (0-1). Reviewers use the full scale and these
band anchors verbatim. Category neutrality is absolute: a game, art piece, music tool,
CRM, weird toy, and developer tool are all eligible for 10/10 on every criterion if they
satisfy that criterion exceptionally well FOR WHAT THEY ARE TRYING TO BE.
No aesthetic penalties. Signs of hurried AI work matter only as actual product-quality
problems (incoherence, broken flow, placeholder content), never as style.

## WebMCP Leverage — what does WebMCP change for this product?
Judge the delta WebMCP makes to reliability, precision, structured state access,
shared human-agent state, repeatability, and capability. Tool COUNT is not leverage.
- 9-10 TRANSFORMATIVE: the intended experience is impractical without the structured
  agent surface; human and agent share live product state; the collaboration could not
  be replicated comparably well by a general agent driving the UI.
- 7-8 MAJOR DELTA: without WebMCP a general agent is markedly less reliable/precise
  here; the agent is central to the product, not a bolt-on.
- 5-6 MEANINGFUL DELTA: existing capability packaged into a genuinely better product
  experience via structured tools; same outcome otherwise possible with real friction.
- 3-4 MINOR DELTA: generic browser automation achieves comparable outcomes; WebMCP is
  a convenience layer.
- 1-2 COSMETIC: exposes an action a UI button already offers; no meaningful change.

## Execution — is the actual product coherent, intentional, complete for its scope?
Stage 1 scores EXECUTION_CLAIMED: coherence/intentionality/completeness as evidenced by
the packet (description + packaging + video frames/transcript). Thin evidence caps the
score (end-to-end credit requires end-to-end evidence) and lowers confidence.
Stage 2 may rescore as EXECUTION_OBSERVED; latest evidence-backed score replaces, never
sums.
- 9-10: all surfaces agree; central workflow demonstrably completes; states handled;
  feels finished for its stated scope.
- 7-8: strong coherence and intentionality; central workflow appears functional; minor
  unfinished edges.
- 5-6: plausibly real with visible gaps; scope unclear or core not demonstrated
  end-to-end.
- 3-4: thin prototype; contradictions between surfaces; placeholder content.
- 1-2: non-functional or incoherent for its claimed scope.

## Creativity & Ambition — novelty of concept and interaction model
Judge ONLY: novelty of the concept, novelty of the interaction model, originality
relative to existing concepts, ambition, memorable/surprising use of the medium, and
the depth with which the concept is pursued. WebMCP indispensability belongs under
WebMCP Leverage, NOT here: a project can be extremely creative even if another agent
API could theoretically have enabled something similar.
- 9-10: genuinely novel concept or interaction model; ambitious scope pursued deeply
  and coherently; memorable.
- 7-8: fresh synthesis or notably original interaction; ambitious for a hackathon.
- 5-6: competent take on a known category with a real new angle.
- 3-4: derivative; novelty mostly surface details.
- 1-2: clone/template with trivial changes.
"That the category exists" is never a penalty; judge the submitted concept.

## Potential Impact — credible value for the intended audience
Judge: is there a real audience; is there a real problem, desire, or use case for that
audience; does the demonstrated solution genuinely address it. NO commercial adoption,
monetization, market pull, or startup viability is required. Entertainment, play, art,
creative expression, learning, niche hobby use, experimentation, and community value
are legitimate forms of impact. A tiny audience fully justifies a top score.
- 9-10: specific real audience with a real problem/desire; solution demonstrably
  addresses it; credible value, appeal, usefulness, or meaning for that audience.
- 7-8: clear audience and problem, good fit, credible value.
- 5-6: identifiable audience, looser fit or partial address.
- 3-4: speculative audience or strained fit; impact mostly aspirational.
- 1-2: no coherent audience/problem pairing.
Audience size is NOT a multiplier.

## Aggregate & ranking
aggregate = leverage + execution + impact + creativity (4-40). Nothing else enters.
Tie-breaks: Leverage, then Execution, then Impact, then Creativity, then higher
combined evidence confidence, then slug alphabetical. See PROTOCOL.md Ranking section.

An agent-ready flood lab where people ask “what if?” and WebMCP agents safely run, inspect, replay, and compare flood scenarios with every assumption visible. Like Comment Story Updates Inspiration Flood simulations can be difficult to use and even harder to explain. A person may need to choose a location, configure a scenario, review assumptions, start a long-running calculation, inspect several results, replay the flood, and compare it with another run. An AI agent looking only at buttons and pixels could easily choose the wrong setup or mistake the colour of an animation for a real result. I built CausaFlow to solve both problems. It is an urban flood investigation lab where a person asks the question and a WebMCP agent safely handles the multi-step work inside the application. What CausaFlow does CausaFlow lets people explore flood situations in two study areas: Texas Medical Center and Brays Bayou in Houston Velachery and its surrounding area in Chennai In Houston, CausaFlow can test heavy rain, a river carrying more water than its assumed capacity, or blocked drains along a hospital access road. In Velachery, it can keep rain off the city itself while applying severe rain around its boundary, then show which monitored neighbourhoods are affected first. Each completed run provides more than an animation. It reports water depth, speed, affected area, arrival times, water accounting, assumptions, limitations, and result identities that can be checked later. Why WebMCP is a strong fit Flood investigation is not a single-button task. It is a chain of connected decisions and actions. Without WebMCP, an agent would have to guess its way through a complex 3D interface. It could select the wrong scenario, run an outdated setup, lose track of which result is displayed, or describe what the animation appears to show instead of reading the actual numbers. WebMCP gives the agent direct access to clearly defined actions and exact application state. The agent knows which study area is active, which scenario is staged, whether the user has approved it, which calculation is running, which result is displayed, and what the result actually says. This makes CausaFlow faster and easier to use while keeping the person in control. What people and agents can do together The person provides the goal, judgment, and approval. The agent handles the repeated application work. For example, a person can ask: Block every represented drain along the hospital access road, show when the route becomes unsafe, then reopen the fewest drains needed and run the same storm again. The WebMCP agent can then: Open the correct Houston study area. Place the represented drains along the access road. Stage the blocked scenario. Show the assumptions on screen. Wait for the person’s explicit approval. Start and monitor the flood calculation. Read the completed numerical result. Save it as the comparison case. Run the tested intervention under the same storm. Compare both results and move the camera to the important location. Completing that entire sequence manually would require navigating several controls and carefully tracking two separate results. With WebMCP, the person can remain focused on the investigation while the agent handles the mechanics. Every important action remains visible in the interface, and the normal controls remain available as a fallback. How I implemented WebMCP I register the application’s tools directly through document.modelContext.registerTool . The tools are divided into two groups: Flood tools for inspecting capabilities, staging scenarios, requesting approval, starting calculations, monitoring progress, reading results, controlling playback, sampling locations, and comparing runs. Workspace tools for changing study areas, reading the visible scene, moving the camera, and focusing on important results. Each tool has a clear description and a strict input format. Tools that only read information are marked separately from tools that change the application. CausaFlow also protects the workflow in several ways: A calculation cannot begin until the exact staged scenario has been approved. Every scenario change creates a new revision, preventing an agent from approving an older setup by mistake. The user’s request to run a scenario is recorded as explicit authorization. Long-running calculations return progress instead of leaving the agent guessing. Completed results have stable identities and cannot be silently changed during replay or comparison. The agent reads numerical results from the application instead of estimating values from the animation. WebMCP is the access and coordination layer. It does not create the flood results. ABOUT TEXT (untrusted evidence):
A Korean navigation app with no map, where screen reader users search places, plan trips and explore their surroundings in plain text, now declared to the browser agent as seven WebMCP tools. Like 1 Comment 1 Story Updates Inspiration Gildongmu means "traveling companion" in Korean. I started it in June 2026 to fix a small, stubborn problem of my own as a screen reader user: I wanted to look up an address and call a place as fast as possible. Map apps make both slow, because their answers live in an interface built around images. So Gildongmu has no map at all. Everything it knows, it says in words: places, routes, what is around you, and the accessibility details a screen reader user actually needs, such as station elevators, audio beacons, accessible restrooms, stairs-free walking routes and crosswalks with audible signals. Almost all of that comes from Korean public data. When I read about WebMCP, I realized that this had quietly prepared the app for agents. Every sentence a screen reader user hears is a sentence an agent can be handed. This entry is what happened when I handed them over. What it does Gildongmu is a free navigation app for South Korea with no account and no ads, on the web, on iOS, and as an npm command line tool and MCP server. It joins about thirty public and commercial APIs from nineteen organizations and turns them into plain text. As far as I know, it is the first navigation app in Korea where a screen reader user can search places, plan a trip and explore the surroundings with no barrier in between. For this challenge the web app declares seven WebMCP tools to the browser agent: describe_app: what this deployment can do and how the tools chain search_places: places, addresses and web results, with refs to pass on get_place_info: a place by ref; for stations the timetable, accessibility facilities, live arrivals and barrier-free facilities plan_directions: transit, walking and driving summaries for a destination get_transit_route_detail: one transit route in full, including the boarding door for a quick exit get_route_steps: walking or driving steps as a numbered page read_current_view: which screen the app is on and what it shows Ask the agent "Tell me the first train, the last train and the elevators at Gangdong Station (강동역)" and one tool call brings all of it back. Ask "Seoul Station (서울역) to Gangnam Station (강남역): which car and door should I board to get off next to the elevator?" and while the agent answers, the app in the browser tab has already moved to that route, so the user can turn on VoiceOver and check every line. Why this use case is a strong fit for WebMCP The answers were already text. Most navigation apps keep their answer in pixels, so an agent driving a browser has to guess at a canvas. Gildongmu had to write every answer as a sentence for VoiceOver users long before it ever met an agent. Those same sentences are what the tools return, and they come from the same code that draws the screen. The agent cannot be told something the screen does not say. Neither a plain browser agent nor a server MCP can do this alone. Behind one station answer are four public APIs, each with its own key, its own response format and, in Korea, its own coordinate system. An agent reading the page cannot rebuild that. A server MCP, on the other hand, cannot answer "where am I standing right now", because it has no browser tab. WebMCP sits exactly in between: the page keeps the keys and the location, and the agent asks in words. Location stays in the browser. A trip starts from the user's current position, which the page already knows. Tool output goes through a list of allowed fields that has no place for coordinates, so the agent's provider receives place names and sentences, never a coordinate pair. Gildongmu has promised since its first release that nothing personal is stored on its servers, and the tools keep that promise. How it creates a better user experience For a screen reader user the expensive part is not reaching a screen, it is exploring it. A transit plan on screen is dozens of items, one swipe at a time. As a tool result it is one spoken answer, and the app has still moved to that same screen, so the user can take over and verify anything. The size of an answer follows the same instinct. A full transit answer came out at 3,700 characters, far past the 1,500 the guidance recommends. Instead of cutting it off, I split it the way I would say it out loud: the recommended route in full, the other routes in one line each, and the details behind a second tool. Nothing got worse for people. The tools press the same functions a person clicks, and a person always wins. Type a new search or close a section while a tool is running and the tool call ends as superseded. If a dialog is open, the tool declines instead of closing it. What people and agents can do together that was difficult or impossible before "Which car and door do I board so that I get off next to the elevator?" Gildongmu matches a public quick exit dataset to the live route, so get_transit_route_detail returns the boarding door for each leg. No map tells you this. Ask it in the chat, and the app is already sitting on that route. "Does Gangdong Station have an elevator, and when is the first train?" One call to get_place_info returns the timetable, the accessibility facilities, the real time arrivals and the barrier-free facilities. Without WebMCP the agent would open four sections one by one, and it could not call the four public APIs behind them at all. The handoff goes both ways. The agent and the user share one screen: the tools move the app, and read_current_view reads whatever the person did by hand. The agent can set up a trip that the user finishes by touch, or the user can search by hand and then ask the agent about what is on the screen. Before this, asking an assistant and using an app were two separate places that did not know about each other. Honest absence. Every tool tells apart "none", "not known" and "lookup failed", and says outOfCoverage outside Korea instead of inventing a result. A screen reader user cannot glance at a screen to catch a made up answer, so the tools never let the agent guess. The app has followed the same rule for people since version 1.0. How I implemented WebMCP I used the imperative API, document.modelContext.registerTool with a name, a description, an input schema and an execute function. Here is how I understand what I built: One set of seven tools, registered once at the app root, because an agent reads the tool list once. A tool that is not configured on this deployment is still declared and answers notConfigured. A tool that needs another screen moves there itself. Each screen publishes a small bridge to a registry, and a tool reads it at the moment it runs. A tool that waits for a screen waits for a specific place id or sequence number, not for a screen name, so a call about place A cannot be satisfied by place B appearing first. Only one tool runs at a time, with a 30 second cap. A place handle is an opaque ref that remembers which search it came from, and a handle from a replaced search returns staleResult with the call that recovers it, rather than pointing at the wrong place. Every output leaves through one function. It keeps only the allowed fields, refuses coordinates, trims to the 1,500 character budget item by item, and pages a long answer with an offset. Calls to paid third party APIs are budgeted per provider. The human sentences come from the same modules the screen uses, so the screen and the tools cannot drift apart. It is covered by 19 test files and 104 tests. I checked the contracts by deliberately breaking the implementation and confirming that the tests caught each break, and I checked the deployed site with Chrome DevTools WebMCP inspection.
