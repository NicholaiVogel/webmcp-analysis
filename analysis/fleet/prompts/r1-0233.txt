Scores are integers 1-10 on each of the FOUR official criteria. Every score carries:
rationale, evidence_surfaces, confidence (0-1). Reviewers use the full scale and these

## WebMCP Leverage — what does WebMCP change for this product?
Judge the delta WebMCP makes to reliability, precision, structured state access,
shared human-agent state, repeatability, and capability. Tool COUNT is not leverage.
- 9-10 TRANSFORMATIVE: the intended experience is impractical without the structured
  agent surface; human and agent share live product state; the collaboration could not
  be replicated comparably well by a general agent driving the UI.
- 7-8 MAJOR DELTA: without WebMCP a general agent is markedly less reliable/precise
  here; the agent is central to the product, not a bolt-on.
- 5-6 MEANINGFUL DELTA: existing capability packaged into a genuinely better product
  experience via structured tools; same outcome otherwise possible with real friction.
- 3-4 MINOR DELTA: generic browser automation achieves comparable outcomes; WebMCP is
  a convenience layer.
- 1-2 COSMETIC: exposes an action a UI button already offers; no meaningful change.

Stage 1 scores EXECUTION_CLAIMED: coherence/intentionality/completeness as evidenced by
the packet (description + packaging + video frames/transcript). Thin evidence caps the
score (end-to-end credit requires end-to-end evidence) and lowers confidence.
Stage 2 may rescore as EXECUTION_OBSERVED; latest evidence-backed score replaces, never
sums.
- 9-10: all surfaces agree; central workflow demonstrably completes; states handled;
  feels finished for its stated scope.
- 7-8: strong coherence and intentionality; central workflow appears functional; minor
  unfinished edges.
- 5-6: plausibly real with visible gaps; scope unclear or core not demonstrated
  end-to-end.
- 3-4: thin prototype; contradictions between surfaces; placeholder content.
- 1-2: non-functional or incoherent for its claimed scope.

## Creativity & Ambition — novelty of concept and interaction model
Judge ONLY: novelty of the concept, novelty of the interaction model, originality
relative to existing concepts, ambition, memorable/surprising use of the medium, and
the depth with which the concept is pursued. WebMCP indispensability belongs under
WebMCP Leverage, NOT here: a project can be extremely creative even if another agent
API could theoretically have enabled something similar.
- 9-10: genuinely novel concept or interaction model; ambitious scope pursued deeply
  and coherently; memorable.
- 7-8: fresh synthesis or notably original interaction; ambitious for a hackathon.
- 5-6: competent take on a known category with a real new angle.
- 3-4: derivative; novelty mostly surface details.
- 1-2: clone/template with trivial changes.
"That the category exists" is never a penalty; judge the submitted concept.

## Potential Impact — credible value for the intended audience
Judge: is there a real audience; is there a real problem, desire, or use case for that
audience; does the demonstrated solution genuinely address it. NO commercial adoption,

creative expression, learning, niche hobby use, experimentation, and community value

Audience size is NOT a multiplier.

aggregate = leverage + execution + impact + creativity (4-40). Nothing else enters.

TaskDrop started as a way to move working context between AI clients. Different AI tools are good at different things, but their context is usually trapped inside individual conversations and applications. TaskDrop originally addressed this with temporary, versioned Markdown Handoffs that AI clients could read and update through MCP. That worked for AI-to-AI transfer, but it exposed a bigger limitation: the human was still mostly outside the shared working state. A Handoff could move from one AI to another, but there was no first-class place where a human could inspect what was being transferred, correct stale assumptions, refine missing details, or collaborate with a browser agent before the next AI continued. TaskDrop started as a way to move working context between AI clients. WebMCP transformed it into a shared human-agent workspace where that context can be inspected, refined, corrected, and safely committed before the next AI continues. What WebMCP changed WebMCP changed TaskDrop from a handoff transport into a shared human-agent workspace. Before WebMCP, TaskDrop could move committed context between AI clients. With WebMCP, that Handoff becomes a visible workspace where the human and browser agent can inspect and refine the same Working Draft . When the agent updates the Working Draft, the change appears immediately in the editor the human is already looking at. The human can continue editing, correct the agent, or discard the Draft. Human + WebMCP agent ↓ Working Draft ↓ explicit Commit ↓ Immutable Revision ↓ Remote MCP clients The Working Draft creates a clear boundary between work in progress and shared state . Human and agent edits remain local until an explicit Commit creates the next immutable Revision. Once committed, another MCP client can immediately continue from that state. WebMCP also makes TaskDrop's shared memory more transparent. The workspace exposes Revision history and records whether a committed Revision came from mcp , human , or webmcp . And when another client commits a newer Revision while the browser is still editing, TaskDrop preserves the local Draft and lets the human decide how to proceed instead of silently overwriting either state. Before WebMCP, TaskDrop moved context. With WebMCP, humans and agents can work on that context together. How we built it We kept TaskDrop's immutable Handoff model and added a lightweight, browser-owned collaboration layer on top of it. The core architectural rule is simple: Browser owns unfinished collaborative work. Server owns committed immutable Handoff state. The Working Draft therefore lives in browser-session storage. Human edits and WebMCP edits modify the same local Draft, while the server continues to store only immutable, append-only Revisions. Remote MCP and the browser workspace use different interaction surfaces, but both ultimately operate on the same Handoff application logic: Remote MCP ──────┐ ▼ Handoff Application ▲ Browser API ─────┘ ▲ Web UI + WebMCP We also designed the WebMCP tools as page-scoped capabilities . The browser agent operates on the Handoff already open in the workspace instead of receiving the user's underlying Space Key as a normal tool parameter. That gave us another principle we want to preserve: The agent should receive capability, not credentials. These choices let us add meaningful human-agent collaboration without turning TaskDrop into a full collaborative document platform. Challenges and what we learned The biggest challenge was not exposing TaskDrop through WebMCP. It was deciding what should become collaborative, and where that collaboration should live . TaskDrop originally assumed that committed Handoff state moved between AI clients through MCP. Once a human and a browser agent could both participate, that model was no longer enough. We needed a place where both sides could safely inspect and refine the same state without turning every intermediate edit into a server Revision. That challenge led directly to the Working Draft model. The same principle also helped us handle concurrency. If another MCP client updates the Handoff while the browser is editing, TaskDrop preserves the local Draft and asks the human how to proceed instead of silently overwriting either version. Credential handling taught us a similar lesson. An agent should operate within the application context the human has already opened, rather than being handed the credentials that created that context. Together, these challenges changed how we think about WebMCP itself. With Remote MCP alone, TaskDrop was good at moving state between AI clients , but there was no natural shared surface where a human and an agent could work on that state together. WebMCP fills that missing layer. Remote MCP AI Client A ↓ Handoff ↓ Human + WebMCP agent Working Draft ↓ Commit ↓ Handoff ↓ Remote MCP AI Client B It allows the agent to participate directly in the application state the human is already seeing. MCP helps agents connect to services. WebMCP helps agents enter the human's application workflow. For TaskDrop, that difference turned an AI-to-AI handoff layer into a place where humans and agents can inspect, correct, refine, and commit shared working context together. Instead of asking users to navigate pricing pages, compare capabilities, and manually piece together trade-offs, StrategyShifu exposes structured decision workflows through WebMCP. Like Comment Story Updates Inspiration Complex cloud architecture and migration decisions are rarely solved by a single recommendation. Teams need to compare costs, technical constraints, operational trade-offs, and implementation risks before committing to a strategy. We built StrategyShifu to explore a better model for human–agent collaboration: an AI agent that can work directly with a live decision-making application through WebMCP, while the user can see every change, review the reasoning, and make the final decision. What it does StrategyShifu is a WebMCP-powered decision workspace for cloud cost planning and data-platform migration strategy. The current version supports two decision workflows: Databricks cost planning: Configure DWH, ETL, and development workloads and compare compute strategies using regional pricing, schedules, networking requirements, Databricks Genie compatibility, and project budgets. Redshift-to-Databricks migration planning: Compare Iceberg coexistence, Lakehouse Federation, UNLOAD to S3, and JDBC-based migration strategies using workload characteristics, downtime tolerance, synchronization plans, target formats, and execution readiness. Through WebMCP, an agent can inspect the current project, modify inputs, test alternatives, calculate costs, compare strategies, and explain the recommendation. Every agent action is reflected immediately in the interface, allowing the user and the agent to work from the same decision context. How we built it StrategyShifu is built with Next.js, React, and TypeScript. WebMCP forms the interaction layer between the application and browser-based AI agents. We followed several WebMCP best practices: Registered focused, task-oriented tools using document.modelContext.registerTool() . Designed tools around user goals instead of exposing low-level UI actions. Used clear tool names, descriptions, and input schemas. Added runtime validation for agent-provided inputs. Returned both human-readable text and structured content. Included tool annotations to communicate read-only and mutating behavior. Used AbortSignal cleanup to prevent duplicate or stale tool registrations. Kept tool operations deterministic and safe. Used stable identifiers for selecting and updating workloads. Returned updated state after mutations so agents can continue without scraping the page. Provided clear errors and recovery guidance for invalid operations. Gracefully preserved the human interface when WebMCP was unavailable. Most importantly, WebMCP tools and human controls operate on the same React state. There is no separate agent-only state or hidden decision model. The application currently exposes: 17 WebMCP tools for workload configuration, pricing, cost estimation, strategy comparison, and recommendation retrieval. 7 WebMCP tools for migration inputs, capability evidence, and Redshift-to-Databricks assessment. The recommendation engines remain deterministic and catalog-driven. The agent explores and configures scenarios, while the application owns calculations, validation, compatibility gates, scoring, and ranking. Challenges we ran into One of the main challenges was designing a WebMCP tool surface that represented meaningful decision operations rather than browser automation. For example, instead of asking an agent to find and click several form fields, StrategyShifu provides tools such as add_workload , set_budget , compare_strategies , and get_recommendation . This makes agent interactions more reliable, understandable, and reusable. Another challenge was synchronizing agent actions with human interactions. We solved this by connecting WebMCP tools directly to the same state update functions used by the visible interface. We also had to balance agent flexibility with predictable recommendations. Agents can explore many scenarios, but technical compatibility, budget enforcement, pricing calculations, and migration scoring must remain reproducible and auditable. Finally, migration decisions depend on evolving platform capabilities. We introduced structured capability evidence with documented-source links and a fallback mechanism so temporary documentation failures do not prevent an assessment. Accomplishments that we're proud of We are proud that StrategyShifu demonstrates WebMCP as a foundation for genuine collaboration rather than a collection of isolated agent commands. Key accomplishments include: A shared live workspace where humans and agents operate on the same state. 24 task-oriented WebMCP tools across cost and migration planning. Structured tool responses that support multi-step agent workflows. Runtime input validation and actionable error messages. Safe tool registration with lifecycle cleanup. Deterministic and explainable recommendation engines. Full-project cost analysis across multiple workloads. Explicit Pass/Fail gates for budget and technical requirements. Transparent DBU and infrastructure cost breakdowns. Migration strategy scoring, alternatives, risks, and readiness evaluation. Suggested implementation steps and production cutover checks. Structured technical evidence supporting migration assessments. Share, copy, CSV, and Print/PDF export options. Responsive views for desktop and mobile users. The agent does not simply summarize information. It can actively construct, modify, and evaluate a decision alongside the user. What we learned We learned that WebMCP works especially well for applications with structured state, repeatable workflows, and multiple possible decision paths. Giving agents semantic tools is more dependable than asking them to infer application state from the visual interface. Structured inputs and outputs also make multi-step reasoning easier to validate and debug. We also learned that agentic interaction should be separated from business logic. WebMCP provides the agent with controlled access to the application, while deterministic engines remain responsible for calculations and recommendations. Finally, transparency is essential. Users are more likely to trust an agent-assisted recommendation when they can see the inputs, understand the failed requirements, compare alternatives, and reproduce the result themselves. What's next for StrategyShifu Our next goal is to turn StrategyShifu into a broader multi-platform strategy workspace. Planned improvements include: Add more migration strategies across AWS, Azure, and Google Cloud. Support additional source and target platforms, including Snowflake, BigQuery, Synapse, PostgreSQL, and other data warehouses. Add cross-platform compute and architecture comparisons. Expand WebMCP tools into guided multi-step planning workflows. Add migration phase tracking and implementation checklists. Allow organizations to add their own policies, pricing agreements, and decision criteria. Our long-term vision is for StrategyShifu to become a WebMCP-powered strategy layer across platforms—helping people and agents evaluate complex technology decisions together with transparency, consistency, and control. ABOUT TEXT (untrusted evidence):
An AI agent plans a whole neighborhood budget through WebMCP tools. It has no tool to adopt the plan, so the decision stays with a named human by design. Like Comment Story Updates Inspiration The reflex in agent tooling right now is to expose more actions. Give the agent another tool, another capability, let it do more. That has a blind spot: some decisions are not ours to delegate. Whether a patient consents to a procedure, whether a family's loan is refused, how a neighborhood spends public money. Each has to be attributable to a named person and contestable afterwards. An agent can and should do the work around such a decision. It should not be the thing that makes it. We wanted a WebMCP project that took that seriously. Not a confirmation dialog the agent could talk its way through, but a structural boundary. The setting is participatory budgeting, inspired by the gram sabha and ward committee, Kerala's People's Plan Campaign, and Pune's ward budgets, where residents themselves decide how a local fund is spent. What it does One resident splits a fixed hypothetical fund of ten lakh rupees across exactly eight local public works that cannot all be funded. An AI browser agent assists. The agent gets seven WebMCP tools: get_budget_state : the whole board in one call. Fund, priorities, protected works, the active draft and its validity, revision numbers. list_projects : the eight works with costs, funding rules, dependencies, incompatibilities, and the full numeric scoring model. list_strategy_options : three valid budget directions, each scored against the resident's current priorities. simulate_allocation : run the shared validator on a hypothetical and get every rule violation at once, each with a machine readable fix. propose_allocation : file a draft as the agent's own, after validation. An invalid draft is still stored, visibly, as a rejected draft. explain_tradeoffs : what changed between two plans, and what it costs. request_allocation_review : hand the draft to the resident. It cannot accept or adopt. Five read only, two that write, none that commits a decision. There is deliberately no tool to set priorities, protect a work, accept, reject, adopt, or reset. Those are the resident's, through visible buttons on the page. The agent can model the entire budget and draft a valid plan. It cannot make the call, because the function that would make it was never registered. When the resident protects a work the agent left out, a flood prone riverside park where her children play, the agent's draft goes stale on screen and it has to read the budget again and rebuild around that choice. The machine adapts to the person's decision. It does not get to freeze one. How we built it A pure domain engine in TypeScript, framework free, with no DOM and no globals: the eight project dataset, one deterministic allocation validator that returns every issue in a stable order and never repairs anything, a benefit scoring model that is fully disclosed so the agent can reproduce every number, and a deterministic rebuild that keeps protected works and trims the rest to fit. One in memory store, with React bindings through useSyncExternalStore. The manual UI and every WebMCP tool handler dispatch into the same store instance and call the same validator, so displayed values and tool output cannot diverge. A human budget change bumps a revision counter and stales any active proposal. Only the reducer writes actor labels, derived from the action prefix, so the final record has trustworthy authorship. The WebMCP adapter registers the seven tools against whichever the runtime provides, navigator.modelContext or document.modelContext, using a shared AbortController so aborting unregisters every tool. Handlers return the MCP result shape with structuredContent, and rule violations come back as data, not errors, so a failed plan stays inspectable. React 19, Vite, Vitest. No backend, no API keys, no external data calls, no persistence beyond the browser tab. Deployed as a static site. Challenges we ran into Registration under React StrictMode. StrictMode double invokes effects, and our first version registered three of seven tools before the second pass cancelled the first. The fix was a caller owned AbortSignal and a deferred registration pass, so the setup, cleanup, setup cycle collapses to one real registration. Two runtimes, one adapter. The editor draft and ChatGPT expose document.modelContext; Chrome's preview build exposes navigator.modelContext. A judge on Chrome would have seen no assistant at all. We now feature detect and prefer navigator. Keeping the boundary honest. What we built governs the cooperative structured tool surface a WebMCP style agent negotiates with the page. It is not a privilege boundary: an agent driving the raw DOM could still click the resident's buttons. We say so plainly, in the app and the README. What omission actually buys is that a cooperative agent has no function to call, and the decision record has a human author by construction. Deployment. Our host froze production deploys mid event on a plan limit, leaving the live site several commits behind. We moved continuous deployment to GitHub Pages, switched to relative asset paths so the build works under a project subpath, and verified the full agent loop against the new URL. Accomplishments that we're proud of The boundary is visible, not asserted. The app shows a live trace of every WebMCP call the agent makes, labelled read or write, and a permanent list of the actions no tool can perform. Ask the agent to adopt the plan and nothing happens: no refusal, no error, no tool call. The absence is the proof. One validated state, shared byte for byte between the human UI and the agent tools. The agent literally cannot be shown a different number than the resident sees. It degrades gracefully. With no WebMCP runtime present, every flow still works by hand, including the rebuild around a protected work. What we learned The interesting lever in WebMCP is not only which tools you add. It is which you withhold, and whether that omission is legible to the agent and to the person. Our get_budget_state returns a structuralLimits field that tells the agent, in plain terms, the things no tool will do. An agent that reads it stops asking. Enforced by omission beats a confirmation dialog for decisions that must stay human. There is no rubber stamp step to automate, no injection target, and a decision record with a named author by construction rather than by policy. What's next for Neighbors Decide Extract the pattern as a small reference other WebMCP builders can adopt: an agent does the preparatory work, the act of deciding stays human and attributable, both sides read the same validated state, and the boundary is a tool that was never registered. The same shape fits clinical informed consent, lending decisions, hiring, content moderation, and legal filing. The budget scenario is a vehicle. The pattern is the point. Like Comment Story Updates The Problem Filing an annual individual tax return (1770 S) under Indonesia's centralized Coretax system creates a learning curve for salaried workers. Translating personal life events into rigid government tax classifications creates a high burden and confusion across generations. Knowing your salary or family situation is different from knowing which tax field or classification it belongs in. While the system offers spreadsheet bulk uploads to bypass manual entry, they frequently fail due to minor error, forcing users back to rework manual entry or exposing them to server timeouts during peak traffic. Independent sentiment analysis of over 53,000 digital conversations during the early rollout Coretax system revealed that 39% of user sentiment was negative, driven by procedural confusion and system navigation (Source: Journal UII / 2025 ). Despite these points, the submission is massive. By late April 2026, over 13 million annual tax returns were filed following the activation of nearly 12.9 million individual accounts (Source: Tempo.co ). The Solution: Human-Agent Collaboration via WebMCP This project demonstrates how WebMCP can turn a complex tax administration portal into a collaborative interface. Rather than hiding the form's complexity or forcing users to navigate hidden form logic, the browser exposes section-level tools directly to the assistant, keeping the detailed form visible to ensure user review control capability. The Workflow Conversational Extraction: The assistant conducts a plain-language interview to extract necessary tax facts such as salary, deductions, and family status without requiring users to memorize form codes or tax classifications. They can use their own ways to describe their condition and an AI assistant will translate it. For example, when a user says, “My employer was PT Nusantara Digital, gross salary 180 million for the year, 6 million in pay deductions,” WebMCP translates it into: “Records the employment slip, derives net income, recomputes the tax.” Deterministic State Updates: As facts are confirmed, the assistant invokes section-level WebMCP tools (such as updating taxpayer profiles, recording employment, or adding cash and assets). The website itself handles all validation, calculation, and conditional branching, deriving fields like PTKP (e.g., K/1) and recomputing the tax balance automatically. Manual Submission: Final review, resolution of data conflicts, declaration, and submission remain strictly manual. No tool can execute a final filing, and the assistant explicitly states this if asked.
