# FROZEN SCORING RUBRIC (pre-registered before any Stage 1 review)

Scores are integers 1-10 on each of the FOUR official criteria. Every score carries:
rationale, evidence_surfaces, confidence (0-1). Reviewers use the full scale and these
band anchors verbatim. Category neutrality is absolute: a game, art piece, music tool,
CRM, weird toy, and developer tool are all eligible for 10/10 on every criterion if they
satisfy that criterion exceptionally well FOR WHAT THEY ARE TRYING TO BE.
No aesthetic penalties. Signs of hurried AI work matter only as actual product-quality
problems (incoherence, broken flow, placeholder content), never as style.

## WebMCP Leverage — what does WebMCP change for this product?
Judge the delta WebMCP makes to reliability, precision, structured state access,
shared human-agent state, repeatability, and capability. Tool COUNT is not leverage.
- 9-10 TRANSFORMATIVE: the intended experience is impractical without the structured
  agent surface; human and agent share live product state; the collaboration could not
  be replicated comparably well by a general agent driving the UI.
- 7-8 MAJOR DELTA: without WebMCP a general agent is markedly less reliable/precise
  here; the agent is central to the product, not a bolt-on.
- 5-6 MEANINGFUL DELTA: existing capability packaged into a genuinely better product
  experience via structured tools; same outcome otherwise possible with real friction.
- 3-4 MINOR DELTA: generic browser automation achieves comparable outcomes; WebMCP is
  a convenience layer.
- 1-2 COSMETIC: exposes an action a UI button already offers; no meaningful change.

## Execution — is the actual product coherent, intentional, complete for its scope?
Stage 1 scores EXECUTION_CLAIMED: coherence/intentionality/completeness as evidenced by
the packet (description + packaging + video frames/transcript). Thin evidence caps the
score (end-to-end credit requires end-to-end evidence) and lowers confidence.
Stage 2 may rescore as EXECUTION_OBSERVED; latest evidence-backed score replaces, never
sums.
- 9-10: all surfaces agree; central workflow demonstrably completes; states handled;
  feels finished for its stated scope.
- 7-8: strong coherence and intentionality; central workflow appears functional; minor
  unfinished edges.
- 5-6: plausibly real with visible gaps; scope unclear or core not demonstrated
  end-to-end.
- 3-4: thin prototype; contradictions between surfaces; placeholder content.
- 1-2: non-functional or incoherent for its claimed scope.

## Creativity & Ambition — novelty of concept and interaction model
Judge ONLY: novelty of the concept, novelty of the interaction model, originality
relative to existing concepts, ambition, memorable/surprising use of the medium, and
the depth with which the concept is pursued. WebMCP indispensability belongs under
WebMCP Leverage, NOT here: a project can be extremely creative even if another agent
API could theoretically have enabled something similar.
- 9-10: genuinely novel concept or interaction model; ambitious scope pursued deeply
  and coherently; memorable.
- 7-8: fresh synthesis or notably original interaction; ambitious for a hackathon.
- 5-6: competent take on a known category with a real new angle.
- 3-4: derivative; novelty mostly surface details.
- 1-2: clone/template with trivial changes.
"That the category exists" is never a penalty; judge the submitted concept.

## Potential Impact — credible value for the intended audience
Judge: is there a real audience; is there a real problem, desire, or use case for that
audience; does the demonstrated solution genuinely address it. NO commercial adoption,
monetization, market pull, or startup viability is required. Entertainment, play, art,
creative expression, learning, niche hobby use, experimentation, and community value
are legitimate forms of impact. A tiny audience fully justifies a top score.
- 9-10: specific real audience with a real problem/desire; solution demonstrably
  addresses it; credible value, appeal, usefulness, or meaning for that audience.
- 7-8: clear audience and problem, good fit, credible value.
- 5-6: identifiable audience, looser fit or partial address.
- 3-4: speculative audience or strained fit; impact mostly aspirational.
- 1-2: no coherent audience/problem pairing.
Audience size is NOT a multiplier.

## Aggregate & ranking
aggregate = leverage + execution + impact + creativity (4-40). Nothing else enters.
Tie-breaks: Leverage, then Execution, then Impact, then Creativity, then higher
combined evidence confidence, then slug alphabetical. See PROTOCOL.md Ranking section.

What it does Team Rocket Command Center is six AI apps behind one Next.js site: a kanban board whose cards are segments of one effort-weighted progress bar, a group restaurant picker decided by counting swipes, a policy diff that ranks changes by who they land on, a resume-to-posting gap finder with a two-week plan, an ambient note taker that files what you say, and a reader that illustrates a chapter scene by scene. Each runs on the visitor's own Gemini key. Every build is drivable by an AI agent through WebMCP. Each page registers its actions as tools on document.modelContext , 44 in all, and every tool calls the same function the button calls, so a person and an agent are always looking at the same page. An "Agent is driving" pill in the header names the running tool. A WebMCP button lists what an agent could do on the current page before one arrives. The full surface, with schemas, lives at teamrocket.website/webmcp and is rendered from the same definitions the pages register. Anything destructive stops at an in-page confirm bar and waits for a human. No tool can read, accept or set an API key. How we built it A single client hook, useWebMCPTools , registers a page's tools while it is mounted and unregisters them through the registration's AbortSignal on unmount. It reads document.modelContext , the current spec and Chrome 153+, falls back to navigator.modelContext for Chrome 149 to 152, and degrades to a console.debug where neither exists so the site works in every browser. Each build keeps a static tools.js with names, descriptions and JSON Schemas, and wires handlers in its component next to the state they touch. Handlers read live state through a ref, so a tool always sees the current board, deck or workspace without re-registering on every render. ok() and fail() build the result shape; failures always carry a next-step hint and nothing ever throws. Four site-wide tools live in the shell: list the builds, open one, check which keys are set as booleans, and open the key panel for the human to type into. One tool is declarative: the name form in This or That carries toolname , tooldescription , toolautosubmit and a toolparamdescription , and answers agent-invoked submits with respondWith() . Destructive tools call gate() , which blocks on a confirm bar in the shell and resolves when the human presses Confirm or Cancel, the agent aborts, or a minute passes. The toolactivated and toolcancel events drive the header pill. Everything is committed in small steps in the open, MIT licensed. Challenges we ran into The spec moved under us. Articles still said navigator.modelContext and unregisterTool ; the current draft says document.modelContext with an AbortSignal, and Chrome 149 and Chrome 153 disagree. We read the Chromium IDL rather than the blog posts and support both. Agents arrive through different doors. The Codex Chrome extension drives the DOM and never saw our tools, while Codex's in-app browser called them directly. The first agent to reach the site used the buttons, which is exactly why the human UI had to keep working. That agent also found a real bug. Giving plan() parameters for the tool meant React handed it the click event when a person pressed the button, and the event's DOM node broke JSON serialisation. One-line fix, and a good reminder that adding an agent path can break the human one. Smaller ones: React's compiler lint rejects setting state in effects, so adopting saved state after hydration needed a useSyncExternalStore pattern instead; and transcribing a three-minute demo in one pass drifted ten seconds by the end, so captions are now generated in silence-aligned chunks. Accomplishments that we're proud of 44 tools across six real multi-stage pipelines, not a to-do list, each with the screen as the record of what the agent did. A safety model you can see: the pill, the confirm bar, and zero ways for a tool to touch a key. A tool catalog generated from the same code that registers the tools, so the documentation cannot drift from what is live. A pattern small enough to copy: one hook, one tools file per page, handlers beside their state. A clean lint run, a compile-checked build, and a commit history that shows the work as it happened. What we learned WebMCP is at its best when it is a second door into an existing room rather than a new building. Because our tools call the button functions, every agent action is visible, undoable where the UI allows it, and testable by clicking. The places where we were tempted to add tool-only shortcuts were the places bugs appeared. Descriptions are the interface. An agent decides what to call from the description alone, so "when to use this" matters more than "what this does", and every error message is a chance to steer the next call. And the confirm bar earned its place. Watching an agent stop and wait for a person on camera made the case for human-in-the-loop better than any paragraph could. What's next for Team Rocket Command Center Re-record the six walkthroughs against the current builds, and add an agent-driven clip for each the way Taskboard has now. Give the site's own player a proper caption track. Add outputSchema to the tools once the spec lands it, and revisit readOnlyHint and consequentialHint as agents start honouring them. Then keep using the site as what it has become: a worked example anyone can fork to wire their own app the same way. Recovery Desk is a deterministic airline disruption-recovery simulation built to explore a question that matters far beyond aviation: What if a website did not expose every action to an AI agent all the time, but instead changed what the agent was allowed to do as the situation evolved? Inspiration Most agent demos focus on whether an AI can call a tool. I wanted to explore a harder problem: when should that tool exist at all? In high-stakes workflows, reading information, proposing a change, cancelling something, and committing a final decision should not carry the same authority. Recovery Desk uses an airline operations-control scenario to make that distinction visible. A fictional airline faces a hub closure and must recover its schedule while respecting aircraft position, crew duty, minimum turn time, and the disruption window. The airline scenario is the testbed. The core idea is state-governed WebMCP capabilities . What it does Recovery Desk exposes seven WebMCP tools: get_disruption get_schedule check_plan_legality propose_reassignment reset_draft cancel_flight commit_recovery_plan But they are not all available at the same time . The website moves through six operational states: DISRUPTED → DRAFTING → CANCELLATION_REQUIRED → DRAFT_LEGAL → AWAITING_APPROVAL → COMMITTED As state changes, the website dynamically changes the capabilities exposed through WebMCP. For example, cancel_flight is a destructive capability. It is not exposed by default. When the current plan leaves an unresolved flight with no feasible aircraft reassignment, the website can enter CANCELLATION_REQUIRED and expose cancel_flight . Once that destructive action is no longer appropriate, the capability is withdrawn. When the draft becomes legal, commit_recovery_plan becomes available. Calling it still does not commit the plan. It moves the workflow to AWAITING_APPROVAL and waits for an explicit human decision. Only human approval commits the recovery plan. Agent proposes → Website validates → Capabilities change → Human authorizes → System commits. How I built it Recovery Desk is a deterministic client-side application with the WebMCP layer separated from the recovery logic. The site registers tools through the browser's Model Context API and dynamically manages their availability according to the current application state. The recovery engine uses integer-minute scheduling and explicit rules for: hub closure aircraft position minimum aircraft turn time crew duty limits Agent mutations affect a draft , never the committed plan directly. Every mutating, destructive, or human-gated action re-checks the current phase at invocation time. This also provides stale-tool defense: if an agent attempts to invoke a capability that is no longer valid in the current state, the website rejects the call without performing the mutation. The application maintains an audit trail of tool calls, state transitions, rejected actions, approval requests, and final human decisions. The demo The deterministic recovery walkthrough deliberately creates and repairs operational conflicts. The operator retimes flights while the website continuously validates the evolving draft. Eventually, the current draft leaves MA442 without a feasible aircraft reassignment, causing the site to enter CANCELLATION_REQUIRED . At that point, cancel_flight dynamically appears. After the remaining recovery actions and cancellation of MA442 produce a legal draft, the destructive capability is withdrawn and commit_recovery_plan becomes available. The agent can request the commit, but the website opens a human approval gate. A human can Reject , leaving the committed schedule unchanged and preserving the draft for further work, or Approve , which is the only path that commits the recovery plan. After commitment, mutating capabilities disappear and the interface returns to three read-only WebMCP tools. Challenges The hardest part was not registering tools. It was defining authority boundaries between the agent, the website, and the human. I also learned that capability availability must be treated as part of application state rather than as a static tool list. Another challenge was keeping the demonstration deterministic enough that the same state transitions can be inspected and reproduced. The current feasibility helper intentionally evaluates aircraft reassignment against the current draft . It is not a global optimization engine and does not claim to exhaustively search every possible retiming, resequencing, aircraft, and crew combination. That limitation helped clarify the real purpose of the project: Recovery Desk is not presented as a production airline optimizer. It is a testbed for state-governed agent capabilities . What I learned WebMCP can represent more than a collection of functions that an agent can call. A website can use its own domain state to determine: what an agent may read, what it may propose, when a destructive capability becomes appropriate, when that capability must disappear, and where human authority must remain absolute. That suggests a broader pattern for agentic websites in finance, healthcare operations, enterprise administration, infrastructure, procurement, and other workflows where actions have different levels of consequence. Recovery Desk demonstrates a simple principle: giving an agent tools is useful; giving it the right tools at the right moment is safer and more powerful. Agent permission systems have not caught up — they let the tool exist and make it refuse. A refusal is a conversation, and a conversation can be argued with. Cleanroom closes the port. Move it and tools are unregistered live, toolchange fires, and an agent holding a handle it discovered thirty seconds ago gets this from the interface itself: NotFoundError: No tool named "sample_rows" is registered. Not a policy error from our code. The browser saying the capability does not exist. A firewall you cannot argue with, because there is nothing to argue with. Drop a CSV into the page and it is parsed and held only in your browser tab, on a page served with connect-src 'none' so it cannot make a network request of any kind. Up to eleven WebMCP tools let an agent profile, query, chart and audit that data — while the file itself never leaves your device and never reaches the agent. A running egress ledger keeps the balance in one line — 955 B kept local · 583 B released — and itemises every call beneath it. Why WebMCP is the right fit In one sentence: a server-side MCP integration is physically incapable of doing this, because the data is never on a server. WebMCP is the only mechanism by which an agent can compute over data that stays on the user's device. Four things make the WebMCP usage structural rather than decorative: 1. The tool surface is the privacy boundary, and the person sets it. query_dataset cannot return individual rows — its schema requires an aggregate and the engine has no raw projection path. The one tool that can reveal a record, sample_rows , is not registered at all until the person turns the dial to Raw , and even then it suspends inside execute until a human answers a dialog. At Sealed , every tool that computes from the data is withdrawn and the agent is left with three. No amount of persuasive text inside a dataset can talk past it. 2. Capabilities are registered and withdrawn live, driving toolchange . An empty page offers two tools. Loading a dataset makes six more appear. Turning the dial to Sealed takes five away; Raw adds one. Adding a report block reveals the block-editing tools. The agent's menu always describes what the app can actually do right now — and what the person is currently willing to let it do. 3. The boundary cannot be moved by an argument. The trust level and the k-anonymity threshold are read from the workspace on every call; an agent that passes minGroupSize or trustLevel is rejected for an unexpected property. The threshold ships at 5 rather than off, and applies to any answer computed from fewer than five records however the set got small — by grouping, by filtering, or by asking how many rows matched. A call that arrives after the dial moved but before the tool was withdrawn is refused by the runner. 4. Errors are designed for a model to recover from. A misspelled column returns the real column names; a numeric aggregate on a text column returns the list of numeric columns; a withdrawn tool tells the agent to call list_datasets , which reports the level in force. What people and agents can do together that was hard before The person supplies data they were never willing to upload, and decides with one control how much power the agent has over it. The agent explores it, runs statistics it could never do reliably by reading a rendered table, and builds a report. The canvas is genuinely two-sided. Charts and notes the agent creates land on the same surface the person writes on, each block badged with who made it. The person can write their own notes, edit the agent's wording in place, and remove either — and editing does not launder the authorship, so the badge stays honest. An agent's update_report_block and a person's Edit button are the same operation on the same store. When the agent needs something genuinely sensitive, the application stops it and asks — quoting the agent's own stated reason back to the person — and the person can change their mind mid-session by turning the dial, which the agent sees as its own tools changing. Neither could do this alone: the agent cannot see the file, and the person does not want to write the queries. How it is built TypeScript, React 19, Vite 8. No backend, no database, no API key, no telemetry. The entire application is static files. The containment guarantee is architectural, not a promise. The app is served with connect-src 'none' , removing the page's ability to make any network request — fetch , XHR , WebSocket , EventSource , sendBeacon . It is delivered both as an HTTP header and embedded in the built markup, so it travels with the artifact. An end-to-end test fires a real cross-origin fetch and asserts the browser blocks it. That policy has teeth: it cost us a dependency. Ajv compiles JSON Schemas with new Function , which script-src 'self' forbids, and every tool call failed in the production build. Rather than weaken the policy we replaced Ajv with a hand-written validator covering exactly the schema subset the tools use. Cleanroom now has no runtime dependencies beyond React . 529 automated tests — 460 unit and integration, 69 end-to-end. The E2E suite drives document.modelContext.getTools() and executeTool() from page context without importing our source, so it verifies what an agent actually receives — including that a tool handle captured at Raw is dead once the dial comes down, and that the browser reports a securitypolicyviolation rather than merely throwing when the page attempts to reach the network. Who it helps Anyone with a spreadsheet they cannot upload: finance and HR analysts, clinicians, teachers, small-business owners, journalists working with confidential material, and everyone at a company with a data-egress policy. Today their options are to break policy or to go without. This is a third option. More broadly, it is a worked example of a pattern the agentic web needs: a page that gives an agent real capability while keeping the authority — over what is released, how much, and what cannot be undone — with the person, and expresses that authority as the set of tools the agent is offered. What is new here The trust dial treats WebMCP registration as a policy surface. Instead of a tool that consults a setting and refuses, the setting decides whether the tool exists. An agent cannot ask for what it has not been offered, and the person can watch the offer change. The egress ledger is a live account of every byte an agent has received from your data, summed against the bytes that never left — kept local · released — and itemised beneath, including the requests it made and was refused. It turns a privacy claim into an audit log.
