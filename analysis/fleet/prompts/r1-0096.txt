# FROZEN SCORING RUBRIC (pre-registered before any Stage 1 review)

Scores are integers 1-10 on each of the FOUR official criteria. Every score carries:
rationale, evidence_surfaces, confidence (0-1). Reviewers use the full scale and these
band anchors verbatim. Category neutrality is absolute: a game, art piece, music tool,
CRM, weird toy, and developer tool are all eligible for 10/10 on every criterion if they
satisfy that criterion exceptionally well FOR WHAT THEY ARE TRYING TO BE.
No aesthetic penalties. Signs of hurried AI work matter only as actual product-quality
problems (incoherence, broken flow, placeholder content), never as style.

## WebMCP Leverage — what does WebMCP change for this product?
Judge the delta WebMCP makes to reliability, precision, structured state access,
shared human-agent state, repeatability, and capability. Tool COUNT is not leverage.
- 9-10 TRANSFORMATIVE: the intended experience is impractical without the structured
  agent surface; human and agent share live product state; the collaboration could not
  be replicated comparably well by a general agent driving the UI.
- 7-8 MAJOR DELTA: without WebMCP a general agent is markedly less reliable/precise
  here; the agent is central to the product, not a bolt-on.
- 5-6 MEANINGFUL DELTA: existing capability packaged into a genuinely better product
  experience via structured tools; same outcome otherwise possible with real friction.
- 3-4 MINOR DELTA: generic browser automation achieves comparable outcomes; WebMCP is
  a convenience layer.
- 1-2 COSMETIC: exposes an action a UI button already offers; no meaningful change.

## Execution — is the actual product coherent, intentional, complete for its scope?
Stage 1 scores EXECUTION_CLAIMED: coherence/intentionality/completeness as evidenced by
the packet (description + packaging + video frames/transcript). Thin evidence caps the
score (end-to-end credit requires end-to-end evidence) and lowers confidence.
Stage 2 may rescore as EXECUTION_OBSERVED; latest evidence-backed score replaces, never
sums.
- 9-10: all surfaces agree; central workflow demonstrably completes; states handled;
  feels finished for its stated scope.
- 7-8: strong coherence and intentionality; central workflow appears functional; minor
  unfinished edges.
- 5-6: plausibly real with visible gaps; scope unclear or core not demonstrated
  end-to-end.
- 3-4: thin prototype; contradictions between surfaces; placeholder content.
- 1-2: non-functional or incoherent for its claimed scope.

## Creativity & Ambition — novelty of concept and interaction model
Judge ONLY: novelty of the concept, novelty of the interaction model, originality
relative to existing concepts, ambition, memorable/surprising use of the medium, and
the depth with which the concept is pursued. WebMCP indispensability belongs under
WebMCP Leverage, NOT here: a project can be extremely creative even if another agent
API could theoretically have enabled something similar.
- 9-10: genuinely novel concept or interaction model; ambitious scope pursued deeply
  and coherently; memorable.
- 7-8: fresh synthesis or notably original interaction; ambitious for a hackathon.
- 5-6: competent take on a known category with a real new angle.
- 3-4: derivative; novelty mostly surface details.
- 1-2: clone/template with trivial changes.
"That the category exists" is never a penalty; judge the submitted concept.

## Potential Impact — credible value for the intended audience
Judge: is there a real audience; is there a real problem, desire, or use case for that
audience; does the demonstrated solution genuinely address it. NO commercial adoption,
monetization, market pull, or startup viability is required. Entertainment, play, art,
creative expression, learning, niche hobby use, experimentation, and community value
are legitimate forms of impact. A tiny audience fully justifies a top score.
- 9-10: specific real audience with a real problem/desire; solution demonstrably
  addresses it; credible value, appeal, usefulness, or meaning for that audience.
- 7-8: clear audience and problem, good fit, credible value.
- 5-6: identifiable audience, looser fit or partial address.
- 3-4: speculative audience or strained fit; impact mostly aspirational.
- 1-2: no coherent audience/problem pairing.
Audience size is NOT a multiplier.

## Aggregate & ranking
aggregate = leverage + execution + impact + creativity (4-40). Nothing else enters.
Tie-breaks: Leverage, then Execution, then Impact, then Creativity, then higher
combined evidence confidence, then slug alphabetical. See PROTOCOL.md Ranking section.

Like Comment Story Updates Inspiration I wanted a small, concrete domain to actually test what WebMCP makes possible, instead of a demo that just claims an agent did something. Planning a night out — a film, then dinner after — is a real multi-constraint problem: the showtime fixes the cinema, the cinema fixes the route, the route decides how much time is really left before the table, and every one of those choices moves the total cost. It's exactly the kind of thing an agent is good at grinding through and a person is good at approving. Most "agentic" demos I'd seen were either a chat box bolted onto a UI it couldn't see, or an agent with unchecked authority to just click "book." I wanted the version where the agent gets real capabilities and the person still holds the only button that matters. What it does PlanOnIt plans a real evening out in Dhaka — a film, the trip across the city, and dinner after — with an AI agent doing the tedious cross-referencing through 13 WebMCP tools registered directly on the page. The agent can search restaurants and showtimes, build a plan, check feasibility, calculate cost, and repair a plan a human edit just broke. It can never approve or reserve anything — that stays a real click in the UI, every time. Once approved, reservation is a genuine sandboxed transaction: a compare-and-swap against shared inventory, idempotent on retry, and it fails closed on conflict instead of quietly double-booking. How we built it React 19 + TypeScript + Vite on the frontend, Zod for runtime validation, deployed as a static build to Vercel. The core decision was to build one domain layer ( src/domain.ts ) that both the human UI and the WebMCP tools call into — the same solver, the same nine feasibility checks, the same versioned mutations — so there's never a "friendly explanation for the person" that can quietly drift from "the raw check the agent gets." Every mutation carries an expectedVersion ; a stale write fails loudly with STALE_PLAN_VERSION instead of silently overwriting someone else's edit, whether that edit came from a human or another agent call. Same-origin tabs stay in sync through a Web Locks-backed compare-and-swap. Inventory sits inside a rolling two-week window computed off the real clock, so the demo never goes stale no matter when a judge opens it. Challenges we ran into The one I'm least proud of, and gladdest I caught before submitting: late in the build I found that a human could never manually pick a transport option — every click failed with "Transport option does not belong to the selected venue route." The agent's tools worked fine, and so did the auto-repair path, which made it easy to miss. It turned out the manual UI was resolving the cinema-to-restaurant route with the two location IDs swapped, and since each direction generates its own distinct set of transport-option IDs, the buttons on screen were always offering IDs from the wrong route object. It only ever broke the human path — a good reminder that "the agent's tools work" and "the manual UI works" are two separate claims, and testing one doesn't verify the other. I added a regression test that reproduces the exact failure so it can't come back silently. Building a reservation that's an actual transaction, not just a returned success string, was its own challenge: idempotency keys are bound to the reservation's content fingerprint, not just a request ID, so a retry after a dropped connection can't accidentally create a second booking, and a replayed or forged confirmation can't be pointed at a different reservation. And a last-minute one that had nothing to do with code: my original LICENSE restricted commercial use without permission, which meant it wasn't actually an open-source license, even though I'd always intended it to be. Caught and fixed to MIT before submission — "open source" has to be literally detectable, not just intended. Accomplishments that we're proud of 198 automated tests plus 21 real-browser Playwright tests, covering adversarial input, concurrency, the full reservation lifecycle, and idempotency — not just the happy path. The evaluation-symmetry property: the UI's "why isn't this plan valid yet" and the agent's validate_plan tool result are always the same function call against the same state. There's no second copy of the business logic that could quietly disagree with the first. Approval isn't gated by a permission check the agent could theoretically satisfy — it's architecturally absent from the tool surface. No WebMCP tool touches approval at all. Shipping with no login requirement, on purpose. It would have been easy to bolt on accounts to look more "complete," but this build doesn't actually need cross-device sync to do its job, so I didn't fake it. That documentation drifts fast: numbers like "12 tools" or "eight checks" written once during development go stale the moment the code moves on, and are worth re-deriving from the running code rather than trusting memory. And that the strongest trust story for an agentic feature isn't a more polite prompt — it's an architectural boundary the agent literally cannot cross, like approval never being expressed as a tool at all. ABOUT TEXT (untrusted evidence):
How I Choose is a local-first rehearsal workspace that checks whether a communication partner followed a person's self-authored protocol-never evaluating the person. Like Comment Story Updates Inspiration Important conversations often become harder when speech, language, or processing gets difficult. Yet many communication tools still ask the person to perform better: answer faster, choose from someone else's categories, or fit a fixed conversational rhythm. We wanted to reverse that responsibility. How I Choose asks whether the communication partner adapted to the person—not whether the person performed correctly. The person defines what helps, what each signal means, how much time is needed, and what a partner must do next. The software turns those choices into a protocol that can be rehearsed and audited without inferring meaning from silence or delay. What it does How I Choose is a local-first communication rehearsal workspace for adults. A person authors their preferred channels, wording, pace, processing time, literal-language rules, semantic signals, and partner actions. They also choose exactly which fields an active browser agent may read. The workspace supports both human-only practice and an optional WebMCP agent rehearsal. Before any partner turn becomes visible, a deterministic engine checks it against the current protocol. It enforces one question at a time, word and option limits, channel rules, pending-signal acknowledgment, Pause, terminal Stop, revision freshness, conflicts, and idempotency. The person directly selects every semantic signal, including yes, no, not sure, more information, more time, rephrase, Pause, and Stop. Silence creates no event and never advances the rehearsal. Reports contain evidence about the partner's adherence and repairs ; they never score the person. The complete human-facing product includes: guided onboarding plus blank and clearly labeled synthetic profiles; profile, signal, and per-field agent-access editing; a large keyboard-accessible signal board; low-stakes rehearsal templates and partner-turn validation; partner-adherence audit and revision history; staged protocol suggestions with exact diffs and provenance; JSON import/export and a printable support guide; visible owner-only review and ratification; text-size, contrast, Quiet, and Plain language preferences. Why WebMCP This workflow needs an agent to operate inside the person's current, visible, revisioned protocol , not inside a detached chat containing copied instructions. WebMCP lets ChatGPT use the page's existing permissions, validator, state machine, and evidence while the person retains direct control of signals, rule changes, and approval. The workspace starts in Human-only mode. All eight Site tools fail closed until the person visibly enables Agent rehearsal. Once enabled, ChatGPT can read only allowlisted fields, audit readiness, start an already approved scenario, offer a validated partner turn, acknowledge the exact person-selected signal, inspect its own adherence, stage a draft improvement, and verify guide derivation. There is deliberately no tool for selecting a person's signal, answering for them, resuming, ratifying, publishing, sharing, exporting private data, deleting a profile, or assessing capacity, consent, diagnosis, comprehension, safety, or coercion. How we built it The app is a static Next.js and React application written in strict TypeScript. Dexie/IndexedDB is the durable local source of truth; Zustand holds hydrated read projections and transient preferences. Zod validates every persisted document, import, command, and Site-tool payload. Human controls and WebMCP handlers use the same application-service command path and pure domain logic. Compare-and-swap revisions, idempotency fingerprints, events, and state writes happen atomically in one IndexedDB transaction. Ratified profile snapshots are immutable and identified by canonical hashes. The top-level /demo/ page feature-detects WebMCP and imperatively registers a stable catalog through document.modelContext.registerTool() : get_rehearsal_brief audit_rehearsal_readiness start_approved_rehearsal offer_partner_turn read_latest_signal get_rehearsal_report stage_protocol_patch verify_support_guide A singleton guard prevents duplicate registration under React Strict Mode. Every JSON Schema is bounded and closed with additionalProperties: false , then Zod validates again at the application boundary. Mutations require expected revisions and an idempotency key. Handlers resolve current IndexedDB state at invocation time instead of trusting stale UI state. The quality system combines Vitest, Testing Library, fast-check property tests, Playwright desktop and narrow-viewport suites, axe-core checks, dependency and secret audits, a static production build, and GitHub Actions. The release includes 76 unit, service, property, import/export, and agent-flow tests plus a complete 15-step browser judge path. Challenges we faced Treating discovery and authorization as separate problems. Registering a tool successfully does not mean it should be authorized in the current state. A real-browser test exposed an early version where Human-only was only presentation state. We fixed this by making owner-controlled agent access durable, revisioned session state and enforcing it inside every handler. Keeping agent authority physically narrow. Removing dangerous fields from a broad object was not enough. We created allowlisted agent DTOs and a separate AgentRehearsalService that cannot import owner-only ratification, signal selection, export, share, delete, or resume operations. Making concurrent changes recoverable. A person may change a rule while an agent is preparing a turn. Expected revisions cause the stale write to fail instead of overwriting the person's newer choice; the agent can reread the brief and adapt. Building safety without making clinical claims. The product is deliberately scoped to synthetic and low-stakes adult communication practice. It does not infer answers, assess a person, or present itself as a consent system, medical device, emergency plan, or legal instrument. Accomplishments that we're proud of One deterministic engine governs both human and agent partner turns. Human-only access fails closed, while owner-enabled agent access remains field-scoped and revisioned. Pause is immediately effective and Stop is terminal—no agent override exists. Stale revisions, duplicate requests, invalid turns, repair, signals, and provenance are visible and recoverable. Private notes are structurally absent from agent projections, and receipts contain metadata rather than authored conversation text. The full human product works without an account, backend, API key, remote database, analytics, or runtime external model call. The deployed production build was exercised in ChatGPT's real built-in browser: all eight tools were discovered and the guarded workflow, including Human-only denial and visible owner-enabled access, was verified. What we learned WebMCP is most useful when it exposes an existing trustworthy application boundary rather than creating a second automation-only path. Tool discovery, state authorization, validation, persistence, and visible evidence all need independent treatment. We also learned that “local-first” needs precise disclosure. Profiles and rehearsals stay in local, unencrypted IndexedDB, but fields explicitly exposed through Site tools are processed by the active browser agent. The interface says that plainly. Most importantly, semantic safety becomes more reliable when it is structural: the agent cannot manufacture a person-authored signal, Stop cannot be reversed, silence cannot become an answer, and suggestions remain drafts until the person reviews them.
