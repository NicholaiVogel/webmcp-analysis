# FROZEN SCORING RUBRIC (pre-registered before any Stage 1 review)

Scores are integers 1-10 on each of the FOUR official criteria. Every score carries:
rationale, evidence_surfaces, confidence (0-1). Reviewers use the full scale and these
band anchors verbatim. Category neutrality is absolute: a game, art piece, music tool,
CRM, weird toy, and developer tool are all eligible for 10/10 on every criterion if they
satisfy that criterion exceptionally well FOR WHAT THEY ARE TRYING TO BE.
No aesthetic penalties. Signs of hurried AI work matter only as actual product-quality
problems (incoherence, broken flow, placeholder content), never as style.

## WebMCP Leverage — what does WebMCP change for this product?
Judge the delta WebMCP makes to reliability, precision, structured state access,
shared human-agent state, repeatability, and capability. Tool COUNT is not leverage.
- 9-10 TRANSFORMATIVE: the intended experience is impractical without the structured
  agent surface; human and agent share live product state; the collaboration could not
  be replicated comparably well by a general agent driving the UI.
- 7-8 MAJOR DELTA: without WebMCP a general agent is markedly less reliable/precise
  here; the agent is central to the product, not a bolt-on.
- 5-6 MEANINGFUL DELTA: existing capability packaged into a genuinely better product
  experience via structured tools; same outcome otherwise possible with real friction.
- 3-4 MINOR DELTA: generic browser automation achieves comparable outcomes; WebMCP is
  a convenience layer.
- 1-2 COSMETIC: exposes an action a UI button already offers; no meaningful change.

## Execution — is the actual product coherent, intentional, complete for its scope?
Stage 1 scores EXECUTION_CLAIMED: coherence/intentionality/completeness as evidenced by
the packet (description + packaging + video frames/transcript). Thin evidence caps the
score (end-to-end credit requires end-to-end evidence) and lowers confidence.
Stage 2 may rescore as EXECUTION_OBSERVED; latest evidence-backed score replaces, never
sums.
- 9-10: all surfaces agree; central workflow demonstrably completes; states handled;
  feels finished for its stated scope.
- 7-8: strong coherence and intentionality; central workflow appears functional; minor
  unfinished edges.
- 5-6: plausibly real with visible gaps; scope unclear or core not demonstrated
  end-to-end.
- 3-4: thin prototype; contradictions between surfaces; placeholder content.
- 1-2: non-functional or incoherent for its claimed scope.

## Creativity & Ambition — novelty of concept and interaction model
Judge ONLY: novelty of the concept, novelty of the interaction model, originality
relative to existing concepts, ambition, memorable/surprising use of the medium, and
the depth with which the concept is pursued. WebMCP indispensability belongs under
WebMCP Leverage, NOT here: a project can be extremely creative even if another agent
API could theoretically have enabled something similar.
- 9-10: genuinely novel concept or interaction model; ambitious scope pursued deeply
  and coherently; memorable.
- 7-8: fresh synthesis or notably original interaction; ambitious for a hackathon.
- 5-6: competent take on a known category with a real new angle.
- 3-4: derivative; novelty mostly surface details.
- 1-2: clone/template with trivial changes.
"That the category exists" is never a penalty; judge the submitted concept.

## Potential Impact — credible value for the intended audience
Judge: is there a real audience; is there a real problem, desire, or use case for that
audience; does the demonstrated solution genuinely address it. NO commercial adoption,
monetization, market pull, or startup viability is required. Entertainment, play, art,
creative expression, learning, niche hobby use, experimentation, and community value
are legitimate forms of impact. A tiny audience fully justifies a top score.
- 9-10: specific real audience with a real problem/desire; solution demonstrably
  addresses it; credible value, appeal, usefulness, or meaning for that audience.
- 7-8: clear audience and problem, good fit, credible value.
- 5-6: identifiable audience, looser fit or partial address.
- 3-4: speculative audience or strained fit; impact mostly aspirational.
- 1-2: no coherent audience/problem pairing.
Audience size is NOT a multiplier.

## Aggregate & ranking
aggregate = leverage + execution + impact + creativity (4-40). Nothing else enters.
Tie-breaks: Leverage, then Execution, then Impact, then Creativity, then higher
combined evidence confidence, then slug alphabetical. See PROTOCOL.md Ranking section.

Write: propose_marks (recognition findings for the page to compute), set_marking_emphasis (raise caution, never silently lower it), request_release (stage a releasable set for human review). Six carry readOnlyHint ; read_answer also carries untrustedContentHint because its body is student content, not an instruction. Every write includes an expectedRevision and a single-use opaque operationId . Old reads are refused as stale-revision ; replayed operations are refused as duplicate-operation without a second receipt or revision. A shared reply() path checks both successful results and refusals, including a second scan for page-owned numbers that might escape in prose. The agent names rubric ids; the page maps them to points. The stack is React 19.2.8, TypeScript and Vite. Arithmetic belongs to src/domain/marks.ts ; hold policy, revisions and receipts belong to src/domain/session.ts ; teacher and agent projections are separated in src/domain/views.ts . There is no backend or network dependency. The production build also uses a strict nine-directive CSP without 'unsafe-inline' . Verified on this machine: 136 tests; 43 browser checks; 17 checks of the agent projection; 19 WebMCP registry/dispatch checks; and 27 refusal and recovery checks. The browser checks include layout, CSP, focus, the human decline/confirm path, responsive behaviour and measured contrast. The agent projection contains none of the 13 page-owned figures, while the teacher's view still contains them. The same browser and WebMCP probes were also run against the recorded live URL, https://androlay.github.io/withheld/ , in Chrome 151.0.7922.137 with the WebMCP testing flags. Those runs are kept in docs/evidence/ with their URL, browser and run metadata. The deterministic dispatch checks were composed by scripts that already knew the tool names and arguments. They show that the page's surface works from outside the page; they should not be confused with proof that a native production host discovered it on its own. Challenges we ran into The hardest part was not adding tools; it was choosing what the agent must never learn. A helpful summary can leak a total. A “close to the boundary” hint can reveal which side. A list of held answers can reveal an identity. Those risks pushed the boundary checks into code instead of leaving them as prompt instructions. The CSP created a smaller but very practical problem. Once 'unsafe-inline' was removed, the progress bars could no longer use inline widths. I moved them into stylesheet classes and kept the policy under test, so the security claim is something the browser checks rather than something the README merely promises. WebMCP itself is still an emerging browser surface. The current probes use Chromium's testing interface and a small bridge, which is useful for exercising the contract but is not the same as a shipped consumer host. Favor: Turn Telegram Work Into Trusted Deals Inspiration Online work already happens in Telegram. A photographer shows recent shoots in a channel. A tutor finds students in a neighborhood chat. A lawyer gets clients through recommendations. A designer sends a portfolio in private messages. Clients post requests in communities and ask friends whom they trust. The conversation is easy. The handoff into actual work is not. Useful posts disappear in the feed, terms stay scattered across messages, and a completed job often leaves no portable proof beyond "thank you" in an old chat. People do not need another place to talk. They need a reliable way to turn existing conversations into work that is searchable, structured, and trustworthy. That is why we built Favor: a Telegram-native marketplace where a post can become an opportunity, an opportunity can become a deal, and a completed deal can become reputation. What it does Favor serves both sides of online work. A professional can turn a public Telegram post into a service offer, then add a price, deadline, category, portfolio, and terms. A client can turn a post describing what they need into a client request. People can search both types, inspect profiles, ratings, reviews, portfolios, and completed work, then manage the deal within the Telegram flow. Optional on-chain escrow in TON or USDT can protect payment when both sides want it. For the WebMCP Challenge, we made this live workflow actionable for browser agents through four focused tools: favor_search_contracts searches public service offers and client requests by text, category, budget, deadline, rating, and escrow support. favor_get_contract returns one listing's terms, author reputation, reviews, completed-deal signals, and Favor URL. favor_read_telegram_post reads a public Telegram work post through Favor's existing import pipeline and returns its text and media metadata. favor_prepare_contract_draft turns the result into a draft with a Telegram review link that expires after 24 hours. A user can ask: "Find a TikTok promotion specialist under $300 who supports escrow, compare the strongest options, and turn my Telegram brief into a client request." The agent handles discovery and preparation. The user checks every field and decides whether to publish. How we built it Favor is a production Next.js 16 and React 19 application written in TypeScript, with Prisma and PostgreSQL, Zod validation, a Telegram Mini App and bot, and TON infrastructure for optional escrow. The product, its users, marketplace, deals, reputation system, and Telegram import flow existed before this challenge. During the challenge, we added a WebMCP extension using the imperative API. An application-level provider detects document.modelContext and registers the four tools once. This is progressive enhancement: without WebMCP, Favor works exactly as before. Registration failures are isolated, and React lifecycle cleanup removes registered tools safely. The tools reuse Favor's production clients and APIs instead of duplicating product logic. Inputs have strict JSON schemas and are validated again with Zod at execution time. Contract identifiers must match Favor's generated slug format and are URL-encoded before requests. Cancellation signals reach the network layer, so stopping a tool stops its underlying work. Trust boundaries are explicit. Public discovery tools declare readOnlyHint: true . Listings, reviews, and imported Telegram text declare untrustedContentHint: true , because user-written content must never become agent instructions. Search is rate-limited and scans a bounded candidate set. The only state-changing WebMCP tool creates a visible, expiring draft for human review. It cannot publish a contract, start a deal, fund escrow, release payment, or issue a refund. Those actions remain in explicit user-controlled Favor flows. Challenges we ran into The hardest problem was not registering a tool. It was deciding where an agent's authority should end. Turning a loose Telegram post into structured work is exactly where an agent helps, but the same flow touches identity, publishing, agreements, and eventually money. Full autonomy would look impressive in a demo and be wrong for a real marketplace. We designed the 24-hour review handoff so the agent removes repetitive work without impersonating the user or committing funds. Marketplace text and reviews created a second challenge. They are valuable context, but also untrusted user content. We had to keep responses concise, label content correctly, restrict slugs, validate inputs twice, rate-limit search, and preserve cancellation across every layer. Finally, this was not a greenfield prototype. We integrated WebMCP into a live Telegram product without breaking unsupported browsers or creating duplicate business rules that could drift from production. Accomplishments that we're proud of The demo uses the live Favor product, not a scripted chatbot over sample data. It shows Favor's four registered tools and a successful production marketplace search; we also verified live contract inspection and Telegram-post import. The private 24-hour draft handoff is implemented, while WebMCP registration, execution, cancellation, and cleanup are covered by automated tests. We deliberately did not create test data in the production account used for the read-only demo. The tools are small enough to be safe and composable, but together cover the tedious path from "I need help" to "this is ready for me to approve." Favor remains fully usable without WebMCP, while a compatible browser gains structured capabilities immediately. We are also proud of the boundary we did not cross. Every public or financial commitment stays human-controlled. Automated tests cover feature detection, registration, execution, cancellation, cleanup, and partial registration failure, alongside Favor's wider contract test suite and production build. To be transparent about challenge scope: Favor predates the event. The challenge-period work is the WebMCP runtime, four production-backed tools, application-level registration, schemas and safety annotations, tests, and documentation that make the existing product agent-ready. The exact source footprint and production checks are documented in the public challenge guide . What we learned The best agent integration is not a second interface pasted over a product. It is a precise set of capabilities attached to the product's existing source of truth. Small tools compose better than one oversized "do everything" tool. Search, inspect, import, and prepare each have a clear contract and permission level. We also learned that human approval is not friction to eliminate when identity, reputation, or money is involved. It is part of the product value. WebMCP can make a browser agent useful inside a real application without making the application dependent on the agent. That balance let us ship the extension into Favor instead of maintaining a separate hackathon demo. What's next for Favor Next we will improve intent-aware ranking and comparisons, expand the reputation signals agents can explain, and learn from real search-to-draft journeys. We want more kinds of Telegram posts to enter Favor while preserving the visible review step. As WebMCP evolves, we plan to expose more preparation and decision support around proposals and deal terms. Publication, deal acceptance, and every escrow action will continue to require explicit human approval. Try the live product at favor.deals or open @FavorDealsBot in Telegram.
