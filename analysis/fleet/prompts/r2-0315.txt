# FROZEN SCORING RUBRIC (pre-registered before any Stage 1 review)

Scores are integers 1-10 on each of the FOUR official criteria. Every score carries:
rationale, evidence_surfaces, confidence (0-1). Reviewers use the full scale and these
band anchors verbatim. Category neutrality is absolute: a game, art piece, music tool,
CRM, weird toy, and developer tool are all eligible for 10/10 on every criterion if they
satisfy that criterion exceptionally well FOR WHAT THEY ARE TRYING TO BE.
No aesthetic penalties. Signs of hurried AI work matter only as actual product-quality
problems (incoherence, broken flow, placeholder content), never as style.

## WebMCP Leverage — what does WebMCP change for this product?
Judge the delta WebMCP makes to reliability, precision, structured state access,
shared human-agent state, repeatability, and capability. Tool COUNT is not leverage.
- 9-10 TRANSFORMATIVE: the intended experience is impractical without the structured
  agent surface; human and agent share live product state; the collaboration could not
  be replicated comparably well by a general agent driving the UI.
- 7-8 MAJOR DELTA: without WebMCP a general agent is markedly less reliable/precise
  here; the agent is central to the product, not a bolt-on.
- 5-6 MEANINGFUL DELTA: existing capability packaged into a genuinely better product
  experience via structured tools; same outcome otherwise possible with real friction.
- 3-4 MINOR DELTA: generic browser automation achieves comparable outcomes; WebMCP is
  a convenience layer.
- 1-2 COSMETIC: exposes an action a UI button already offers; no meaningful change.

## Execution — is the actual product coherent, intentional, complete for its scope?
Stage 1 scores EXECUTION_CLAIMED: coherence/intentionality/completeness as evidenced by
the packet (description + packaging + video frames/transcript). Thin evidence caps the
score (end-to-end credit requires end-to-end evidence) and lowers confidence.
Stage 2 may rescore as EXECUTION_OBSERVED; latest evidence-backed score replaces, never
sums.
- 9-10: all surfaces agree; central workflow demonstrably completes; states handled;
  feels finished for its stated scope.
- 7-8: strong coherence and intentionality; central workflow appears functional; minor
  unfinished edges.
- 5-6: plausibly real with visible gaps; scope unclear or core not demonstrated
  end-to-end.
- 3-4: thin prototype; contradictions between surfaces; placeholder content.
- 1-2: non-functional or incoherent for its claimed scope.

## Creativity & Ambition — novelty of concept and interaction model
Judge ONLY: novelty of the concept, novelty of the interaction model, originality
relative to existing concepts, ambition, memorable/surprising use of the medium, and
the depth with which the concept is pursued. WebMCP indispensability belongs under
WebMCP Leverage, NOT here: a project can be extremely creative even if another agent
API could theoretically have enabled something similar.
- 9-10: genuinely novel concept or interaction model; ambitious scope pursued deeply
  and coherently; memorable.
- 7-8: fresh synthesis or notably original interaction; ambitious for a hackathon.
- 5-6: competent take on a known category with a real new angle.
- 3-4: derivative; novelty mostly surface details.
- 1-2: clone/template with trivial changes.
"That the category exists" is never a penalty; judge the submitted concept.

## Potential Impact — credible value for the intended audience
Judge: is there a real audience; is there a real problem, desire, or use case for that
audience; does the demonstrated solution genuinely address it. NO commercial adoption,
monetization, market pull, or startup viability is required. Entertainment, play, art,
creative expression, learning, niche hobby use, experimentation, and community value
are legitimate forms of impact. A tiny audience fully justifies a top score.
- 9-10: specific real audience with a real problem/desire; solution demonstrably
  addresses it; credible value, appeal, usefulness, or meaning for that audience.
- 7-8: clear audience and problem, good fit, credible value.
- 5-6: identifiable audience, looser fit or partial address.
- 3-4: speculative audience or strained fit; impact mostly aspirational.
- 1-2: no coherent audience/problem pairing.
Audience size is NOT a multiplier.

## Aggregate & ranking
aggregate = leverage + execution + impact + creativity (4-40). Nothing else enters.
Tie-breaks: Leverage, then Execution, then Impact, then Creativity, then higher
combined evidence confidence, then slug alphabetical. See PROTOCOL.md Ranking section.

What it does Each demo walks you through a conventional user interface, and then cordons off a bounded portion of it as WebMCP to redesign (the Live Canvas) The Storefront demo lets the agent curate the search results with a toolbox of approved card presets and badges. How we built it The architecture north star is "agent composition, application execution". The application exposes its UI primitives through WebMCP. What we learned WebMCP shows great potential to break interfaces out of the "ai chatbot jail". Like Comment Story Updates Agent Riff — Play a Musical Idea Together Live Musical Conversation in the webpage Human Plays the First Phrase Agent Responds with Its Own Phrase Life in Tech is already complicated, so many tools and frameworks to learn each day and tons of stress to deal with. So why not add more stress to your life when you find out your agent is better than you at playing music!! Inspiration for this Project Most AI music tools work in roughly the same way: you describe what you want, wait for the model, and receive a finished piece of music. We wanted to try somethingball that felt more collaborative. Instead of asking an AI to make music for us, we asked a different question: could a browser agent actually play music with us? That idea became Agent Riff , a musical call-and-response experience between a person and their browser agent. The person plays a short phrase, the agent receives the notes and timing through WebMCP, and then responds with a phrase of its own. The goal was not to create another song generator. We wanted the agent to feel like a second musician who listens before deciding what to play. What it does Agent Riff is a playable browser instrument built for live human and AI collaboration. A person can play notes using a mouse, touchscreen, or the keys on their computer keyboard. The application captures the performance as structured musical events, including the notes, timing, tempo, key, scale, instrument, register, and density. The browser agent can then: Join the current session Analyze the latest human phrase Choose a complementary instrument and register Perform a precisely timed response Wait for the person to play another phrase Change the shared tempo, key, or scale when requested Perform longer musical sets efficiently Agent Riff does not use the microphone or record the user's audio. The agent receives the musical information already created inside the application. This gives it accurate notes and timing without asking it to interpret sound or guess what happened from a screenshot. Human and agent performances appear in a shared live score. Each completed phrase is also saved as a clickable card. A person can replay their own phrase, replay the agent's response, or play both simultaneously to hear how well they work together. The agent can remain available for up to ten minutes between turns. This means the person can continue playing without repeatedly returning to the chat and typing "listen again." If they want to stop sooner, they can release the agent directly from the interface. How we built it Agent Riff is built with vanilla JavaScript, HTML, and CSS. All instrument sounds are synthesized directly in the browser using the Web Audio API. There are no recorded samples, user accounts, external music services, or runtime dependencies. A person can open the page and begin playing immediately. The application exposes seven WebMCP tools through document.modelContext.registerTool() : riff_join_session riff_get_session_state riff_listen riff_wait_for_human_phrase riff_perform_phrase riff_perform_set riff_set_compass These tools give the agent musical abilities rather than generic browser controls. For example, the agent does not need to find individual piano keys on the screen and click them at the correct moment. It can call riff_listen to receive an analysis of the recent performance, then call riff_perform_phrase with the notes, durations, timing offsets, instrument, and velocity of its response. The performance tool supports sequential notes, rests, chords, overlapping notes, and exact beat-relative timing. For speed, the agent can use a compact score format instead of sending a large object for every note. The waiting tool keeps the call-and-response session active. When the person finishes another phrase, the tool returns both the new notes and the analysis needed for the next response. This lets the agent perform directly without making an additional listening call. We also created riff_perform_set for requests involving complete songs or multiple sections. It can schedule several public-domain or custom pieces in one call, reducing repeated model and browser round trips. Every tool uses a bounded JSON Schema, and all inputs are validated again during execution. The project also includes automated tests for musical analysis, input constraints, WebMCP registration, tool behavior, cleanup, and the production deployment artifact. Challenges The first version technically worked, but it did not feel much like a live musical interaction. The agent would inspect the session, analyze the notes, think about its response, call another tool, play a very short phrase, and then return to the chat. The delay between turns was long enough that it was easy to forget what had just been played. We reduced that delay by combining related information. The waiting tool now returns the completed phrase together with its musical analysis and a suggested response shape. We also added compact score notation so a complete response can be sent in one efficient tool call. Response quality created another tradeoff. Faster models replied more quickly, but their musical answers were often only a few notes long or sounded like random keyboard input. More capable models produced better phrases, but took longer to respond. We improved this by giving the agent clearer musical guidance. Normal responses are encouraged to develop across roughly 12 to 16 notes, with an echo of the human idea, a variation, and a resolution. Dense human phrases receive shorter or more spacious answers so the agent does not overcrowd the music. We also had to account for ChatGPT's side-by-side browser layout. A design that looked good in a full browser window became extremely tall and difficult to use when the browser occupied only half the screen. We redesigned the responsive layout so the instrument, score, activity, agent status, and copied prompt remain accessible in that narrower configuration. Long waits created a separate usability problem. People needed a clear way to tell whether the agent was still present and how to stop it. We added visible agent status, a bounded waiting period, and a release button that ends the current wait and cancels queued agent playback. Finally, browser audio has its own restrictions. Audio must begin from a user interaction, and keyboard input should not interfere with focused form controls. We had to balance reliable instrument input with normal keyboard and accessibility behavior. What we learned The biggest lesson was that a good agent experience depends just as much on tool design as it does on the model. When an agent receives vague page content and generic browser controls, it spends much of its effort figuring out how to operate the interface. When it receives focused tools with compact, structured context, it can spend that effort making a better creative decision. We also learned that fewer tool calls do not necessarily mean less capable tools. Combining the right information at the right moment made the experience faster while giving the agent more useful musical context. WebMCP was a natural fit for Agent Riff because the important information is difficult to communicate through pixels alone. A screenshot can show a piano, but it cannot reliably describe the exact notes, timing, density, and musical direction of the phrase that was just played. With WebMCP, the website becomes more than an interface the agent operates. It becomes a shared creative space where a person and an agent can listen, respond, and build something together. Like Comment Story Updates 1 made by chatgpt made by chatgpt made by chatgpt made by chatgpt made by chatgpt youtube screenshot (for some reason) made by chatgpt Inspiration WordPress already has a strong human collaboration model: authenticated sessions, roles, capabilities, drafts, review, and publishing. Most agent integrations bypass that model by asking for a separate API key or by giving an automation service broad access. AgentPress asks a different question: what if the agent could join the workspace the human is already using, while WordPress remained the source of authority? What it does AgentPress is an open-source WordPress plugin that exposes a permission-filtered set of site tools to compatible agents through WebMCP. It divides operations into three visible classes: automatic operations that are safe within the current user's authority; approval-required operations that should stop for an explicit human decision; and unavailable areas that are never exposed as tools, including users, plugins, themes, code, credentials, and settings. In the recorded live workflow, ChatGPT used AgentPress tools from its built-in browser to inspect the current WordPress context and structure, create one useful service-page draft, and read the result back. WordPress showed the item as a draft. The visible page count changed from 15 to 16, the one new page was identified, and all 15 pre-existing page metadata records remained unchanged. Nothing was published. Why WebMCP is a strong fit The browser already holds the most important context: the page the human chose, their authenticated WordPress session, and their real WordPress permissions. WebMCP lets that page offer structured tools directly to the agent. The user does not have to copy content into chat, hand over a permanent API key, or teach the model where buttons live. That makes a multi-step WordPress task possible in one request: inspect the site, understand the allowed operations, create a draft, and verify the result. The outcome remains native WordPress content that a human can preview, edit, review, or publish through the normal workflow. How we built it AgentPress defines a fixed catalog of typed WordPress Abilities and adapts the permitted surface to WebMCP using document.modelContext.registerTool . Calls travel through a private same-origin REST transport using the signed-in WordPress cookie session and REST nonce. The server does not trust discovery alone. It revalidates the current user's WordPress capabilities, object-level access, operation policy, origin, nonce, request size, and closed input schema at execution time. Draft writes are forced to draft status and pass through an idempotent Change Set coordinator. Attempts and outcomes produce bounded, sanitized audit records. The wp-admin Overview makes the boundary legible: connection health, the connected site, operations available automatically, operations classified as approval-required, and areas that are always blocked. Challenges The hardest problem was not generating WordPress content. It was preserving WordPress authority across browser discovery, agent execution, REST transport, object-level permissions, retries, and a human-readable admin interface. We treated every layer as a boundary and kept a dated experiment ledger containing failures as well as successful results. Accomplishments A real ChatGPT built-in-browser WebMCP run created and verified one WordPress page draft. The write was idempotent and represented as Change Set AP-1. The result remained unpublished and existing page metadata remained unchanged. The plugin uses the human's current WordPress session instead of a copied API key. The public repository contains implementation tests and an auditable experiment trail. What we learned Agent capability should be narrower than user capability, and the distinction must be visible to both the human and the model. Tool discovery is useful, but enforcement must happen again on the server. We also learned that an honest, bounded demonstration—with request identifiers, WordPress state verification, and explicit untested limits—is more valuable than a broad automation claim. What's next The recorded challenge build proves the core read-and-draft workflow. The next milestones are the visible approval and classic-navigation flows, the canonical Administrator-versus-Author permission test, and five consecutive clean reliability runs. Those are deliberately described as next steps, not as completed features. Today you ask your AI agent to track competitors. It's OK until it misses important update. We build an AI-friendly app that does that better. But agents couldn't sign up... Until we added WebMCP :) Like Comment Story Updates ChatGPT using WebMCP Website with WebMCP Inspiration If you want to track a competitor today, you don't search for an app; you ask your AI agent to do that. That works OK at the beginning, but then it misses important things after a few weeks. We built an app that tracks competitors better, and it's AI agent-friendly. But agents couldn't sign up and set up everything for you without your action. Until we added WebMCP :) What it does Every page of competitortracker.io now registers 11 WebMCP tools - in production, in four languages, for any agent riding along in the visitor's browser. Three groups: Q&A over the site's own words. search_site , get_page , get_pricing , compare_with_rival , about_competitor_tracker . An agent can answer any question about the product from what the pages actually say — the agent and the human are never told different things. Public competitor data. search_companies , get_company , list_industries , get_industry , get_recent_changes over our open CC BY 4.0 data: 300+ tracked companies, 50 industries, and the dated, classified change feed. This is the product doing its actual job in the agent's hands, not a description of it. Agent signup. start_tracking_competitors : the agent names the companies you care about, we create an unclaimed organization (no email, nothing charged, self-deletes in four days), and hand back a claim link plus a short-lived read token. The tool then tells the agent the follow-up move: wait ~15 seconds, call GET /v1/changes with the token, and lead with what changed at your competitors — then hand over the claim link so the human takes ownership. So the full arc works with zero forms: ask a question, see real competitor changes, get tracking set up, claim it - all before you have an account. How we built it The site is Hugo on Cloudflare Workers, and most of the machinery already existed for AI crawlers: every page has a markdown twin, llms.txt is a curated per-page index, and our competitor data ships as an open JSON tree. So the WebMCP layer needed no search backend and no new data - the tools are framework-free ES modules that read what the site already publishes, plus one POST to our live agent-signup API. Details we care about: A 411-byte loader rides every page; the ~13 KB tool bundle is injected only when a modelContext runtime actually exists. Ordinary visitors pay nothing. The two required headers ( Permissions-Policy: tools=(self) , Origin-Agent-Cluster: ?1 ) ship from our edge worker, the single chokepoint for all responses. Every execute() is wrapped so no failure ever throws - a browser agent runtime has no defined behavior for an uncaught rejection, so every path returns a result the agent can read. Telemetry counts calls, never contents: tool name, page, language, outcome. The module is open source (MIT) - the loader, registration helper and validation utils are generic enough to point at your own tools. Challenges we ran into The spec moved under us. The getter lives on document.modelContext but half the browsers on the origin trial still carry navigator.modelContext ; we probe both. And unregisterTool() / provideContext() are gone from the spec - teardown is aborting the signal you registered with. Generated code confidently suggests the removed API. Testing without a runtime. No browser in our CI has WebMCP, so we built a Node harness that runs the real tool modules with stubbed browser globals against the live site and API — every tool, every error path. Honest copy is a design constraint here. A 503 from the signup API must never be spoken as "we don't track that company," and the 15-second wait is a write-settle margin, not "we're fetching your data now" - the change history is already published. Agents repeat what tools tell them, so the tool result text is a product surface. How do I test this? It took some time to figure out how to test WebMCP in Chrome. Accomplishments that we're proud of It's live on our real production site , not a hackathon sandbox - every page, every language, behind a one-line kill switch. The signup flow keeps the human in charge without a single confirmation dialog: the org holds no money, sends no email, expires unclaimed, and the claim page is the confirmation step. The whole thing reuses surfaces we'd already built for fetching agents (markdown twins, llms.txt , open data) - the pre-account story just needed the last mile into the tab. Fifteen seconds after asking, an agent shows you what your competitors changed. That's the demo, and it's real. What we learned A tool declaration is a hint to a cooperative agent, never a security boundary — validation lives server-side, and the published-catalog-only rule (the tool never fetches a URL you supply) is the guarantee the whole feature stands on. The right scope for in-page tools isn't feature parity with our MCP server (50 tools behind OAuth). The web page is the before-the-account surface; the MCP server is after . Drawing that line made every other decision easy. Sites that already publish machine-readable views get WebMCP almost for free. The work we'd done for crawlers became the engine for in-tab agents. WebMCP is fun! What's next for Competitor Tracker & Co. WebMCP Teach people how to use this. Join the WebMCP origin trial (Chrome 149+) so the tools work for real visitors without flags. Declarative form annotations for the few forms the site has, and page-scoped context - a company page pre-scoping its own tools. Localized tool descriptions for our FR/DE/JA pages. Watching our telemetry to learn which tools agents actually call, and feeding that back into both the WebMCP surface and the full MCP server.
