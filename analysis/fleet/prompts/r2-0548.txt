=== RUBRIC (frozen) ===
# FROZEN SCORING RUBRIC (pre-registered before any Stage 1 review)

Scores are integers 1-10 on each of the FOUR official criteria. Every score carries:
rationale, evidence_surfaces, confidence (0-1). Reviewers use the full scale and these
band anchors verbatim. Category neutrality is absolute: a game, art piece, music tool,
CRM, weird toy, and developer tool are all eligible for 10/10 on every criterion if they
satisfy that criterion exceptionally well FOR WHAT THEY ARE TRYING TO BE.
No aesthetic penalties. Signs of hurried AI work matter only as actual product-quality
problems (incoherence, broken flow, placeholder content), never as style.

## WebMCP Leverage — what does WebMCP change for this product?
Judge the delta WebMCP makes to reliability, precision, structured state access,
shared human-agent state, repeatability, and capability. Tool COUNT is not leverage.
- 9-10 TRANSFORMATIVE: the intended experience is impractical without the structured
  agent surface; human and agent share live product state; the collaboration could not
  be replicated comparably well by a general agent driving the UI.
- 7-8 MAJOR DELTA: without WebMCP a general agent is markedly less reliable/precise
  here; the agent is central to the product, not a bolt-on.
- 5-6 MEANINGFUL DELTA: existing capability packaged into a genuinely better product
  experience via structured tools; same outcome otherwise possible with real friction.
- 3-4 MINOR DELTA: generic browser automation achieves comparable outcomes; WebMCP is
  a convenience layer.
- 1-2 COSMETIC: exposes an action a UI button already offers; no meaningful change.

## Execution — is the actual product coherent, intentional, complete for its scope?
Stage 1 scores EXECUTION_CLAIMED: coherence/intentionality/completeness as evidenced by
the packet (description + packaging + video frames/transcript). Thin evidence caps the
score (end-to-end credit requires end-to-end evidence) and lowers confidence.
Stage 2 may rescore as EXECUTION_OBSERVED; latest evidence-backed score replaces, never
sums.
- 9-10: all surfaces agree; central workflow demonstrably completes; states handled;
  feels finished for its stated scope.
- 7-8: strong coherence and intentionality; central workflow appears functional; minor
  unfinished edges.
- 5-6: plausibly real with visible gaps; scope unclear or core not demonstrated
  end-to-end.
- 3-4: thin prototype; contradictions between surfaces; placeholder content.
- 1-2: non-functional or incoherent for its claimed scope.

## Creativity & Ambition — novelty of concept and interaction model
Judge ONLY: novelty of the concept, novelty of the interaction model, originality
relative to existing concepts, ambition, memorable/surprising use of the medium, and
the depth with which the concept is pursued. WebMCP indispensability belongs under
WebMCP Leverage, NOT here: a project can be extremely creative even if another agent
API could theoretically have enabled something similar.
- 9-10: genuinely novel concept or interaction model; ambitious scope pursued deeply
  and coherently; memorable.
- 7-8: fresh synthesis or notably original interaction; ambitious for a hackathon.
- 5-6: competent take on a known category with a real new angle.
- 3-4: derivative; novelty mostly surface details.
- 1-2: clone/template with trivial changes.
"That the category exists" is never a penalty; judge the submitted concept.

## Potential Impact — credible value for the intended audience
Judge: is there a real audience; is there a real problem, desire, or use case for that
audience; does the demonstrated solution genuinely address it. NO commercial adoption,
monetization, market pull, or startup viability is required. Entertainment, play, art,
creative expression, learning, niche hobby use, experimentation, and community value
are legitimate forms of impact. A tiny audience fully justifies a top score.
- 9-10: specific real audience with a real problem/desire; solution demonstrably
  addresses it; credible value, appeal, usefulness, or meaning for that audience.
- 7-8: clear audience and problem, good fit, credible value.
- 5-6: identifiable audience, looser fit or partial address.
- 3-4: speculative audience or strained fit; impact mostly aspirational.
- 1-2: no coherent audience/problem pairing.
Audience size is NOT a multiplier.

## Aggregate & ranking
aggregate = leverage + execution + impact + creativity (4-40). Nothing else enters.
Tie-breaks: Leverage, then Execution, then Impact, then Creativity, then higher
combined evidence confidence, then slug alphabetical. See PROTOCOL.md Ranking section.

Most agent systems expose tools continuously and rely on the model to decide when not to use them. I wanted to test a different model where trusted application state changes what the agent can actually do. That became SHIPGATE. Evidence becomes capability. Instead of keeping deployment authority permanently available, SHIPGATE changes the browser's WebMCP tool surface as release evidence and human decisions change. What SHIPGATE does SHIPGATE turns release state into agent authority. A release moves through four states BLOCKED → REVIEW_REQUIRED → READY → SHIPPED In BLOCKED , the agent can inspect evidence and request evaluation, but ship_release is not registered. When the hard evidence passes, request_signoff becomes available. The agent can request approval, but it cannot approve itself. Human confirmation stays outside WebMCP. Only after human approval does the release become READY and ship_release appear as a registered WebMCP capability. After shipping, that capability retires and temporary rollback_release authority appears. Two rules shaped the project. The agent can request evidence, but it cannot author the evidence. The agent can request authority, but it cannot grant authority to itself. Why WebMCP WebMCP is the mechanism, not an interface added afterward. SHIPGATE uses the browser's actual tool inventory to represent the agent's current authority. BLOCKED → ship_release absent REVIEW_REQUIRED → request_signoff available READY → ship_release available SHIPPED → ship_release retired and rollback_release available temporarily This improves the experience for both sides. People can see what authority the agent currently has and what evidence or human decision is still missing. Agents do not need to reason over a deployment tool that should not yet be available. The collaboration is explicit. The agent gathers evidence and asks for judgment. The human contributes authority the agent cannot give itself. The application turns those inputs into the capabilities that exist. How I built it The core architecture is: trusted evidence → application state → capability → runtime recheck → verified effect SHIPGATE is built with JavaScript, HTML, CSS, Node.js, Netlify, and native WebMCP. Tools are registered through document.modelContext.registerTool() . Six tools remain permanently available for inspection and evaluation get_release_state get_release_policy inspect_check compare_to_baseline list_evidence_gaps run_check_group Three capabilities appear only when the release contract allows them. request_signoff during REVIEW_REQUIRED ship_release during READY rollback_release after SHIPPED while rollback remains valid I also separated requesting evidence from creating evidence. run_check_group lets the agent request an evaluation, but the agent cannot supply PASS or FAIL, choose the evidence source, change the candidate identity, or provide human confirmation. The evaluator owns the result. Evidence is tied to the current candidate and its provenance. Stale or untrusted evidence cannot satisfy the release contract. Human approval is deliberately outside WebMCP. Tool availability also is not the security boundary. Consequential tools recheck the contract when invoked. Tool availability narrows the action surface. Runtime validation remains the enforcement boundary. Challenges I faced Dynamic tool registration The first challenge was making authority change safely at runtime. ship_release needs to disappear when authority is lost and later return under the same name without duplicate registrations or race conditions. I implemented a registration lifecycle. HIDDEN → REGISTERING → ACTIVE → RETIRING → HIDDEN SHIPGATE waits for registration or retirement to settle before the same capability can reappear. A native Chrome race condition The hardest bug appeared only during native WebMCP execution in Chrome 152. ship_release could successfully move the release to SHIPPED, but immediate retirement of that tool could cancel the still-running executeTool() promise with an UnknownError . The release had succeeded, but the browser call looked like it had failed. I fixed this by separating policy authority from physical registration retirement . The policy changes immediately, so another ship attempt already fails its runtime precondition. The browser registration retires only after the active invocation finishes. That distinction made the native WebMCP lifecycle reliable. Evidence authority I also had to prevent the agent from creating the evidence that would unlock its own capability. The solution was simple but important. The agent can request an evaluation. The evaluator decides the outcome. That separation became one of the central design principles of SHIPGATE. Testing I tested both the application logic and the real browser capability surface. SHIPGATE has 20 passing automated tests covering release transitions, evidence provenance, stale and untrusted evidence, human approval boundaries, runtime enforcement, conditional registration, same-name registration, and in-flight retirement. I also tested the complete lifecycle in Chrome 152 using native document.modelContext WebMCP without mocks or stubs. The browser confirmed the intended progression. BLOCKED → ship_release absent REVIEW_REQUIRED → request_signoff appears pending approval → release does not advance READY → ship_release appears SHIPPED → ship_release retires and rollback_release appears rollback → temporary rollback authority retires For me, this was the important proof. SHIPGATE does not only change a button in the interface, but also changes the capability the browser exposes to the agent. Demo candidate SHIPGATE gates a real browser-game release candidate called FAKE LEFT. FAKE LEFT provides the behavioral and regression evidence used in the demo release contract. FAKE LEFT is the candidate being evaluated. SHIPGATE is the system deciding which capabilities become available around that candidate. What I learned The biggest lesson was that agent judgment and agent authority are different product problems. I began by thinking about whether an agent could correctly decide that a release was safe. Building SHIPGATE changed the question. Even if an agent can reason correctly, why should a consequential capability remain available before the surrounding evidence and human judgment are complete? WebMCP let me make that distinction concrete. The agent can inspect, evaluate, and request. The human provides judgment the agent cannot give itself. The application translates both into the authority that actually exists. I think this pattern can extend beyond deployment to infrastructure changes, administrative actions, sensitive data access, financial operations, and other workflows where agent authority should follow trusted state. What's next The next step is connecting this capability model to real operational evidence. The current demo uses deterministic evidence so every state transition is reproducible. I would next connect SHIPGATE to CI pipelines, evaluation systems, production monitoring, approval workflows, deployment infrastructure, and rollback systems. The long-term idea is straightforward. Consequential agent capabilities should follow trusted real-world state rather than remain permanently exposed. Like Comment Story Updates VibeCheck: what people actually said Inspiration This came out of a habit I didn't think about much until I tried to explain it to someone. When I'm actually deciding on something — a laptop, a bank, a framework — I don't trust the first page of results. I search for it, then I add "reddit" to the end and go read threads until I've got a real picture in my head. Marketing pages tell you what a product is supposed to be. Threads tell you what it's actually like to own the thing. I wasn't trying to build another search tool or another recommendation engine. There's no shortage of those and most of them aren't much help when you're actually trying to decide something. What I wanted to build was the reading part — go gather what real people are saying, put it somewhere useful, and then get out of the way. Because there's one part of this I don't think a model should be doing for you, and that's deciding who you trust. WebMCP came into the picture later than the idea itself, but once it clicked, it kind of reframed the whole thing. Say you're researching a laptop and you've got an agent helping. Halfway through, you notice one of the sources reads like an affiliate blog wearing a Reddit post's clothes, so you reject it. Or you find one comment that nails the exact problem you're worried about, so you pin it. None of that exists anywhere except in your head and on your screen, right then. It's not sitting in a database somewhere, and it's definitely not something you'd think to type into a prompt. WebMCP is what lets the agent reach into that. Not a summary of it, not a description of it after the fact — the actual state, live, while you're both looking at the same board. That's also, concretely, what it buys you that you didn't have before. Reject a source mid-conversation and the agent doesn't need you to explain what changed — its next call against the board already sees the claim re-weighted, because it's reading the same object you just edited. And it runs the other way too: the agent can pin a claim or apply a constraint on its own, and instead of that decision getting buried three messages back in a chat log, it shows up as a card on your board, right where you'd notice it, and you can undo it with a click if it got it wrong. Neither of you is re-explaining state to the other. You're both just looking at the same thing. That turned into more than an integration detail for me. It's a big part of why the whole app is shaped the way it is. What I learned Honestly the biggest lesson wasn't technical. It's that "gather opinions" is a lot harder than "run a search." A search API hands you results. Turning those into something usable is where it gets messy. Ask it "is the Mac Mini a good device for developers?" and you'll get close to nothing back. Strip it down to "mac mini developers" and suddenly there are real threads with real replies. Search engines want keywords, not questions, and getting from one to the other without losing what the person actually meant took more tuning than I expected going in. Then there's the discussion-vs-not-discussion problem. A big chunk of what came back technically matched the query but wasn't a discussion at all — Facebook groups, LinkedIn posts, promotional junk dressed up to look like a real thread. I ended up writing a whole layer just to translate a person's actual sentence into something the search API would respect, and then another pass to figure out which of the results were even worth reading. Trust modeling was the other one. My first pass at "how strong is this claim" was just a source count — more sources, stronger claim. Which sounds fine until you notice that three people copying the same affiliate article isn't three opinions, it's one opinion wearing a disguise three times. So I moved to weighting the evidence instead of counting it: $$ \text{raw}(claim) = \sum_{s \in \text{evidence}} w(s) $$ where $$ w(s) = \begin{cases} 0 & \text{you rejected it} \ 1.5 & \text{you trusted it} \ 0.4 & \text{it's a low-signal source} \ 1 & \text{ordinary discussion source} \end{cases} $$ and then normalized against whatever the strongest claim on the board is: $$ \text{weight}(claim) = \min\left(1,\ \frac{\text{raw}(claim)}{\max_{c \in \text{claims}} \text{raw}(c)}\right) $$ Once that was in place the UI more or less designed itself. The support bar isn't a decoration sitting on top of the claim — it's that formula, visible. Reject a source and the segment it owned just goes away, and you can watch a claim that looked like consensus a second ago drop down to "thin." That's probably my favorite thing in the whole app. It makes "trust" a thing you can actually see move, instead of a number some model handed you with no way to check its work. How I built it I kept the architecture small on purpose. One state shape, one reducer — and both the UI and the six WebMCP tools go through that same reducer to touch the board. Nothing gets a side door. That constraint came from something specific I wanted to avoid: a tool that quietly changes something you can't see. The second that happens, this stops being collaboration and starts being something closer to surveillance, and I didn't want to build that even by accident. The actual pipeline under the UI is smaller than you'd guess. SerpApi's google_forums engine goes and finds the threads first. Then an LLM — routed through OpenRouter, free model first with a paid one as backup — does three things with what comes back: decides whether a thread is actually relevant, figures out whether the person sounds like they've used the thing versus just repeating something they heard, and clusters the recurring points into claims with the evidence attached to each one. Everything downstream of that — the reducer, the support math, the whole board — is only as good as that step, so it ended up eating far more of my time than I planned for. Challenges SerpApi's free tier is 250 searches a month, so credit discipline wasn't optional. I built it as two stages — google_forums runs alone first, and only if that comes back too thin does a second, broader search fire. There's also a disk cache for local dev, so I'm not burning real credits every time I tweak a font size. Deciding what "too thin" even means turned into its own small project, since real google_forums results are noisier than I expected — Facebook and LinkedIn show up constantly, looking like discussion when they're really not, which pushes the fallback to trigger more than I originally planned for. The more frustrating problem was getting the model to stop finishing other people's sentences. Search snippets get cut off mid-thought, sometimes literally ending in "…", and a language model's instinct is to complete that thought for you. Normally that's a fine instinct. Here it's dangerous — if a source says "the battery life is good, but…" and the model decides the rest was probably "not great under heavy load," that's a completely plausible sentence sitting right next to a citation, and it's also something nobody ever actually said. I had to write out very literal rules against it: don't finish a cut-off snippet, don't invent a detail that isn't in the text, don't borrow a phrase from one source's snippet into another source's line. None of that is clever prompting. It's just closing off, one by one, all the small ways a model fills gaps with confidence instead of silence. The last challenge is the one with no clear finish line, which is making the board itself worth spending time on. It's not hard to build something that's technically correct and still nobody wants to open twice — an accurate list of claims with checkboxes next to them is not, on its own, a reason to come back.
