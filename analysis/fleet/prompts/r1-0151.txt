## WebMCP Leverage — what does WebMCP change for this product?
Judge the delta WebMCP makes to reliability, precision, structured state access,
shared human-agent state, repeatability, and capability. Tool COUNT is not leverage.
- 9-10 TRANSFORMATIVE: the intended experience is impractical without the structured
  agent surface; human and agent share live product state; the collaboration could not
  be replicated comparably well by a general agent driving the UI.
- 7-8 MAJOR DELTA: without WebMCP a general agent is markedly less reliable/precise
  here; the agent is central to the product, not a bolt-on.
- 5-6 MEANINGFUL DELTA: existing capability packaged into a genuinely better product
  experience via structured tools; same outcome otherwise possible with real friction.
- 3-4 MINOR DELTA: generic browser automation achieves comparable outcomes; WebMCP is
  a convenience layer.
- 1-2 COSMETIC: exposes an action a UI button already offers; no meaningful change.

## Execution — is the actual product coherent, intentional, complete for its scope?
Stage 1 scores EXECUTION_CLAIMED: coherence/intentionality/completeness as evidenced by
the packet (description + packaging + video frames/transcript). Thin evidence caps the
score (end-to-end credit requires end-to-end evidence) and lowers confidence.
Stage 2 may rescore as EXECUTION_OBSERVED; latest evidence-backed score replaces, never
sums.
- 9-10: all surfaces agree; central workflow demonstrably completes; states handled;
  feels finished for its stated scope.
- 7-8: strong coherence and intentionality; central workflow appears functional; minor
  unfinished edges.
- 5-6: plausibly real with visible gaps; scope unclear or core not demonstrated
  end-to-end.
- 3-4: thin prototype; contradictions between surfaces; placeholder content.
- 1-2: non-functional or incoherent for its claimed scope.

## Creativity & Ambition — novelty of concept and interaction model
Judge ONLY: novelty of the concept, novelty of the interaction model, originality
relative to existing concepts, ambition, memorable/surprising use of the medium, and
the depth with which the concept is pursued. WebMCP indispensability belongs under
WebMCP Leverage, NOT here: a project can be extremely creative even if another agent
API could theoretically have enabled something similar.
- 9-10: genuinely novel concept or interaction model; ambitious scope pursued deeply
  and coherently; memorable.
- 7-8: fresh synthesis or notably original interaction; ambitious for a hackathon.
- 5-6: competent take on a known category with a real new angle.
- 3-4: derivative; novelty mostly surface details.
- 1-2: clone/template with trivial changes.
"That the category exists" is never a penalty; judge the submitted concept.

## Potential Impact — credible value for the intended audience
Judge: is there a real audience; is there a real problem, desire, or use case for that
audience; does the demonstrated solution genuinely address it. NO commercial adoption,
monetization, market pull, or startup viability is required. Entertainment, play, art,
creative expression, learning, niche hobby use, experimentation, and community value
are legitimate forms of impact. A tiny audience fully justifies a top score.
- 9-10: specific real audience with a real problem/desire; solution demonstrably
  addresses it; credible value, appeal, usefulness, or meaning for that audience.
- 7-8: clear audience and problem, good fit, credible value.
- 5-6: identifiable audience, looser fit or partial address.
- 3-4: speculative audience or strained fit; impact mostly aspirational.
- 1-2: no coherent audience/problem pairing.
Audience size is NOT a multiplier.

## Aggregate & ranking
aggregate = leverage + execution + impact + creativity (4-40). Nothing else enters.
Tie-breaks: Leverage, then Execution, then Impact, then Creativity, then higher
combined evidence confidence, then slug alphabetical. See PROTOCOL.md Ranking section.

What it does A human demonstrates a task once. Inspiration Most AI experiences revolve around a chat box: the human asks, the agent answers, and one side usually has all the important context. We wanted to explore something more physical, cooperative, and playful. CROSSTALK began with a simple question: What if WebMCP was not merely an integration layer, but a core game mechanic? We drew inspiration from cooperative bomb-defusal games, escape rooms, radio communication, and the tension of working with someone who knows things you cannot see. In CROSSTALK, the human and AI agent are deliberately given different abilities. The human can see colors, symbols, gauges, displays, and flashing signals. The agent has the operating manual, machine-readable data, and control tools—but cannot perceive the device itself. Success comes from communication, not automation. What it does CROSSTALK is a cooperative browser game in which the second player is an AI agent. The human player is presented with a physical-looking device containing modules such as: A wire bay with colored wires A keypad covered in unfamiliar glyphs A regulator with an analog gauge An echo module with changing labels and displays A signal transmitter producing short and long beeps The AI agent receives a changing set of WebMCP tools. These let it consult the manual, scan machine-readable identifiers, inspect recorded history, adjust controls, and review completed sessions. However, the tools intentionally do not reveal information that only the human should perceive. The resulting loop is: Describe → Look up → Decide → Confirm → Act For example, the agent may know exactly how to decode a signal frequency but must ask the human what rhythm they hear or see. The human may know where the gauge needle is, while only the agent can operate the regulator. Neither participant has enough information or control to finish alone. CROSSTALK contains three escalating missions: HANDSHAKE introduces communication through the wire bay. CROSSED WIRES combines wires, glyphs, and regulator controls. SILENT FREQUENCY tests the full partnership with keypad, echo, signal, and wire modules. Every agent action is shown in the in-game TEAM RADIO , so the human can see what the agent inspected or changed. Mistakes cause strikes, while successful coordination produces a detailed debrief and training record. How we built it CROSSTALK is a client-side TypeScript application built with Vite and the WebMCP API. We treated the WebMCP tool schema as a sensory boundary . Each tool defines not only what the agent can do, but also what it is allowed to know. Tool descriptions explicitly explain missing sensory information and direct the agent to ask the human instead of guessing. The game currently exposes 11 imperative tools and one declarative form tool. These include manual lookups, device inspection, RFID scanning, regulator controls, echo-history retrieval, transmitter tuning, and post-mission review. The toolset changes dynamically throughout a mission. When a module becomes active, its relevant tools appear. When that module is solved, those tools are removed using scoped AbortController lifecycles and a toolchange event is emitted. Read-only sensors and irreversible actuators are also distinguished through tool annotations. To prevent puzzle logic and documentation from contradicting each other, the operating manual and the actual module behavior share the same underlying rule data. We built the interface as a physical workbench rather than a conventional web dashboard. Mission case files, taped instructions, a clipped briefing docket, radio receipts, paper textures, switches, lamps, and a ring-binder manual make the experience feel like handcrafted field equipment. Audio is synthesized with WebAudio, while the visual assets and textures are original, code-native work. Everything runs without accounts, analytics, or a game server. The local operator dossier records only relevant game events—not the player’s conversation—and remains on the device. Reliability is covered by Vitest and Playwright, including: 27 automated tests Deterministic zero-strike runs through all three missions Native WebMCP testing in Chromium Firefox and WebKit compatibility checks Accessibility and interaction checks Responsive layouts at desktop, mobile, and 320-pixel widths Reduced-motion, keyboard, and forced-color support Production deployment verification Challenges we ran into The hardest challenge was creating information asymmetry that felt fair. If the agent received too much state, the human became unnecessary. If the agent received too little, the interaction became frustrating. We had to decide carefully which facts belonged to human perception, which belonged to machine-readable sensors, and which actions should require explicit confirmation. Dynamic tool registration created another challenge. Mission resets, completed modules, and stale browser sessions could otherwise leave the agent with tools that should no longer exist. We built lifecycle management around each tool and added tests for repeated missions and state transitions. We also spent significant time making the interface feel authored rather than generated. Early versions resembled a collection of polished panels, but not a believable game. Moving toward a cohesive physical workbench—with imperfections, paper layers, hardware controls, and mission-specific case files—gave CROSSTALK a much stronger identity. Human playtesting caught an especially useful issue in the final mission: the signal module’s beeps could be heard, but its visual feedback was too subtle to notice. Automated logic tests passed because the signal state was technically correct, yet the experience was still unclear. We redesigned it with a large synchronized VISUAL BEEP lamp and a reacting speaker grille, then added rendered-state regression coverage so the problem could not silently return. Accomplishments that we're proud of We are most proud that the AI agent is not simply a hint system or chatbot attached to the side of the game. It is a genuine second player with unique knowledge and controls. Other accomplishments include: Making WebMCP itself part of the game design Creating puzzles that neither participant can solve independently Dynamically changing the agent’s capabilities as the device changes Keeping every agent action visible and understandable to the human Building evidence-bounded coaching without recording conversations Creating an original, tactile visual identity without external game assets Supporting desktop and small mobile layouts without sacrificing clarity Completing deterministic, zero-strike automated runs of every mission Shipping the complete experience as a private, static, client-side application Most importantly, CROSSTALK is fun to play. The tension does not come from pretending the AI is human. It comes from learning how to communicate effectively with an intelligence that perceives and controls a different part of the world. Explicitly telling the agent what it cannot see produces better cooperation than giving it vague access to everything. Dynamic capabilities are more understandable than one enormous permanent toolset. Line-Mate is one more technician on the line: a digital twin of a repair shop in a browser tab, where an agent works the floor through WebMCP and the manager keeps authority. Like Comment Story Updates Cover Page Board View Isometric Floor View Inspiration Every shop floor I've seen runs on one person's head: the manager who knows which bay frees up at 3:45 and which customer will actually wait. When a part slips, that person starts moving cars by gut feel. I wanted an agent on that floor, not a chatbot beside it, working the same board, with the manager still holding the pen. What it does The agent inspects the floor through WebMCP tools, searches alternative schedules across thousands of seeded simulation runs, and lands a plan grounded with real-time data. The manager can click around all dropdowns, drag any car, or even lock a slot; whereas the agent catches up on all movements, understands them and explains what trade-off would each implicate. How we built it Developed 13 tools via document.modelContext.registerTool({ name, description, inputSchema, execute }). Deterministic seeded engine in pure TypeScript. Next.js + Zustand + Vercel, isometric SVG floor, blueprint design language. Built with Claude Code and Codex. Challenges we ran into Defining the role of the app's agentic end was the biggest challenge of the project. My first instinct was an extension of the human: given a task, the agent would click the elements a person would click. That framing collapsed fast, it makes the agent a fragile imitation of the manager, fighting the same UI. The turn was to give the agent its own door into the same world: typed tools that carry context, instead of decorated CRUD calls. Accomplishments that we're proud of How fast an agent from whichever harness becomes another member of the team on the field. The context lives in the typed tools, and that alone was enough for the agent to map the whole situation and pick up the line of thinking behind my prompt. The page onboards the agent, not the other way around. What we learned WebMCP breaks the whole panorama of "what a web page should expose". What counts the most is now how future web apps are no longer designed for an intended-human use only, but for an agentic layer that coexists without one competing with the other. Therefore, they both UI and MCP tools will be meant for the same collaborative goal. What's next for Line-Mate Scenario packs beyond the repair shop (the engine is domain-agnostic: manufacturing cells), technician skills and shift handovers as first-class objects, and a replayable feed of agent tool calls.
