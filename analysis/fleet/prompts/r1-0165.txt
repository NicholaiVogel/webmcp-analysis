# FROZEN SCORING RUBRIC (pre-registered before any Stage 1 review)

Scores are integers 1-10 on each of the FOUR official criteria. Every score carries:
rationale, evidence_surfaces, confidence (0-1). Reviewers use the full scale and these
band anchors verbatim. Category neutrality is absolute: a game, art piece, music tool,
CRM, weird toy, and developer tool are all eligible for 10/10 on every criterion if they
satisfy that criterion exceptionally well FOR WHAT THEY ARE TRYING TO BE.
No aesthetic penalties. Signs of hurried AI work matter only as actual product-quality
problems (incoherence, broken flow, placeholder content), never as style.

## WebMCP Leverage — what does WebMCP change for this product?
Judge the delta WebMCP makes to reliability, precision, structured state access,
shared human-agent state, repeatability, and capability. Tool COUNT is not leverage.
- 9-10 TRANSFORMATIVE: the intended experience is impractical without the structured
  agent surface; human and agent share live product state; the collaboration could not
  be replicated comparably well by a general agent driving the UI.
- 7-8 MAJOR DELTA: without WebMCP a general agent is markedly less reliable/precise
  here; the agent is central to the product, not a bolt-on.
- 5-6 MEANINGFUL DELTA: existing capability packaged into a genuinely better product
  experience via structured tools; same outcome otherwise possible with real friction.
- 3-4 MINOR DELTA: generic browser automation achieves comparable outcomes; WebMCP is
  a convenience layer.
- 1-2 COSMETIC: exposes an action a UI button already offers; no meaningful change.

## Execution — is the actual product coherent, intentional, complete for its scope?
Stage 1 scores EXECUTION_CLAIMED: coherence/intentionality/completeness as evidenced by
the packet (description + packaging + video frames/transcript). Thin evidence caps the
score (end-to-end credit requires end-to-end evidence) and lowers confidence.
Stage 2 may rescore as EXECUTION_OBSERVED; latest evidence-backed score replaces, never
sums.
- 9-10: all surfaces agree; central workflow demonstrably completes; states handled;
  feels finished for its stated scope.
- 7-8: strong coherence and intentionality; central workflow appears functional; minor
  unfinished edges.
- 5-6: plausibly real with visible gaps; scope unclear or core not demonstrated
  end-to-end.
- 3-4: thin prototype; contradictions between surfaces; placeholder content.
- 1-2: non-functional or incoherent for its claimed scope.

## Creativity & Ambition — novelty of concept and interaction model
Judge ONLY: novelty of the concept, novelty of the interaction model, originality
relative to existing concepts, ambition, memorable/surprising use of the medium, and
the depth with which the concept is pursued. WebMCP indispensability belongs under
WebMCP Leverage, NOT here: a project can be extremely creative even if another agent
API could theoretically have enabled something similar.
- 9-10: genuinely novel concept or interaction model; ambitious scope pursued deeply
  and coherently; memorable.
- 7-8: fresh synthesis or notably original interaction; ambitious for a hackathon.
- 5-6: competent take on a known category with a real new angle.
- 3-4: derivative; novelty mostly surface details.
- 1-2: clone/template with trivial changes.
"That the category exists" is never a penalty; judge the submitted concept.

## Potential Impact — credible value for the intended audience
Judge: is there a real audience; is there a real problem, desire, or use case for that
audience; does the demonstrated solution genuinely address it. NO commercial adoption,
monetization, market pull, or startup viability is required. Entertainment, play, art,
creative expression, learning, niche hobby use, experimentation, and community value
are legitimate forms of impact. A tiny audience fully justifies a top score.
- 9-10: specific real audience with a real problem/desire; solution demonstrably
  addresses it; credible value, appeal, usefulness, or meaning for that audience.
- 7-8: clear audience and problem, good fit, credible value.
- 5-6: identifiable audience, looser fit or partial address.
- 3-4: speculative audience or strained fit; impact mostly aspirational.
- 1-2: no coherent audience/problem pairing.
Audience size is NOT a multiplier.

## Aggregate & ranking
aggregate = leverage + execution + impact + creativity (4-40). Nothing else enters.
Tie-breaks: Leverage, then Execution, then Impact, then Creativity, then higher
combined evidence confidence, then slug alphabetical. See PROTOCOL.md Ranking section.

Without WebMCP, an agent would need to visually locate rooms, estimate coordinates, find connection handles, and manipulate a canvas designed for human input. With WebMCP, the agent instead receives typed mission state with stable IDs and calls explicit domain operations. The agent does not scrape the canvas. I just met with a dentist from NYC who makes $400,000 a year and never heard of Codex. Then when I learned about WebMCP I realized this was the perfect technology to help doctors figure out how to deploy AI in their practices and be assured they're not letting the AI run loose and they stay HIPAA compliant. HIPAA.technology gives a doctor and an AI agent one shared workspace, where the doctor sees everything the agent does and keeps control of everything that matters. What it does A physician or medical-practice leader can: Learn three essential boundaries: minimum necessary data, the vendor path, and human clinical judgment. Compare four realistic healthcare AI workflows. Assess whether a workflow is ready for a bounded synthetic rehearsal. Prepare a 21-day AI adoption mission. Review and personally approve - or refuse - the mission's policy gates. Enter a public practice domain for a website debrief that never touches portals, private pages, or PHI. The AI agent can inspect the workspace, focus lessons, select workflows, assess readiness, stage and revise a mission, deliver a public-site debrief, and update the contextual guide. How I used WebMCP The live webpage registers ten structured JavaScript tools using document.modelContext.registerTool() : inspect_adoption_workspace focus_learning_lesson select_clinical_ai_use_case assess_use_case_readiness stage_21_day_adoption_mission revise_adoption_mission prepare_practice_website_review deliver_practice_website_debrief set_contextual_guidance focus_mission_week Without WebMCP, an agent has to guess what your app means from pixels and click targets. With WebMCP, it gets real tools with real schemas - bounded inputs, safety annotations, structured results. No guessing. And when the agent focuses a lesson, assesses a workflow, or stages a mission, the doctor sees the interface change immediately. Same workspace, same screen, both of them. Human authority is engineered - expert in the loop. The most important capability is one I intentionally did not build. The AI cannot: Accept organizational policy. Approve the adoption mission. Launch a live-patient-data workflow. Make clinical or compliance decisions. Enter or request PHI. The agent can inspect, explain, focus, assess, stage, and revise. The doctor keeps every decision that matters. Practice Website Debrief A doctor can enter their public practice domain and the agent looks at the public pages and comes back with: What the practice already does well. What could be clearer or more useful. One safe AI opportunity to explore next. This is educational, not a compliance audit. No patient portals, no credentials, no private pages, no PHI. How I built it HIPAA.technology is a React and TypeScript application I created with ChatGPT and Codex, built with Vinext and Vite, and hosted with ChatGPT Sites on the custom domain lab.hipaa.technology . Each WebMCP tool has: A narrow, descriptive name. An explicit JSON input schema. Additional properties disabled. Bounded and validated inputs. Clear descriptions of its effects and limitations. WebMCP safety annotations. Structured results. A visible shared-interface update when applicable. The healthcare model came from a ChatGPT Deep Research paper, Middleware Encryption for HIPAA-Compliant Use of Frontier LLMs in U.S. Healthcare . Its central lesson is baked into the whole experience: encryption is one layer, but you have to govern the entire information path - minimum necessary data, the exact vendor and feature path, logs, agent capabilities, and a human making the calls. A screenplay you write together with your agent- live on the same page. Like Comment Story Updates CoWriter in session Inspiration Co-writing with an AI these days usually mean pasting your script into a chat window, getting a lot of text back and then copying these into your document. You loose formatting and context for every round trip. I wanted to see what happens when the agent isn't a chat window, but a real collaborator working on the same page as you are, through the same interface as you use. WebMCP made that possible: expose the app's real actions as tools, and an agent can write directly into your live document instead of describing what it would write. What it does CoWriter is a small screenwriting tool: it has a Story Bible panel (title, logline, theme, genre, tone), a real act-by-act Outline panel (paste in your own — Act 1 with its beats, Act 2 with its beats, and so on, including acts with no scenes written yet), that breaks acts and scenes into real screenplay format (sluglines, action, character cues, dialogue). It has a character bible, and a loose idea board where you can tweek ideas on the go. Every action on that script; revising the premise, importing or editing the outline, adding a scene, editing a line, revising a character, dropping an idea, reordering scenes, checking the whole thing for consistency — is exposed as a WebMCP tool ( document.modelContext.registerTool ). The agent is not clicking around a UI it has to guess at; it's calling the exact same functions the interface calls, and the result shows up on your screen instantly, on the same live document. You can keep editing by hand at any moment if you wish to. Crucially, get_script 's description tells the agent to call it first, always. It returns the human-authored, act-by-act outline and the Story Bible up front, so every scene the agent drafts, and every consistency check it runs, is explicitly grounded in the story's real throughline and theme. It does not guess at what happens next from whatever scene happens to be open. The outline (the plan) and the written scenes (what's actually on the page) are kept deliberately distinct, the way a real writer's outline and draft are. You can ask, in plain language, "write a tense scene where she finds the letter, no dialogue yet" — the agent calls add_scene and it appears, correctly formatted, right in front of you. You read it and asks for some more edit if you want it tweeked, and the agent calls edit_scene on that exact scene. The character bible updates the same way as new characters get introduced. And check_consistency hands the agent every dialogue line labeled by speaker plus the full character bible in one structured payload, so it can catch things like a character's voice contradicting itself across acts — the kind of full-document consistency pass that's genuinely tedious for a human to do by re-reading, but natural for an agent to do well. Nothing about the human side of the app changes because an agent is present — you can type directly into any line at any time, exactly like a normal document. That's the point: one shared script, two collaborators, no export/import step between them. How we built it Plain HTML, CSS, and vanilla JavaScript — no framework, no build tooling, no backend. All 12 WebMCP tools are registered in a single readable file ( script.js ) so the integration itself is easy to inspect end to end. Data persists to the browser's local storage. Tool responses are serialized as compact JSON to keep the token footprint of every agent call low. Challenges we ran into Keeping the outline and the written scenes as two genuinely separate sources of truth, rather than letting them silently collapse into one, took deliberate design. It would have been simpler to let the agent treat whatever is on the page as the plan, but this would have let scenes drift from the story's real throughline. Instead, get_script always returns both, and the agent's own tool descriptions tell it the outline is the plan and the scenes are what is actually been written — they won't always be in sync, and that is intentional. Getting an agent to actually call a tool rather than just answer a writing request conversationally in chat also took some care in how the tool descriptions were phrased — an ambiguous prompt can be satisfied just as easily by the model generating prose in the chat window as by it calling add_scene on the page. Accomplishments that we're proud of Twelve WebMCP tools, all grounded in one shared Story Bible, with a UI that never has to know or care whether the human or the agent made the last edit. Watching a scene appear live on the page, correctly formatted and fitting the outline is genuinely satisfying to demo. What we learned That grounding matters more than raw capability. An agent that can call add_scene isn't useful on its own. It is useful because get_script forces it to read the outline and premise first, every time, so what it writes actually belongs in the story. What's next for CoWriter Multi-scene consistency diffing — let check_consistency compare against the previous run's report so an agent can flag only new contradictions instead of re-surfacing everything each time. PDF/Fountain export of the finished screenplay. Multi-document projects — more than one script per browser profile, with a lightweight project switcher. Real backend/cloud persistence, so a script isn't tied to one browser's local storage. A visible scene-level undo/history stack for agent-made edits. A richer character bible — arcs, relationships, and a "first appearance" pointer the agent can use to catch characters who show up before being introduced. A more efficient architecture for managing token consumption for each 'get_script' tool call. A studio for fictional coding-agent sessions. The agent in your browser writes and directs, you approve, the page renders SVG and video. Like 1 Comment Story Updates Agent Stage: two showcase scripts rendered by svgent, a terminal and an app Inspiration svgent is a client-only studio for fictional coding-agent sessions. A script describes a chat window or a terminal, with typing, thinking, tool runs, approvals and choices, and the studio renders it to SVG, PNG, GIF and MP4. People use it for READMEs, docs, talks and posts, where a recording of a real session would leak secrets and could not be re-shot or translated. Before this, the studio's AI assist was copy and paste: copy a prompt into a chat, paste the JSON back. WebMCP replaces that with tool calls on the page. The page can also answer with facts a language model cannot see: how long a page runs, when each message lands, what the frame looks like at eleven seconds. The agent works on the stage itself, not on a guess about it. What it does Agent Stage is the studio with WebMCP site tools. The agent in ChatGPT's built-in browser writes the script, sets the surface, look, camera and pages, measures the result, looks at a frame, proposes rewrites, and asks to export. The person keeps the decisions: rewrites and exports wait on a card, every string the script puts on the stage is listed, and each change can be undone. load_script validates a script and previews it at once. direct_camera turns on a deterministic follow camera. inspect_timeline returns per-message timing and a review. snapshot_frame returns a PNG of any moment. fit_duration lands a page on an exact length and reports what it changed. propose_patch puts rewrites on a card. approve_proposal exists only while a proposal is pending and waits for the person's click. export asks first. The rail shows every call. The "Every word on the stage" list holds every string the script puts on the stage, including what a collapse or a page turn hides. The tool descriptions tell the agent to treat what it knows from the conversation as private and to use stand-ins for real names, unless the person relaxes that on the page or in the chat. Credentials never pass. A loaded script keeps only the fields that render. Looks, fonts, images and camera go through their own tools. Live: https://agent.svgent.zakideee.dev/ Code: https://github.com/zakideee/svgent (the page is apps/webmcp/ ) How we built it 13 tools on document.modelContext , with navigator.modelContext as the fallback, registered with an AbortSignal for teardown. approve_proposal is registered only while a proposal is pending. Results are plain objects plus an MCP-style content array (text, and an image part for frames). The studio package gained a small imperative handle: read, replace and patch the project, seek, play, export, render a frame, spotlight a message. An agent's edit goes through the same state the person's controls use, so undo, autosave, validation and preview all see it. Text layout and rasterization run in WASM (boundsvg) in the browser. The animated SVG is CSS keyframes with no JavaScript. There is no server; nothing leaves the tab except the files the person downloads. The submission is apps/webmcp/ : the WebMCP tools, the agent rail, the privacy default, the showcase scripts and the share card, plus the studio handle ( StudioHandle , chrome , renderFrame , spotlight ) and one preview aspect-ratio fix. The studio, scene and render packages predate the challenge. Challenges we ran into ChatGPT's browser exposes document.modelContext , Chromium exposes navigator.modelContext , and the API can appear after the page has loaded. Registration prefers the document, polls for a late API, and reports the outcome in the rail. A dynamic tool ( approve_proposal ) that unregisters itself while a call is in flight fails that call. It now stays registered for a short grace period after the proposal resolves. A script an agent writes from a conversation can carry real names, paths, hosts and keys from that conversation. The privacy default, the script allowlist and the "every word" list exist for that. Accomplishments that we're proud of The whole loop runs in the ChatGPT desktop app (built-in browser, GPT-5.6 Sol) and in Chromium with WebMCP enabled, and the demo video is a recording of it. The agent edits through the same state as the person's controls, so undo, autosave, validation and preview treat both alike. Four of the showcase scripts are problems agent-app users have: a clip for the team channel without the secrets; a reproduction of a decline that was missed, when recording is not allowed; three slides that walk a colleague through choices and approvals; a README hero for your own MCP server. What we learned A page can hand an agent facts it cannot infer (timing, frames, what would render), and that is worth more than letting the agent guess well. Approval and visibility have to be part of the tools, not a note in a prompt. What's next for svgent Agent Stage A deny list of the person's own words (names, hosts, projects) checked on the page, next to the pattern-based hints. The same tools on the other surfaces the studio renders, and on the CLI for headless use. The demo video was recorded in the ChatGPT desktop app. Waits are sped up where marked. The narration is a synthetic voice.
