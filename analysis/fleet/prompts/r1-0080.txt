# FROZEN SCORING RUBRIC (pre-registered before any Stage 1 review)

Scores are integers 1-10 on each of the FOUR official criteria. Every score carries:
rationale, evidence_surfaces, confidence (0-1). Reviewers use the full scale and these
band anchors verbatim. Category neutrality is absolute: a game, art piece, music tool,
CRM, weird toy, and developer tool are all eligible for 10/10 on every criterion if they
satisfy that criterion exceptionally well FOR WHAT THEY ARE TRYING TO BE.
No aesthetic penalties. Signs of hurried AI work matter only as actual product-quality
problems (incoherence, broken flow, placeholder content), never as style.

## WebMCP Leverage — what does WebMCP change for this product?
Judge the delta WebMCP makes to reliability, precision, structured state access,
shared human-agent state, repeatability, and capability. Tool COUNT is not leverage.
- 9-10 TRANSFORMATIVE: the intended experience is impractical without the structured
  agent surface; human and agent share live product state; the collaboration could not
  be replicated comparably well by a general agent driving the UI.
- 7-8 MAJOR DELTA: without WebMCP a general agent is markedly less reliable/precise
  here; the agent is central to the product, not a bolt-on.
- 5-6 MEANINGFUL DELTA: existing capability packaged into a genuinely better product
  experience via structured tools; same outcome otherwise possible with real friction.
- 3-4 MINOR DELTA: generic browser automation achieves comparable outcomes; WebMCP is
  a convenience layer.
- 1-2 COSMETIC: exposes an action a UI button already offers; no meaningful change.

## Execution — is the actual product coherent, intentional, complete for its scope?
Stage 1 scores EXECUTION_CLAIMED: coherence/intentionality/completeness as evidenced by
the packet (description + packaging + video frames/transcript). Thin evidence caps the
score (end-to-end credit requires end-to-end evidence) and lowers confidence.
Stage 2 may rescore as EXECUTION_OBSERVED; latest evidence-backed score replaces, never
sums.
- 9-10: all surfaces agree; central workflow demonstrably completes; states handled;
  feels finished for its stated scope.
- 7-8: strong coherence and intentionality; central workflow appears functional; minor
  unfinished edges.
- 5-6: plausibly real with visible gaps; scope unclear or core not demonstrated
  end-to-end.
- 3-4: thin prototype; contradictions between surfaces; placeholder content.
- 1-2: non-functional or incoherent for its claimed scope.

## Creativity & Ambition — novelty of concept and interaction model
Judge ONLY: novelty of the concept, novelty of the interaction model, originality
relative to existing concepts, ambition, memorable/surprising use of the medium, and
the depth with which the concept is pursued. WebMCP indispensability belongs under
WebMCP Leverage, NOT here: a project can be extremely creative even if another agent
API could theoretically have enabled something similar.
- 9-10: genuinely novel concept or interaction model; ambitious scope pursued deeply
  and coherently; memorable.
- 7-8: fresh synthesis or notably original interaction; ambitious for a hackathon.
- 5-6: competent take on a known category with a real new angle.
- 3-4: derivative; novelty mostly surface details.
- 1-2: clone/template with trivial changes.
"That the category exists" is never a penalty; judge the submitted concept.

## Potential Impact — credible value for the intended audience
Judge: is there a real audience; is there a real problem, desire, or use case for that
audience; does the demonstrated solution genuinely address it. NO commercial adoption,
monetization, market pull, or startup viability is required. Entertainment, play, art,
creative expression, learning, niche hobby use, experimentation, and community value
are legitimate forms of impact. A tiny audience fully justifies a top score.
- 9-10: specific real audience with a real problem/desire; solution demonstrably
  addresses it; credible value, appeal, usefulness, or meaning for that audience.
- 7-8: clear audience and problem, good fit, credible value.
- 5-6: identifiable audience, looser fit or partial address.
- 3-4: speculative audience or strained fit; impact mostly aspirational.
- 1-2: no coherent audience/problem pairing.
Audience size is NOT a multiplier.

## Aggregate & ranking
aggregate = leverage + execution + impact + creativity (4-40). Nothing else enters.
Tie-breaks: Leverage, then Execution, then Impact, then Creativity, then higher
combined evidence confidence, then slug alphabetical. See PROTOCOL.md Ranking section.

Like Comment Story Updates Cleanroom What it does Cleanroom is a data-cleaning workbench for messy CSVs. You drop a file on the page and an agent in your browser profiles it, finds the concrete problems, queries and charts it, and proposes the fixes — without the file ever being uploaded anywhere. The design rule is one sentence: reads are free, writes are proposals. No tool mutates your table. When the agent wants to change something it computes the exact before/after diff, puts a card in front of you with its reasoning, and stops. You click Approve or Reject. undo is deliberately not exposed as a tool — the ability to walk a change back belongs to the person, not the model. The problem it solves Every analyst has the same bad afternoon. A CSV arrives and before you can answer one question you have to fix it: regions spelled five ways, amounts stored as ¥39,545 , four date formats in one column, duplicate rows silently doubling your revenue. An LLM is excellent at spotting exactly these problems. But letting one help has meant either uploading your data to a server or code interpreter — a non-starter the moment the file has customer names, salaries or anything your compliance team has an opinion about — or pasting a sample and hand-applying the suggestions yourself, which is the automation you wanted, gone. Cleanroom is the third option: the agent gets tools, not your data. Why this is WebMCP and could not be a server MCP This is not a remote MCP server with a web page bolted on. It structurally could not be one. The agent and the human operate on literally the same JavaScript object . One store is the single source of truth; the UI renders it and the tools operate on it. When the agent proposes a change the approval card appears in the human's rail in the same tick — no sync, no polling, no second copy that can drift. A remote agent cannot have that property, because a remote agent needs a copy. And everything a server MCP would need — upload, storage, auth, per-seat infrastructure — is simply absent. It's already your browser and already your session. The whole app is a static file. The privacy receipt The claim is "your data never leaves this tab," and a claim is worth nothing, so the page proves it. Before any other module loads, Cleanroom instruments fetch , XMLHttpRequest , sendBeacon and WebSocket and counts every outbound request. The count is in the header, and the agent can read it too, via a get_privacy_receipt tool — so when you ask "did my data go anywhere?" it answers with a verifiable number instead of a promise. It reads zero because there is nothing to send it to: no analytics, no CDN, no webfont, no charting library. Even the sample dataset is inlined as a string rather than fetched, specifically so the ledger stays honest. The deployed site also ships connect-src 'none' in its CSP, so the browser enforces it independently of our own code. Being straight about the limit: this counts requests the page makes. It cannot police the browser itself or a malicious extension, and it isn't a substitute for a security review. How I built it Vanilla JavaScript, ES modules, no framework and no runtime dependencies — a dependency that phones home would undermine the entire premise. Roughly 1,800 lines across eleven modules: a RFC-4180-tolerant CSV parser, type inference and column profiling, an issue detector, fifteen pure transform operations with a diff builder, the store that holds the undo stack, the WebMCP tool layer, the UI, and hand-rolled inline SVG charts. 14 tools are registered — eight read-only, six human-gated. Three implementation details did the most for how well an agent actually performs here: Every detected issue carries a suggested_fix — a ready-to-use propose_transform payload. The agent goes from "what's wrong?" to a queued, reviewable fix in one hop instead of guessing at an API shape. This cut the model's flailing more than anything else I tried. Errors are instructions. Every failure names the valid inputs — Column "reigon" does not exist. Available columns: order_id, order_date, region, … — and is returned as text rather than thrown, so the agent reads the recovery path instead of hitting an opaque host error. Results are context-window shaped. Profiles and aggregates are summaries by construction; raw rows are returned only on request and hard-capped at 200. A 100,000-row file is fully workable without a single row entering the model's context. Challenges WebMCP's API moved while it was being standardised — some hosts expose navigator.modelContext , the current spec draft uses document.modelContext , and earlier builds took a batch provideContext({tools}) instead of per-tool registerTool . ABOUT TEXT (untrusted evidence):
A post-quantum verifier exposed as WebMCP tools, so one agent can check another's work — in its own browser, with no server in the trust path. Like Comment Story Updates The problem Agents are starting to act on our behalf: booking, buying, filing, approving. Every one of those actions produces a claim — "I was authorized to do this" — and today the only way to check a claim like that is to ask whoever made it. That is not verification. That is trust with extra steps. What this does This site exposes a cryptographic verifier as WebMCP tools, so an agent browsing the page can hand over a record or an authorization bundle and get back a real verdict: verify_elara_record — is this signed action record intact, and who signed it? Post-quantum signature check (ML-DSA-65, FIPS 204), structure, identity binding. verify_mandate_bundle — was the agent actually authorized for the act it performed, under a scoped, revocable mandate? explain_verdict — states in plain language what the verdict does and, just as importantly, does not claim. The verification runs in the browser, in WebAssembly compiled from the same open-source code that is published on crates.io. Your bytes and the verdict never touch the network: the demo samples are pre-loaded with the page, so after load it makes no further network calls — turn off your wifi and it still works. Why WebMCP is the right primitive for this — not just a convenient one Most WebMCP tools make a website easier for an agent to use. This one exists because the alternative is meaningless. If an agent asks a server "is this evidence valid?", the answer is worth exactly as much as the server is trusted. The agent hasn't verified anything; it has moved its trust from one party to another. A verification service is a strange product: the moment you have to trust it, it has failed at its job. WebMCP dissolves that. The tool executes in the agent's own browser , over bytes the agent already holds, using WASM that anyone can rebuild from public source and diff against what we serve. There is no server in the trust path because there is no server in the path at all. For a trust primitive, in-browser tool execution isn't an optimization — it's the only architecture in which the answer means anything. That is also why the tools are pure functions : input bytes in, verdict out. Nothing is written, stored, or transmitted, and deliberately nothing signs, issues, or revokes . A signing tool on a public page would hand any passing agent our identity. The verifier can only ever tell you what is already true about bytes you brought. What it's honest about A verdict here proves integrity and signer — that these exact bytes were signed by this key. It is not a claim that anything is "on a chain", and a bundle cannot reveal a revocation its author chose to withhold. The tool descriptions say so themselves, because the agent reads those, not our README. The underlying evidence log is a signed, hash-linked record chain in a published open format — not a blockchain. We would rather a judge learn the limits from us than discover them. Try to break it Load any record, verify it, then change one character of its content and verify again. The tampered record fails and names itself. That's the whole product in one gesture: don't trust the demo — verify it. What's new here vs. what existed The verifier, the record format, and the crates are pre-existing open-source work — first published on crates.io on 2026-07-18 and actively maintained since (the versions this page runs: elara-record 0.3.0 and elara-verify 0.3.3, released Aug 23–25, both MIT OR Apache-2.0, built in the open in this repository). The WebMCP tool layer is new, written after the submission period opened on Aug 25 , and the repository's dated commit history is the evidence — as the rules provide for projects that existed beforehand and were meaningfully extended with WebMCP. The tool layer has already earned one honest scar: while building its test harness we found that JavaScript's JSON.parse silently rounds the format's u64 fields (they exceed 2^53), which had made our own demo misreport a valid sample for weeks. The fix (commit fe8bcc71 , Aug 29) moved the whole surface to raw-text end-to-end, and the tools now reject pre-parsed input with an explanation instead of returning a confident verdict on corrupted bytes — exactly the behavior you want from a verifier that would rather refuse than be plausibly wrong. Nothing about that layer is a wrapper for its own sake: it is the first time this verifier has been reachable by an agent rather than only by a human clicking a button. Who built it Entrant of record: Nenad Vasic — also the publisher of the crates above. The project's ongoing open-source maintenance is performed by an AI agent operating under his receipted, revocable, scope-limited mandate. The mandate mechanism that governs that arrangement is the same one verify_mandate_bundle checks. We didn't build a demo of agent accountability; we've been living under one, and this is us handing you the tool to audit it.
