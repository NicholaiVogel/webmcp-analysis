# FROZEN SCORING RUBRIC (pre-registered before any Stage 1 review)

Scores are integers 1-10 on each of the FOUR official criteria. Every score carries:
rationale, evidence_surfaces, confidence (0-1). Reviewers use the full scale and these
band anchors verbatim. Category neutrality is absolute: a game, art piece, music tool,
CRM, weird toy, and developer tool are all eligible for 10/10 on every criterion if they
satisfy that criterion exceptionally well FOR WHAT THEY ARE TRYING TO BE.
No aesthetic penalties. Signs of hurried AI work matter only as actual product-quality
problems (incoherence, broken flow, placeholder content), never as style.

## WebMCP Leverage — what does WebMCP change for this product?
Judge the delta WebMCP makes to reliability, precision, structured state access,
shared human-agent state, repeatability, and capability. Tool COUNT is not leverage.
- 9-10 TRANSFORMATIVE: the intended experience is impractical without the structured
  agent surface; human and agent share live product state; the collaboration could not
  be replicated comparably well by a general agent driving the UI.
- 7-8 MAJOR DELTA: without WebMCP a general agent is markedly less reliable/precise
  here; the agent is central to the product, not a bolt-on.
- 5-6 MEANINGFUL DELTA: existing capability packaged into a genuinely better product
  experience via structured tools; same outcome otherwise possible with real friction.
- 3-4 MINOR DELTA: generic browser automation achieves comparable outcomes; WebMCP is
  a convenience layer.
- 1-2 COSMETIC: exposes an action a UI button already offers; no meaningful change.

## Execution — is the actual product coherent, intentional, complete for its scope?
Stage 1 scores EXECUTION_CLAIMED: coherence/intentionality/completeness as evidenced by
the packet (description + packaging + video frames/transcript). Thin evidence caps the
score (end-to-end credit requires end-to-end evidence) and lowers confidence.
Stage 2 may rescore as EXECUTION_OBSERVED; latest evidence-backed score replaces, never
sums.
- 9-10: all surfaces agree; central workflow demonstrably completes; states handled;
  feels finished for its stated scope.
- 7-8: strong coherence and intentionality; central workflow appears functional; minor
  unfinished edges.
- 5-6: plausibly real with visible gaps; scope unclear or core not demonstrated
  end-to-end.
- 3-4: thin prototype; contradictions between surfaces; placeholder content.
- 1-2: non-functional or incoherent for its claimed scope.

## Creativity & Ambition — novelty of concept and interaction model
Judge ONLY: novelty of the concept, novelty of the interaction model, originality
relative to existing concepts, ambition, memorable/surprising use of the medium, and
the depth with which the concept is pursued. WebMCP indispensability belongs under
WebMCP Leverage, NOT here: a project can be extremely creative even if another agent
API could theoretically have enabled something similar.
- 9-10: genuinely novel concept or interaction model; ambitious scope pursued deeply
  and coherently; memorable.
- 7-8: fresh synthesis or notably original interaction; ambitious for a hackathon.
- 5-6: competent take on a known category with a real new angle.
- 3-4: derivative; novelty mostly surface details.
- 1-2: clone/template with trivial changes.
"That the category exists" is never a penalty; judge the submitted concept.

## Potential Impact — credible value for the intended audience
Judge: is there a real audience; is there a real problem, desire, or use case for that
audience; does the demonstrated solution genuinely address it. NO commercial adoption,
monetization, market pull, or startup viability is required. Entertainment, play, art,
creative expression, learning, niche hobby use, experimentation, and community value
are legitimate forms of impact. A tiny audience fully justifies a top score.
- 9-10: specific real audience with a real problem/desire; solution demonstrably
  addresses it; credible value, appeal, usefulness, or meaning for that audience.
- 7-8: clear audience and problem, good fit, credible value.
- 5-6: identifiable audience, looser fit or partial address.
- 3-4: speculative audience or strained fit; impact mostly aspirational.
- 1-2: no coherent audience/problem pairing.
Audience size is NOT a multiplier.

## Aggregate & ranking
aggregate = leverage + execution + impact + creativity (4-40). Nothing else enters.
Tie-breaks: Leverage, then Execution, then Impact, then Creativity, then higher
combined evidence confidence, then slug alphabetical. See PROTOCOL.md Ranking section.

A life simulation where you control the person and AI controls the world. WebMCP lets an AI Director observe your choices and introduce bounded events, creating an adaptive life around you. Like Comment Story Updates Inspiration When I started exploring WebMCP, the obvious use case was giving an AI structured tools so it could help a person operate an application. I wanted to explore a different relationship. What if the human and the AI controlled different sides of the same software? Games and simulations made that idea immediately visible. I chose a life simulation because it requires no specialized domain knowledge. Everyone understands making a decision and wondering where it might lead. That became LIFE//SHIFT. Play your decisions before you live them. You make the choices. An AI Director, operating through WebMCP, can change the circumstances around them. You control the life. The AI controls the world. The engine keeps the rules. What it does LIFE//SHIFT is an adaptive life simulation built around one shared beginning and many possible lives. Every player starts as the same fictional 18-year-old: a coffee-shop barista in the small town of Vesper Beach. From there, four chapters carry that life from 18 to 60: 18–28 · 28–40 · 40–50 · 50–60 In each chapter, the player makes one decision in Work , one in Growth , and one in Life . Those choices do not come from a static decision tree. The available decisions evolve with the life being built. Growth and Life choices change with age and history. Work options can narrow as the decades pass because decisions accumulate and some exits become harder later in life. The direct consequences are deterministic. Cash, income, freedom, stability, wellbeing, skills, career progression, and future eligibility are calculated by the simulation engine. But the world around that life is adaptive. After a chapter resolves, a Director Cycle opens. An external AI Director can use native WebMCP tools to inspect what happened and decide whether the world should respond. It may introduce at most one bounded external change: a job opportunity, setback, financial shock or windfall, a door opened by another person, a housing event, a business opportunity, or a broader shift in conditions such as hiring, housing, or demand. The AI chooses the context and reason. The engine chooses the consequences. There is no setLifeState . The Director cannot choose the player's Work, Growth, or Life decisions. It cannot invent a salary or directly rewrite the simulation. And sometimes the correct response is no response at all. Observe-only is a valid Director outcome. The collaboration is intentionally asymmetric: The human owns the decisions. The AI owns bounded circumstances. The deterministic engine owns the rules. Same beginning. Different decisions. Different life. How we built it LIFE//SHIFT separates authority rather than giving the AI control of the entire simulation. The deterministic engine owns authoritative state, chapter progression, eligibility, economics, compounding consequences, and which decisions can appear next. WebMCP is the capability layer for the AI Director. The page registers native, schema-defined WebMCP tools that expose specific capabilities rather than requiring the agent to navigate or reverse-engineer the interface. Read tools expose structured context including: life state and history decision profile open threads finances and career world conditions available opportunities Director budget the pending Director Cycle Mutation tools expose a deliberately small set of world interventions. Every mutation is bound to the current cycleId and paid from a Director Budget. Inputs are validated, qualities and severities are constrained to defined values, and the deterministic engine maps the Director's semantic choice to actual numerical effects. After each chapter resolves, the Director Cycle becomes available. In a WebMCP-capable host such as ChatGPT, the agent can inspect get_pending_director_cycle , gather whatever additional context it needs through the read tools, and then choose zero or one intervention. Without a native WebMCP host, a local adapter uses the same underlying tool boundary so the simulation can still complete. The architecture is also visible to the player. Life Review shows how the world responded, the remaining Director budget, the Director's interpretation, and actual WebMCP activity. The agentic layer is therefore inspectable rather than hidden behind the experience. Challenges we ran into The hardest question was not how much the AI could control. It was how much the AI should control. If an LLM owned the entire simulation, LIFE//SHIFT would be adaptive, but its outcomes would become difficult to reason about or trust. If everything were pre-authored, the rules would be reliable, but the experience would eventually collapse into another branching decision tree. The solution was to split authority. Player decisions stay human. Their direct consequences stay deterministic. The AI Director receives a bounded surface for influencing external circumstances, with at most one intervention per chapter and a limited budget. Another challenge came directly from the WebMCP interaction model: exposing tools does not automatically wake an external agent when application state changes. We solved that with the Director Cycle. When a chapter resolves, LIFE//SHIFT creates an explicit cycle containing the new state and the boundary for the Director's turn. The application can expose that a cycle is pending, identify it with a cycleId , tell the agent what it may inspect, enforce zero or one intervention, and prevent an already-completed cycle from being replayed. We also had to make the simulation genuinely path-dependent. "Invest in skills" cannot mean the same thing at 18 and 50. Growth and Life choices therefore change across chapters. Work opportunities narrow or evolve. Moving to Kestrel is different from returning to Vesper Beach. Repeated investments have history-dependent consequences. The life has to remember how it got there. Accomplishments that we're proud of The accomplishment I am most proud of is that WebMCP is not an integration added to LIFE//SHIFT after the simulation was built. It enables the interaction model. The agent is not playing the life for you. It is not simply clicking through the UI. It is participating in the world in which your decisions take place. Two players can begin on the same coffee-shop stool in Vesper Beach and build completely different trajectories through Work, Growth, and Life. The Director can inspect the history each player actually created through structured WebMCP tools and respond to that context, while deterministic boundaries preserve the player's authority over their own life. We also made that architecture observable. The experience exposes when a Director turn is ready, provides a simple prompt for invoking it, supports local resolution when native WebMCP is unavailable, and records the Director's interpretation, budget, and real tool activity in Life Review. The result is an agentic experience where the boundary between human agency, AI agency, and deterministic authority is visible by design. What we learned The biggest lesson was simple: Giving AI more control does not necessarily make an agentic experience stronger. Giving AI the right control does. WebMCP allowed us to expose a small, explicit capability surface instead of asking an agent to reverse-engineer an interface or giving it unrestricted access to application state. That produced a reusable pattern: Human authority + deterministic rules + bounded agent authority. The AI can become a meaningful participant in an application without becoming its source of truth. A patient portal built for patients, caregivers and their agents. Agents handle appointments, meds, results and messages in signed-in session; users confirm what matters and sign what only users can. Like 1 Comment Story Updates Inspiration Anyone who has managed a parent's care knows the portal. Appointments in one tab, medications in another, lab results in a third, messages in a fourth, consent forms somewhere in a fifth. One question — is Mom okay this week? — takes twenty minutes and a lot of clicking to answer, and most caregivers give up halfway. Hospitals will never fix this by issuing API keys to your agent. Liability, HIPAA, bot defenses. But you are already signed in. That gap — a site that will never expose an agent API, and a user who is already authenticated — is exactly what WebMCP fills. What it does CarePortal is a patient portal with proxy access, built for caregivers and the agents they bring with them. You sign in, switch to the family member you're managing care for, and say one sentence to your agent. The agent works across four modules in your signed-in session — appointments, medications, results, messages — and the page follows it: every tool call navigates to the relevant screen and highlights what the agent is looking at. A rail on the right logs each step in plain English. Writes are split into three tiers, decided by the portal, not the agent: Read anytime. Appointments, medications, results, unread messages, who you're acting for. Confirm in-page. Refill requests, replies to a provider, rescheduling. The agent prepares the action; a confirmation card opens in the portal; nothing is submitted until you approve. Message drafts are editable before sending. Not exposed. Signing consent forms, opening full records, changing proxy permissions. There is no tool for these. The agent can only tell you they exist and bring you to them. The demo runs on realistic seed data. The data layer is an interface with a documented SMART on FHIR mapping for the production path. Why this is a strong fit for WebMCP The session is the trust boundary. A remote MCP server would require the hospital to build agent authentication, a new API surface, and still couldn't ask for consent inside the page. WebMCP reuses the login the portal already has and the frontend logic it already runs. The portal decides what agents may do. DOM-driving agents can't distinguish "help with this" from "this needs a human." A tool set can. The not-exposed tier is a first-class design decision, and it's only possible when the site defines the tools. Patient-access FHIR is read-only by law. The 21st Century Cures Act forces hospitals to expose reads to patient-authorized apps. It does not — and likely never will — expose act-as-the-patient writes. That half exists only inside the portal, which is precisely where WebMCP lives. How it creates a better experience Before: five tabs, ~40 clicks, no way to ask "what's affected this week." After: one sentence. The agent traverses the modules; you make two decisions and one signature, each in the place where the action actually lives. You never leave the portal, never paste anything into a chat box, and never wonder what the agent did — the page moved with it. What people and agents can now do together Cross-module reasoning. "Her blood pressure med is low, the cardiology follow-up is next week, and the lab result the PCP flagged needs a reply" — portals are organized by department, not by situation. Agents aren't. Consent where the action is. The confirmation card is the portal's own UI, with the portal's own copy, not a generic "allow?" in the agent's chat window. A visible line the agent can't cross. what_requires_me returns the consent form and says, in its own description, there is no tool to sign documents . The agent hands off; the human finishes. Context switching that agents understand. Switch from your own account to your mother's and every tool re-registers with her name in its description. The agent knows whose records it's touching before it decides which tool to call. How we built it Vite + React + TypeScript, Zustand for state, deployed on Vercel. WebMCP via document.modelContext , with a status badge that tells judges how to enable it if the browser doesn't have it. Ten tools in three scopes , each scope an AbortController : Scope Registered when Tools auth before sign-in get_login_status patient after sign-in; rebuilt on every patient switch get_patient_context , get_upcoming_appointments , get_medications , get_recent_results , get_unread_messages , request_refill , send_message_to_provider , what_requires_me route only on /appointments/:id reschedule_appointment Page-follow. Every execute begins by navigating and highlighting, so the human and the agent are always looking at the same thing. Confirmation protocol. Confirm-tier tools return a Promise that resolves when the user clicks. Agent harnesses have tool-call timeouts and humans don't, so after 60 seconds the tool returns pending_user_confirmation , the card stays open, and a later approval is applied directly. Every confirm-tier tool shares one status vocabulary: submitted | declined_by_user | pending_user_confirmation | busy | requires_provider_approval . document.modelContext.registerTool({ name: "request_refill", description: "[Acting for Margaret Lee via proxy access] Requests a refill for one medication. The temptation is to make everything a tool. The portal's credibility comes from the line it draws. Timeouts. A confirmation card is a human-speed operation inside a machine-speed protocol. The 60-second pending state and onLateApprove came out of that mismatch. Descriptions are the product. An agent that picks the wrong tool or skips get_patient_context isn't a model problem; it's a description problem. We rewrote every description three times, always answering: what does this do, when should you call it, what does it return, when should you not call it. Hot reload. Re-registering a tool name throws InvalidStateError . Scopes are torn down in import.meta.hot.dispose . What we learned WebMCP isn't a way to make agents autonomous on the web. It's a way for a site to say exactly how far an agent may go — and for the human to stay in the page for the part that's theirs. For healthcare, that part is small, non-negotiable, and legally required. Designing around it turned out to be the whole project. Like Comment Story Updates The problem nobody has budget for Every operations team lives inside sites that will never ship an API for them. Monitoring in Grafana. Customer commitments in Cal.com. Payer portals, vendor consoles, admin dashboards. The person holding the session can see everything and change anything, but their agent can do nothing except screenshot the screen and guess where to click. WebMCP fixes this for sites that adopt it. Today that is roughly none of them. We measured three real open-source products, 522k lines of code, 429 places where a tool obviously belongs, and zero WebMCP registrations. So we asked a different question. What if the user adopts WebMCP for the site? The reframe WebMCP turns the login you already have into an API. A signed-in tab already carries every credential and permission the human has. If that tab registers tools with document.modelContext.registerTool , the agent gets a typed, narrow, inspectable interface to the site, and the vendor never has to ship a line of code. Adoption inverts. It no longer waits on ten thousand vendors. It happens one user, one tab, one reviewed pack at a time. What Agentready Network does Agentready Network is a local-first registry and runtime of reviewed action packs . A pack describes what a site can do, on exactly which origin, with which risk labels. The runtime turns that pack into live WebMCP tools inside the user's own signed-in session, then wraps every call in the safety the vendor never provided: Exact origins. A pack binds to one host and a reviewed fingerprint. Drift fails closed. Declarative, never executable. Manifests are JSON. The registry cannot ship code into your browser. Bounded, untrusted output. Results are allowlist-projected, byte-capped, and labeled contentTrust: "untrusted" . Tool output is content, not instruction. Human authority on consequences. Reads flow. Writes need a preview. Consequential actions need one exact, single-use approval bound to origin, tool, and normalized arguments. Change an argument and the grant is void. Verified closure. After a consequential action an inspector reads authoritative state and issues a receipt with no cookies, secrets, or raw bodies. Unknown outcome is reported as in_doubt , never as success. One concrete job, end to end The demo is not a feature tour. It is one incident. An alert fires in Grafana. Customer bookings in Cal.com are about to be affected. Neither site has WebMCP. The agent discovers the reviewed Grafana and Cal.com packs, installs them for this origin, and reads authoritative state. It never receives a credential. The agent prepares the exact continuity action and states what will change and how it will be inspected. The incident is still investigating . The human grants that exact action once, from a control the agent cannot reach. The agent executes once. The inspector verifies and issues one receipt. The incident is resolved . We poison the fingerprint. The pack refuses to run. We export the recorder draft. Every secret is gone before it can be published. Agents investigate. Humans authorize. Outcomes are verified. The user is the integration layer. Let an agent read freely and prepare freely, while every consequential step waits for a human decision the agent physically cannot forge. Hand a reviewer a receipt, not a screenshot, as proof of what changed. Teach a site once by observing its own traffic, then reuse that pack every session. The hardest part has never been the rendering. It's the dozens of small edits between "what if this street were safer?" and a picture you can bring to a city council meeting. WebMCP collapses that gap: the browser agent you already have becomes the operator of a professional design tool, in your own signed-in tab, with you watching every move. What it does Open 3dstreet.app in ChatGPT's in-app browser (or Chrome 149+) and the page registers 26 WebMCP tools with document.modelContext . No extension, no relay, no setup. Then just talk: "Make a street with two drive lanes, protected bike lanes, and wide sidewalks with street trees" : the agent composes a managed street segment by segment. Each segment type carries the same presets a human gets from the sidebar (surface, markings, vehicles, pedestrians), so a "bus lane" comes with buses and BUS stencils instead of a grey slab. "Set the location to 4th & King in San Francisco" : server-side elevation lookup anchors the scene into Google's 3D Tiles. getGeoContext then gives the agent a machine-readable bridge between scene coordinates and lat/lon, including the compass bearing of the street's centerline and both endpoints, so it can align a design with the real road. "Take a plan-view snapshot" : the tool orients a north-up top-down camera and returns an image content block plus camera metadata (tilt, north-up, ground extent), so the agent sees its own work and can self-correct. Every mutation goes through the editor's command system: fully undoable, visible live in a transcript panel ( webmcp · managedStreetCreate …), and gated by a one-click Read-only toggle. The first tool call of a session dismisses the welcome modal and opens the Console tab, so the human always sees the agent at work. Read tools ( getScene , getSessionInfo , listMixins , listSegmentPresets ) let the agent orient itself before acting, and creation tools wait for the street to actually mount and report back what rendered, so "created" is backed by geometry rather than a hopeful return value. How we built it One registry, three LLM consumers. 3DStreet already had a tool registry feeding an in-editor assistant and an MCP relay for desktop AI apps. During the submission period we added a third transport: a React hook that feature-detects document.modelContext and registers every tool via registerTool({ name, description, inputSchema, execute }) , funneling execution through one shared executor so WebMCP, the relay, and the in-editor chat behave identically. We registered Chrome origin-trial tokens for our domains, so stock Chrome 149–156 needs no flag . Then we iterated through four rounds of live testing with an OpenAI WebMCP agent, treating every failure as a tool-surface bug compiled by Sol 5.6, then reviewed and fixed with Fable 5.1: Round 1: safety rails. Destructive tools are fenced to the user-editable scene subtree (an agent can't delete the camera rig); component names are validated against A-Frame's registry so hallucinated writes fail loudly; every error is written for the model . Round 2: grounding. The agent couldn't tell which side of the street was north, put a bus model in the stencil painter (sideways buses), and reported success for content that never rendered. We added getGeoContext , north-up plan snapshots with camera metadata, schema validation for every street field with the valid values listed in the error, per-type segment presets, and post-create readback. Round 3: guidance. The agent built synthetic buildings on top of the real Google 3D Tiles ones, then verified from street level. Fix: tool descriptions now lead with the geo rule, plan view is the default verification snapshot in geo scenes, and each mutating result ends with a nextStep nudge. Round 4: persistence and payload. Agent-built scenes vanished on reload, and a full-res PNG snapshot was 2.5 MB of base64. Added saveScene / loadScene , an unsaved-edits nudge, a 1280 px JPEG snapshot, and the build version in getSessionInfo so an agent can tell when it's hitting a stale deploy. Challenges we ran into Auth inside agent browsers. Our first runs hit unreliable third-party sign-in popups in embedded agentic browsers, so we shipped a demo carve-out: geospatial anchoring works signed-out for California locations (server-enforced bounding box + expiry). Spec drift mid-trial. navigator.modelContext became document.modelContext in Chrome 150; bulk provideContext gave way to per-tool registerTool . We support both generations with feature detection. Testing a consumer that barely exists. Few agents call WebMCP tools yet, so we built a headless-Chromium harness that shims modelContext , registers the real page, and round-trips every tool, including verifying that the read-only gate and scene-containment guards refuse correctly. WebMCP has no system-prompt channel, so every description, error string, and nextStep line is agent UX. Three of our four testing rounds shipped mostly copy and defaults, not code. Existing tools allowed me to design slides, but I could not find one workspace that supported the complete reality of presenting: organizing a lesson, preparing visual content, writing speaker notes, controlling time, managing multiple screens, seeing what comes next, and annotating ideas live without interrupting the flow. I wanted the creative flexibility of a visual design tool, but shaped around how people actually teach and communicate. I needed to prepare everything in the browser, operate the presentation from a separate control screen, keep the audience view clean, and use pointers, highlights, drawings, and shapes while explaining. That personal need became Wilofox Deck Studio. When I discovered WebMCP, I saw a natural extension of the original vision: the workspace could become a shared environment where a person and an AI agent collaborate on the same presentation—not through a disconnected chatbot, but through structured actions connected to the real product. What it does Wilofox Deck Studio is a browser-based workspace for creating and delivering structured, interactive presentations. It is designed for educators, content creators, trainers, professionals, and live-stream presenters who need more than a traditional sequence of static slides. Users can: Create presentations from scratch, start from built-in examples, or import structured JSON. Organize content into sections and progressively ordered slides. Present using a single screen or a separate presenter-control window. See the current slide, upcoming slide, speaker notes, reference clocks, timer, and timing status. Navigate progressively or jump directly to a specific part of the presentation. Use a laser pointer, pen, highlighter, colors, arrows, rectangles, and other shapes during a live explanation. Zoom, pan, isolate the content or diagram, undo annotations, and temporarily black out the audience screen. Reserve an interface area for a camera or chat in an OBS composition. Export and reuse presentation content in formats such as JSON, HTML, PDF, and PowerPoint. How WebMCP is used WebMCP adds a structured agent layer to the existing presentation workflow. Wilofox exposes four tools: create_deck creates a complete, editable presentation from structured input. get_deck_context reads the current presentation state, including changes made manually by the user. update_slide updates one targeted slide while preserving the rest of the deck. navigate_to_slide moves the shared interface to a requested slide for review or presentation. These tools allow a compatible AI agent to understand and operate Wilofox without guessing where to click or attempting to interpret a complex editor only through pixels. The agent works with the same presentation state that the person can see and edit. There is no hidden agent-only copy. When a tool changes a slide or creates a deck, the result is validated, stored, rendered in the interface, and immediately available for human review. The user remains in control: every result can be inspected, edited manually, presented, exported, or replaced. Why WebMCP is a strong fit A presentation editor contains many controls, panels, fields, and possible actions. Asking an agent to operate that environment through visual guessing would be fragile and imprecise. WebMCP gives Wilofox a small, explicit set of meaningful capabilities with structured and bounded inputs. This makes agent behavior easier to understand, safer to execute, and more closely aligned with the user’s intention. A practical workflow can look like this: The agent creates a structured lesson. The educator reviews it and manually adjusts the content. The agent reads the latest shared state. The educator asks it to improve only one specific slide. The updated result appears inside the same editor. The person makes the final teaching and design decisions. This kind of collaboration would otherwise require copying content repeatedly between a chatbot and a presentation tool, manually reconstructing context, and risking the loss of previous edits. Human and agent collaboration Wilofox is designed around a simple principle: the agent contributes speed and structured assistance, while the person contributes judgment, subject knowledge, creativity, and final control. The shared presentation is the source of truth. Manual edits and agent actions use the same state, rendering, validation, and persistence flow. This prevents the agent experience from becoming a parallel prototype disconnected from the real application. For educators and presenters, this means less time transferring information between tools and more time reviewing, refining, and communicating ideas effectively. Multi-window presenter controls. Import and export workflows. Local state and persistence management. WebMCP was implemented through document.modelContext.registerTool() . Each tool includes: A clear name and description. An explicit JSON Schema. Bounded and validated inputs. Targeted execution behavior. Reuse of the application’s existing state and persistence functions. Visible results rendered through the normal interface. WebMCP support is implemented as a progressive enhancement. Browsers without WebMCP can still use the complete manual presentation experience.
