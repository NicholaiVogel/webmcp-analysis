# FROZEN SCORING RUBRIC (pre-registered before any Stage 1 review)

Scores are integers 1-10 on each of the FOUR official criteria. Every score carries:
rationale, evidence_surfaces, confidence (0-1). Reviewers use the full scale and these
band anchors verbatim. Category neutrality is absolute: a game, art piece, music tool,
CRM, weird toy, and developer tool are all eligible for 10/10 on every criterion if they
satisfy that criterion exceptionally well FOR WHAT THEY ARE TRYING TO BE.
No aesthetic penalties. Signs of hurried AI work matter only as actual product-quality
problems (incoherence, broken flow, placeholder content), never as style.

## WebMCP Leverage — what does WebMCP change for this product?
Judge the delta WebMCP makes to reliability, precision, structured state access,
shared human-agent state, repeatability, and capability. Tool COUNT is not leverage.
- 9-10 TRANSFORMATIVE: the intended experience is impractical without the structured
  agent surface; human and agent share live product state; the collaboration could not
  be replicated comparably well by a general agent driving the UI.
- 7-8 MAJOR DELTA: without WebMCP a general agent is markedly less reliable/precise
  here; the agent is central to the product, not a bolt-on.
- 5-6 MEANINGFUL DELTA: existing capability packaged into a genuinely better product
  experience via structured tools; same outcome otherwise possible with real friction.
- 3-4 MINOR DELTA: generic browser automation achieves comparable outcomes; WebMCP is
  a convenience layer.
- 1-2 COSMETIC: exposes an action a UI button already offers; no meaningful change.

## Execution — is the actual product coherent, intentional, complete for its scope?
Stage 1 scores EXECUTION_CLAIMED: coherence/intentionality/completeness as evidenced by
the packet (description + packaging + video frames/transcript). Thin evidence caps the
score (end-to-end credit requires end-to-end evidence) and lowers confidence.
Stage 2 may rescore as EXECUTION_OBSERVED; latest evidence-backed score replaces, never
sums.
- 9-10: all surfaces agree; central workflow demonstrably completes; states handled;
  feels finished for its stated scope.
- 7-8: strong coherence and intentionality; central workflow appears functional; minor
  unfinished edges.
- 5-6: plausibly real with visible gaps; scope unclear or core not demonstrated
  end-to-end.
- 3-4: thin prototype; contradictions between surfaces; placeholder content.
- 1-2: non-functional or incoherent for its claimed scope.

## Creativity & Ambition — novelty of concept and interaction model
Judge ONLY: novelty of the concept, novelty of the interaction model, originality
relative to existing concepts, ambition, memorable/surprising use of the medium, and
the depth with which the concept is pursued. WebMCP indispensability belongs under
WebMCP Leverage, NOT here: a project can be extremely creative even if another agent
API could theoretically have enabled something similar.
- 9-10: genuinely novel concept or interaction model; ambitious scope pursued deeply
  and coherently; memorable.
- 7-8: fresh synthesis or notably original interaction; ambitious for a hackathon.
- 5-6: competent take on a known category with a real new angle.
- 3-4: derivative; novelty mostly surface details.
- 1-2: clone/template with trivial changes.
"That the category exists" is never a penalty; judge the submitted concept.

## Potential Impact — credible value for the intended audience
Judge: is there a real audience; is there a real problem, desire, or use case for that
audience; does the demonstrated solution genuinely address it. NO commercial adoption,
monetization, market pull, or startup viability is required. Entertainment, play, art,
creative expression, learning, niche hobby use, experimentation, and community value
are legitimate forms of impact. A tiny audience fully justifies a top score.
- 9-10: specific real audience with a real problem/desire; solution demonstrably
  addresses it; credible value, appeal, usefulness, or meaning for that audience.
- 7-8: clear audience and problem, good fit, credible value.
- 5-6: identifiable audience, looser fit or partial address.
- 3-4: speculative audience or strained fit; impact mostly aspirational.
- 1-2: no coherent audience/problem pairing.
Audience size is NOT a multiplier.

## Aggregate & ranking
aggregate = leverage + execution + impact + creativity (4-40). Nothing else enters.
Tie-breaks: Leverage, then Execution, then Impact, then Creativity, then higher
combined evidence confidence, then slug alphabetical. See PROTOCOL.md Ranking section.

Like Comment Story Updates ShopCanvas: a visual WebMCP storefront. compare_products presents key tradeoffs side by side. inspect_product expands product details within stable bento cells. show_reviews filters critical low-light feedback. search_products reshapes the catalog around the request. Inspiration Shopping online often means repeatedly leaving the product you are considering. Specifications live in one section, reviews farther down the page, comparisons somewhere else, and the cart on another route. That visual travel creates cognitive travel: shoppers must remember their original question while the interface continuously changes context. At the same time, browser agents can feel disconnected from the websites they operate. Actions happen in the background, and users only see the final response in a chat. ShopCanvas began with a question: what if using an agent felt like having a conversation directly with the website? We wanted every request to produce an immediate, understandable visual response on one shared surface. What it does ShopCanvas is a fictional camera store presented as an adaptive bento-grid workspace. Users can ask a browser agent to: Search and sort products Focus on a specific camera Compare multiple cameras Filter product reviews Update a local mock cart ShopCanvas exposes these capabilities through five imperative WebMCP tools: search_products , inspect_product , compare_products , show_reviews , and set_cart_quantity . Every tool call visibly affects the page. The relevant cells expand smoothly, receive a temporary highlight, and display an acknowledgement naming the agent’s action. An accessible live region also announces what changed. All seven cells remain mounted in permanent grid positions. They expand or contract without teleporting, reordering, or appearing from nowhere. Dense content scrolls inside its cell while the overall page remains fitted to the viewport. Manual controls and agent actions use the same state transitions, so the user and agent always share one source of truth. A Back control restores previous canvas states, while session data persists locally between reloads. How we built it ShopCanvas is a static React and TypeScript application built with Vinext, Tailwind CSS, shadcn/ui, Zustand, Motion, and AJV. The mock catalog, reviews, product names, and imagery are bundled locally. There is no authentication, backend database, payment processing, or checkout flow. WebMCP tools are registered from the top-level document using document.modelContext.registerTool(...) . Each tool includes: A bounded JSON Schema Runtime AJV validation Rejection of unknown properties Cancellation support Concise, JSON-serializable results A visible UI acknowledgement A result returned only after the interface commits the change Both manual interactions and WebMCP calls dispatch the same semantic store actions. Session state is versioned and persisted in localStorage . Agent mutations are serialized, and cancelled operations use guarded rollback logic to avoid corrupting later changes. Because reviews represent user-generated content, review results are explicitly marked with untrustedContentHint: true . We used Playwright to validate the interface across desktop, tablet, mobile, and short-laptop viewports. Tests cover all five tools, persistence, malformed input, cancellation, concurrent calls, keyboard navigation, internal scrolling, fixed panel identity, responsive geometry, and visual regressions. A separate native-browser test discovers and executes the tools through the browser’s real document.modelContext implementation. Challenges we ran into The hardest challenge was preserving spatial continuity. Our early layouts replaced and reordered cells whenever the user entered a new state. Although functional, the result felt abrupt and contradicted the core idea of a stable workspace. We redesigned the grid so every cell has a permanent named slot. State changes now animate only the grid-track dimensions, allowing panels to expand and contract without being removed or moved. Fitting seven useful panels inside one viewport was another significant challenge. We avoided shrinking text until it became unreadable. Instead, each cell received a carefully bounded layout and its own keyboard-accessible scrolling region. We also had to coordinate asynchronous tool execution with visible feedback. Updating application state was not enough: the tool result needed to wait until the user could actually see the committed interface. Cancellation, overlapping calls, local persistence, and manual actions all had to remain consistent. Finally, WebMCP is still an evolving draft. We needed to accommodate differences between the current specification and native browser implementations while keeping the public tool surface small, predictable, and testable. Accomplishments that we're proud of We are especially proud that ShopCanvas makes agent activity visible without turning the interface into a debug console. Every successful tool call has a clear visual consequence: The action is named The affected cells are highlighted The content changes on screen The grid resizes smoothly The tool waits for the visible commit The user can reverse the state with Back We also maintained the identity and position of all seven panels across every state transition. The same interface works from a 320-pixel mobile viewport through desktop layouts without introducing page-level scrolling. The complete application uses fictional data and original visual assets, requires no account, and remains fully usable through manual controls when WebMCP is unavailable. What we learned We learned that good agentic UX is not only about exposing capable tools. Users need to understand what the agent changed, where it changed, and whether the action completed. Tool schemas are also part of the user experience. Small, bounded tools produced more predictable behavior than one large tool with many ambiguous responsibilities. Another important lesson was that a state update and a visible UI update are not the same event. Waiting for the interface to commit made the interaction feel significantly more trustworthy. Most importantly, spatial stability matters. When panels retain their location, users can build a mental map of the interface even while its proportions change. The long-term goal is broader than e-commerce: to explore websites as adaptive, shared canvases where people and agents collaborate visibly instead of exchanging commands with hidden background processes. Inspiration AI assistants can help plan a product, research an idea, or explain a difficult concept. But their best work often ends up buried inside a chat. The person then has to copy the result into a document, rebuild the relationships, and explain the same context again in the next conversation. I built Nodebook to give people and AI assistants a shared visual workspace. The person chooses the direction, while the assistant helps organize the work directly on the canvas. What Nodebook does Nodebook is an infinite visual canvas for planning, tracing, and learning. A workspace can contain three kinds of connected maps: Build maps for features, scope, progress, risks, and evidence Trace maps for user journeys, system flows, and failure paths Learn maps for concepts, examples, exercises, and quizzes People can create and edit nodes, decide what is in scope, track delivery, attach evidence, leave comments, and lock important decisions. An AI assistant can read the same workspace through WebMCP. It can understand which node the person selected, create connected maps, update plans, add evidence and comments, and highlight important paths. For example, a person can select “Crash-safe recovery,” click “Trace,” and ask the assistant to map the recovery process. The assistant can read that selection, create the complete flow, connect it to the original feature, highlight the riskiest path, and leave an explanation, all without the person copying the project into the prompt. Why WebMCP is a strong fit Nodebook is useful because the person and the assistant work with the same information. Without WebMCP, an assistant would need to interpret screenshots, click through the interface, or ask the person to repeatedly copy information into a chat. Those approaches are slow and can lose important details. With WebMCP, Nodebook gives the assistant clear actions for reading and changing the workspace. The assistant receives the exact nodes, relationships, comments, evidence, and selected task. Its changes then appear immediately on the canvas, where the person can inspect and edit them. This creates a better experience than an embedded chatbot. The conversation is not the final product. The shared, lasting workspace is. How I implemented WebMCP Nodebook registers 15 browser tools with document.modelContext.registerTool() . These tools allow an assistant to: Read the workspace, maps, nodes, comments, and human selection Search across titles, descriptions, tags, evidence, and comments Create linked Build, Trace, and Learn maps Add or update nodes and connections Make scope and delivery updates Attach evidence and attributed comments Focus nodes and highlight important paths Every request is checked before it changes the workspace. Invalid links, missing nodes, duplicate IDs, unsafe source URLs, oversized requests, and broken connections are rejected. Each successful assistant action is applied as one undoable change. Locked nodes are skipped, and assistants are not given a tool that permanently deletes nodes. This keeps important decisions under human control. How it was built Nodebook is built with Next.js, React, TypeScript, and React Flow. Dagre arranges assistant-created maps into a predictable layout. Zustand manages the workspace, while Zod checks tool requests and imported files. The workspace is saved in the browser using IndexedDB. There are no accounts, API routes, analytics, cloud database, or Nodebook storage server. People can export the complete workspace as JSON and import it again later. The included demo contains a product roadmap, user journeys, a learning map, release checks, evidence, comments, locked decisions, and a research parking lot. Challenges The hardest part was making assistant actions feel like real product actions instead of hidden changes. I needed to make sure that a large graph update either succeeded completely or changed nothing. I also had to keep the visible canvas, undo history, browser storage, and WebMCP responses in agreement. Another challenge was balancing useful assistant access with human control. Nodebook solves this with strict validation, visible activity, attributed comments, undo support, locked nodes, and human-only deletion. Accomplishments I am proud that Nodebook is a complete working application rather than only a WebMCP experiment. It includes: 15 working WebMCP tools A detailed, editable demo workspace Linked maps with breadcrumbs Evidence and human-assistant comments Scope, progress, and learning states Path highlighting and automatic layout Undo and redo for assistant changes Local browser persistence Safe JSON import and export Automated unit and browser tests What I learned I learned that good human-AI collaboration needs more than a prompt box. The assistant needs clear actions and reliable context. The person needs to see what changed, understand why it changed, and remain able to edit, lock, or undo the result. WebMCP makes that possible using the website’s real data and actions. What’s next Next, I would improve keyboard navigation, test larger workspaces, add more starter templates, and make recovery guidance even clearer. I would also explore shareable, read-only workspace snapshots while keeping Nodebook’s main promise: the person owns the workspace and stays in control. ABOUT TEXT (untrusted evidence):
A conversational credit ledger that helps small businesses and their AI agents manage customer debt together, without taking financial control away from the human. Like Comment Story Updates Talli helps small businesses and informal traders record and manage customer credit using ordinary speech, text, or Telegram. Instead of filling out accounting forms, a merchant can simply say, “Adele took goods worth $40 and will pay on Friday,” or “She paid $10 today.” Talli turns those conversations into structured customer balances and transaction histories. Through WebMCP, a browser agent can securely read the same live ledger, answer questions, identify overdue debts, and prepare proposed financial updates. However, the agent cannot finalize those changes. The merchant reviews and confirms every consequential update inside Talli’s visible interface. The Problem Millions of small businesses and informal traders sell goods on credit every day. A customer may take food, clothing, supplies, or other goods and promise to pay later. The business owner needs to remember: who took the goods how much they owe when they promised to pay whether they made a partial payment how much remains outstanding which customers are overdue Many business owners manage this information using paper notebooks, memory, calculator notes, chat messages, or a mixture of all four. This creates serious problems. Records can be lost, payments may not be reflected correctly, and business owners can struggle to explain how a customer’s balance was calculated. When several customers have similar names, it is also easy to update the wrong person’s record. Traditional accounting applications can help, but they are often designed around forms, tables, categories, and bookkeeping terminology. For someone who is busy serving customers and may not consider themselves an accountant, recording a simple credit transaction can feel like administrative work. The real challenge is not that these business owners do not understand their businesses. The challenge is that most financial software does not work the way they naturally communicate. The Solution Talli is a conversational credit ledger built around the way small businesses already talk about customer debt. Instead of completing a long form, a merchant can speak or type naturally: “Adele took goods worth $40 and will pay next Friday.” Talli converts that statement into a structured financial record containing the customer, amount, currency, transaction type, and relevant date. Later, the merchant can say: “Adele paid $10.” Talli records the partial payment, updates Adele’s outstanding balance, and preserves the history of how that balance changed. The merchant can also ask questions such as: “How much does Adele still owe?” “Who owes me money?” “Which payments are overdue?” “Show me Adele’s transaction history.” “How much have my customers paid?” “Mark this debt as settled.” Talli supports voice, typed conversations, a web interface, and Telegram. The same underlying ledger can therefore remain useful while the merchant moves between the channels they already use. Talli also understands that financial records require caution. If a request is unclear, Talli does not silently guess. It asks for clarification before allowing the wrong customer or obligation to be changed. The WebMCP Extension For the WebMCP Challenge, I extended Talli so that the live website can expose its financial capabilities directly to browser-based AI agents. Without WebMCP, an agent trying to assist a Talli user would have to inspect the page, interpret text on the screen, locate buttons, fill forms, and simulate clicks. That approach is fragile and can be especially dangerous when the website contains financial information. With WebMCP, Talli explicitly tells the agent what it can do, what information each operation requires, and which operations are read-only. The agent can discover structured tools for searching customers, checking balances, reviewing histories, finding overdue debts, and preparing ledger changes. It receives structured results from the same live ledger the person is viewing. This creates a shared workspace between the merchant, the browser agent, and Talli. A Typical Human-Agent Workflow Imagine that a merchant asks their browser agent: “Check Talli and tell me how much my customers owe me.” The agent calls Talli’s get_ledger_summary tool and receives a structured summary from the current ledger. The merchant then says: “Adele paid $10. Update her balance.” The agent calls prepare_ledger_mutation , but Talli discovers that several customers match the name “Adele.” Instead of choosing one, Talli returns clarification_required with a short list of matching customers and their balances. The ledger remains unchanged. The agent can then ask: “I found more than one Adele. Which customer made the payment?” After the merchant identifies the correct customer, the agent prepares the payment again. Talli validates the request and displays a visible review card containing the exact proposed change. The merchant reviews the amount and customer, then clicks Confirm inside Talli. Only at that point does the ledger change. This workflow demonstrates more than an agent operating a website. The agent helps with interpretation and preparation, Talli enforces financial safety, and the human retains final authority. Why Talli Is a Strong Fit for WebMCP Talli is a strong WebMCP use case because customer credit management combines repetitive work with consequential financial decisions. An AI agent is well suited to: searching records summarizing balances finding overdue obligations reviewing transaction histories translating a natural-language request into a structured proposal However, an agent should not silently make an irreversible or incorrect financial change. WebMCP provides the structured boundary Talli needs. It allows the agent to interact with the application through clearly defined tools instead of guessing its way through the interface. Talli builds on that boundary with three important rules: Read operations can happen directly. Financial mutations must first become proposals. Only the human can confirm a proposal in Talli’s visible interface. The most important behavior is not simply that Talli knows how to act. It also knows when not to act. If two customers match a name, an obligation cannot be safely identified, or the ledger has changed since a proposal was prepared, Talli pauses the operation instead of guessing. This makes WebMCP part of the product’s safety model, not just another way to call backend functions. How Talli Creates a Better User Experience Talli reduces the amount of effort required to maintain accurate credit records. The merchant does not need to learn database fields, accounting terminology, or a complicated menu system. They can continue communicating naturally while Talli and their agent handle the structured work underneath. WebMCP improves this experience in several ways: The agent can access reliable structured data instead of trying to read values from the visual interface. The merchant can ask broader questions without manually searching through individual customer records. The agent can prepare a transaction while the merchant remains focused on running the business. Ambiguous requests produce clarification instead of silent errors. Proposed financial changes are shown in a visible, understandable review card. The merchant keeps control over the final decision. The agent and the person work from the same current ledger rather than separate or outdated copies of the data. Talli remains usable in browsers that do not support WebMCP. WebMCP is added as a progressive enhancement, so the normal voice, text, web, and Telegram experiences continue to work.
