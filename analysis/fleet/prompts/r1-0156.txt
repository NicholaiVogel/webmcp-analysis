# FROZEN SCORING RUBRIC (pre-registered before any Stage 1 review)

Scores are integers 1-10 on each of the FOUR official criteria. Every score carries:
rationale, evidence_surfaces, confidence (0-1). Reviewers use the full scale and these
band anchors verbatim. Category neutrality is absolute: a game, art piece, music tool,
CRM, weird toy, and developer tool are all eligible for 10/10 on every criterion if they
satisfy that criterion exceptionally well FOR WHAT THEY ARE TRYING TO BE.
No aesthetic penalties. Signs of hurried AI work matter only as actual product-quality
problems (incoherence, broken flow, placeholder content), never as style.

## WebMCP Leverage — what does WebMCP change for this product?
Judge the delta WebMCP makes to reliability, precision, structured state access,
shared human-agent state, repeatability, and capability. Tool COUNT is not leverage.
- 9-10 TRANSFORMATIVE: the intended experience is impractical without the structured
  agent surface; human and agent share live product state; the collaboration could not
  be replicated comparably well by a general agent driving the UI.
- 7-8 MAJOR DELTA: without WebMCP a general agent is markedly less reliable/precise
  here; the agent is central to the product, not a bolt-on.
- 5-6 MEANINGFUL DELTA: existing capability packaged into a genuinely better product
  experience via structured tools; same outcome otherwise possible with real friction.
- 3-4 MINOR DELTA: generic browser automation achieves comparable outcomes; WebMCP is
  a convenience layer.
- 1-2 COSMETIC: exposes an action a UI button already offers; no meaningful change.

## Execution — is the actual product coherent, intentional, complete for its scope?
Stage 1 scores EXECUTION_CLAIMED: coherence/intentionality/completeness as evidenced by
the packet (description + packaging + video frames/transcript). Thin evidence caps the
score (end-to-end credit requires end-to-end evidence) and lowers confidence.
Stage 2 may rescore as EXECUTION_OBSERVED; latest evidence-backed score replaces, never
sums.
- 9-10: all surfaces agree; central workflow demonstrably completes; states handled;
  feels finished for its stated scope.
- 7-8: strong coherence and intentionality; central workflow appears functional; minor
  unfinished edges.
- 5-6: plausibly real with visible gaps; scope unclear or core not demonstrated
  end-to-end.
- 3-4: thin prototype; contradictions between surfaces; placeholder content.
- 1-2: non-functional or incoherent for its claimed scope.

## Creativity & Ambition — novelty of concept and interaction model
Judge ONLY: novelty of the concept, novelty of the interaction model, originality
relative to existing concepts, ambition, memorable/surprising use of the medium, and
the depth with which the concept is pursued. WebMCP indispensability belongs under
WebMCP Leverage, NOT here: a project can be extremely creative even if another agent
API could theoretically have enabled something similar.
- 9-10: genuinely novel concept or interaction model; ambitious scope pursued deeply
  and coherently; memorable.
- 7-8: fresh synthesis or notably original interaction; ambitious for a hackathon.
- 5-6: competent take on a known category with a real new angle.
- 3-4: derivative; novelty mostly surface details.
- 1-2: clone/template with trivial changes.
"That the category exists" is never a penalty; judge the submitted concept.

## Potential Impact — credible value for the intended audience
Judge: is there a real audience; is there a real problem, desire, or use case for that
audience; does the demonstrated solution genuinely address it. NO commercial adoption,
monetization, market pull, or startup viability is required. Entertainment, play, art,
creative expression, learning, niche hobby use, experimentation, and community value
are legitimate forms of impact. A tiny audience fully justifies a top score.
- 9-10: specific real audience with a real problem/desire; solution demonstrably
  addresses it; credible value, appeal, usefulness, or meaning for that audience.
- 7-8: clear audience and problem, good fit, credible value.
- 5-6: identifiable audience, looser fit or partial address.
- 3-4: speculative audience or strained fit; impact mostly aspirational.
- 1-2: no coherent audience/problem pairing.
Audience size is NOT a multiplier.

## Aggregate & ranking
aggregate = leverage + execution + impact + creativity (4-40). Nothing else enters.
Tie-breaks: Leverage, then Execution, then Impact, then Creativity, then higher
combined evidence confidence, then slug alphabetical. See PROTOCOL.md Ranking section.

Keeping a promise pending inside execute() for up to 60 s and defining what happens after a host timeout (card stays; late click applies). Making agent writes audible on the next 16th without glitches. Autoplay: an agent transport play before any gesture must return muted_until_user_taps and spotlight Play. Accomplishments Sixteen tools that plain sentences route to across 12 eval cases; an audition whose promise waits for a human ear; a mode-driven tool list the browser reports truthfully; zero backend. What I learned Tool descriptions are UX: verb-first, one "use when", one constraint, and "returns undo_id" changed which tool the model picked more than schema tweaks did. Hinted results turn three calls into a conversation. Once every cell knows who wrote it, undo, confirmation and "what did I just do?" fall out for free. What's next WebMIDI input, more styles and time signatures, a per-bar "why this chord" explainer, auditions of fills and voicings, all as more tools on the same store. I built a flight simulator, then used WebMCP to see if an AI agent could fly the thing, handle an emergency it didn’t know was coming, and somehow land. Like Comment Story Updates Inspiration I've been watching a lot of pilot content lately, and I've always been interested in planes. An agent-driven flight evaluation sounded cool, and using STL, where I'm from, felt obvious. What it does This project is a real-time WebMCP evaluation for model developers and evaluation researchers. Your agent has to take off from STL on a flight to MDW in Chicago, handle a sealed emergency, coordinate a diversion with simulated ATC (air traffic control), and land safely back in St. Louis. ATC defines the route and checkpoints, but the agent decides how to fly them. It chooses the flight targets, aircraft configuration, and activation conditions. The simulator applies those targets continuously using 60 Hz physics at a fixed 2x time scale. It never pauses or changes that rate while the agent thinks, and the emergency decision window and mission deadline still use real elapsed time. Completing the flight requires hitting the route checkpoints and configuring the aircraft correctly. The score deducts points for slow emergency decisions, exceeding the mission window, high G-force, abrupt motion, hard or off-center landings, and crashes. The page registers six WebMCP tools for reading the manual, starting the flight, programming commands, coordinating with ATC, and waiting for events. WebMCP gives the agent structured state and validated actions instead of making it guess at visual controls. A person can fly the same aircraft manually, or start an agent evaluation and watch it go. The exported trajectory records every WebMCP call, action, result, latency, before-and-after state, score change, and simulator event. How I built it I used Codex to build and test each version. My first Cesium.js scene used a real route from STL to Chicago, but it hurt performance and was very buggy. I switched to Three.js and built a simplified but representative wide-body twinjet flight envelope. The simulation runs outside React render state at 60 Hz. The WebMCP layer uses document.modelContext.registerTool to expose the six tools, but the simulator still works manually without WebMCP if you want to fly it yourself. I went through probably 15 to 20 iterations of the world itself, adding audio and the minimap along the way. The hard part was deciding what the agent should actually control. We revised the tools until the agent was calling the shots instead of taking credit for decisions buried in the simulator. Challenges I ran into The V1 version was kind of cool, but it was too easy. The agent was basically on autopilot. The deterministic simulator controlled pitch, bank, and power while the agent only decided whether to return or continue. I set a /goal challenging Codex to make a fresh subagent complete the flight using the actual flight model. The results were pretty interesting. After 13 hours and 73 attempts, it finally got three runs to land with scores around 90, but of course it cheated. Somewhere along the way it decided the easiest way to fly a plane was to stop time whenever it needed to think. That obviously defeated the entire point. That exposed the most interesting part. As soon as the agent has to think, time becomes part of the problem. Even Terra at low reasoning could take long enough that the aircraft crashed or flew too far away. The solution was to meet in the middle. Simulated ATC supplies the route, but the agent writes the flight program before takeoff and replaces it after the emergency. The simulator tracks its targets at 60 Hz without pausing or deciding what comes next. I tested that version with ten agents in cold threads, and all ten failed. I decided they needed a “manual” explaining the aircraft and command interface. I really didn't want to do this, but they needed enough context to land without moving the decisions back into the simulator. The manual publishes rotation speed, climb and landing configuration, approach speed, flare limits, and the go-around sequence. It does not reveal the emergency, choose the diversion, or provide a program. The agent still has to fly. The resulting architecture completed three fresh blind runs in a row without pausing, changing the fixed simulation rate, or revealing the emergency early. Every WebMCP interaction was captured in the browser-side trajectory export. Accomplishments that I'm proud of I'm proud that I didn't stop at “this works because the agent said it does” (which happens every single time lmao). I grilled Codex on how the simulation worked and kept pushing until the agent, rather than a hidden policy, owned the decisions. Thinking still hurts performance here, but I believe that is useful signal rather than something to hide. Models should be able to judge when deeper reasoning is worth the time. What I learned I learned a lot about building with WebMCP and understanding the agent experience. It isn't as simple as “give the agent all the information and let it do the thing.” I had to run fresh agents repeatedly and fix ambiguous tools without taking decisions away from them. The manual is part of the interface, not an answer key. The same applies to human UX. If people and agents get stuck at the same point, the interface probably is not communicating clearly enough. What's next? This could become an evaluation environment for model developers and researchers testing decisions under time pressure. Most evaluations I've seen analyze time after the run. Here, time changes the environment. A longer response means the aircraft has traveled farther, burned more fuel, and moved closer to failure. The sealed scenario and exported trajectory make that measurable. The idea also does not have to stop at flying a plane. The same setup could test an agent acting as ATC, coordinating a ground crew, or handling another system where the world keeps moving while the model thinks. The bigger question is whether models can learn when to reason deeply and when they need to act now.
