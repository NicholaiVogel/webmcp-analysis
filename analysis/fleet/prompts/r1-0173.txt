# FROZEN SCORING RUBRIC (pre-registered before any Stage 1 review)

Scores are integers 1-10 on each of the FOUR official criteria. Every score carries:
rationale, evidence_surfaces, confidence (0-1). Reviewers use the full scale and these
band anchors verbatim. Category neutrality is absolute: a game, art piece, music tool,
CRM, weird toy, and developer tool are all eligible for 10/10 on every criterion if they
satisfy that criterion exceptionally well FOR WHAT THEY ARE TRYING TO BE.
No aesthetic penalties. Signs of hurried AI work matter only as actual product-quality
problems (incoherence, broken flow, placeholder content), never as style.

## WebMCP Leverage — what does WebMCP change for this product?
Judge the delta WebMCP makes to reliability, precision, structured state access,
shared human-agent state, repeatability, and capability. Tool COUNT is not leverage.
- 9-10 TRANSFORMATIVE: the intended experience is impractical without the structured
  agent surface; human and agent share live product state; the collaboration could not
  be replicated comparably well by a general agent driving the UI.
- 7-8 MAJOR DELTA: without WebMCP a general agent is markedly less reliable/precise
  here; the agent is central to the product, not a bolt-on.
- 5-6 MEANINGFUL DELTA: existing capability packaged into a genuinely better product
  experience via structured tools; same outcome otherwise possible with real friction.
- 3-4 MINOR DELTA: generic browser automation achieves comparable outcomes; WebMCP is
  a convenience layer.
- 1-2 COSMETIC: exposes an action a UI button already offers; no meaningful change.

## Execution — is the actual product coherent, intentional, complete for its scope?
Stage 1 scores EXECUTION_CLAIMED: coherence/intentionality/completeness as evidenced by
the packet (description + packaging + video frames/transcript). Thin evidence caps the
score (end-to-end credit requires end-to-end evidence) and lowers confidence.
Stage 2 may rescore as EXECUTION_OBSERVED; latest evidence-backed score replaces, never
sums.
- 9-10: all surfaces agree; central workflow demonstrably completes; states handled;
  feels finished for its stated scope.
- 7-8: strong coherence and intentionality; central workflow appears functional; minor
  unfinished edges.
- 5-6: plausibly real with visible gaps; scope unclear or core not demonstrated
  end-to-end.
- 3-4: thin prototype; contradictions between surfaces; placeholder content.
- 1-2: non-functional or incoherent for its claimed scope.

## Creativity & Ambition — novelty of concept and interaction model
Judge ONLY: novelty of the concept, novelty of the interaction model, originality
relative to existing concepts, ambition, memorable/surprising use of the medium, and
the depth with which the concept is pursued. WebMCP indispensability belongs under
WebMCP Leverage, NOT here: a project can be extremely creative even if another agent
API could theoretically have enabled something similar.
- 9-10: genuinely novel concept or interaction model; ambitious scope pursued deeply
  and coherently; memorable.
- 7-8: fresh synthesis or notably original interaction; ambitious for a hackathon.
- 5-6: competent take on a known category with a real new angle.
- 3-4: derivative; novelty mostly surface details.
- 1-2: clone/template with trivial changes.
"That the category exists" is never a penalty; judge the submitted concept.

## Potential Impact — credible value for the intended audience
Judge: is there a real audience; is there a real problem, desire, or use case for that
audience; does the demonstrated solution genuinely address it. NO commercial adoption,
monetization, market pull, or startup viability is required. Entertainment, play, art,
creative expression, learning, niche hobby use, experimentation, and community value
are legitimate forms of impact. A tiny audience fully justifies a top score.
- 9-10: specific real audience with a real problem/desire; solution demonstrably
  addresses it; credible value, appeal, usefulness, or meaning for that audience.
- 7-8: clear audience and problem, good fit, credible value.
- 5-6: identifiable audience, looser fit or partial address.
- 3-4: speculative audience or strained fit; impact mostly aspirational.
- 1-2: no coherent audience/problem pairing.
Audience size is NOT a multiplier.

## Aggregate & ranking
aggregate = leverage + execution + impact + creativity (4-40). Nothing else enters.
Tie-breaks: Leverage, then Execution, then Impact, then Creativity, then higher
combined evidence confidence, then slug alphabetical. See PROTOCOL.md Ranking section.

At every branch, I made sure to prepare at least three lines: one for a good reaction, one for a bad reaction, and one for a reaction that was hard to read. I had not always worked that way. Countless times, when the audience did not respond as expected, I forced myself to continue with the material exactly as I had prepared it and lost heart partway through. The method above grew directly from reflecting on those failures. Strangely, once I began preparing responses for several plausible cases, I sometimes found that I could hold the audience’s attention with improvisations I had not prepared at all. However, that kind of advance planning had several drawbacks. Writing down all the branches in text documents or data files was cumbersome. Listing several options at a single branch point quickly made the page cluttered. I could not always cover all the Situations that might arise at each branch point. From one person’s perspective, it is easy to overlook things. I thought WebMCP might be exactly the right mechanism for solving these problems. That idea led me to create Moshimo Tag: I felt that if this kind of preparation could be applied to a broader range of events, it might help many people. Most people do not examine multiple scenarios carefully enough. Even among the performers who shared the stage with me, many imposed a self-centered, single-track script that created a divide between the stage and the audience. People in general likewise tend to write schedules around the path where everything goes as planned and stop there. Yet even in real events such as trips, interviews, and dates, lateness, forgotten items, weather, and reactions that differ from expectations often force plans to change. Even when people understand the importance of advance planning, it takes effort to think through these possibilities beforehand, identify where each one relates to the Plan, and write a countermeasure for each. It is only natural that people would rather avoid such meticulous preparation. If WebMCP can make it easier, I believe more people may be able to avoid failure. Plan └─ What-if ├─ Situation 1 │ ├─ main countermeasure │ ├─ Plan B 1 countermeasure │ ├─ Plan B 2 countermeasure │ └─ ... └─ Situation 2 ├─ main countermeasure └─ ... Your agent reads the contract, flags what will cost you, and drafts the fixes as redlines you can accept or reject. It has no tool that edits the document. Not by policy. By construction. Like 1 Comment Story Updates Inspiration Everyone has signed a contract they didn't fully read. The current answer is to paste it into a chat window, which gets you advice about the document: a paragraph you then retype into the file yourself. We wanted the agent working inside the contract, with one hard rule: it can suggest anything and change nothing. What it does Redline is a contract review workspace for humans and their agents. The agent reads the agreement clause by clause, flags risks with severity and plain-English consequences, compares terms against a market-standard playbook, and drafts replacement language. Every change it suggests arrives as a redline — old text struck through, proposed text in green, reasoning attached, that a human accepts or rejects. Accepting is the only action that alters the contract, and no agent can take it. Why this is a strong fit for WebMCP Contract review is stateful, multi-step, and unforgiving of hallucination, exactly where screen-scraping agents fail and where a typed tool surface shines. Three things WebMCP makes possible that a chat upload cannot: The output is document state, not prose. A suggestion is an object anchored to clause 6 with an accept button, an author, and a status, not a sentence to transcribe. The document is live. Tools read the contract as it currently stands, so "re-check the clauses I just changed" works. An upload is a snapshot that goes stale immediately. The safety is structural. Any AI with write access is safe only while it behaves. Here there is no mutation tool registered, and the server refuses any write tagged as an agent. "Please don't edit without asking" versus "there is no API to edit" is the whole difference. Typed schemas help too: flag_risky_clause requires a clause id that exists, so the agent cannot cite clause 27 of a 13-clause contract the way chat routinely does. How it creates a better user experience You stop being a transcription layer between a chat window and a document. The agent does the reading, the comparing, and the drafting; you do the deciding, on a screen that shows exactly what would change, why, and who suggested it. Rejections carry reasons the agent can read back, so the review gets sharper as it goes. And because the tool surface is public, you bring your own agent, the one that knows your last eight contracts, your rates, and your risk tolerance. A document dropped into a fresh chat window knows nothing about the person dropping it. What people and agents can do together that was difficult before Work on the same legal document simultaneously, with a guarantee specifying who can change it. Before WebMCP, an agent either had no access (advice you retype) or full access (write permission you have to trust). Redline is the third option: full analytical reach, zero write capability , enforced by which tools exist rather than by how well the model behaves. The phase model makes that dynamic. In analysis, the agent has 8 tools and literally cannot propose. In redlining, it has 12. Once the contract is executed, it has 5, all read-only — a signed contract cannot be marked up, and the agent's own capabilities reflect that the moment the phase changes. How we implemented WebMCP Twelve tools registered through document.modelContext.registerTool (with a navigator.modelContext fallback for older Chrome previews), unregistered via AbortSignal whenever the review phase changes: document.modelContext.registerTool({ name: "propose_edit", description: "Propose replacement text for a clause. This does NOT change the contract — it " + "creates a redline the human accepts or rejects.", inputSchema: { type: "object", properties: { clauseId: { type: "string" }, proposedText: { type: "string" }, rationale: { type: "string", maxLength: 400 }, }, required: ["clauseId", "proposedText", "rationale"], additionalProperties: false, }, execute: async (input) => { const { proposalId } = await api.propose(reviewId, { type: "replace", ...input, via: "agent" }); return `Proposal ${proposalId} created. Status: pending_human_approval — the contract ` + `is unchanged until a human accepts it. Use list_proposals for the outcome.`; }, }, { signal: phaseController.signal }); Two design decisions did most of the work: Errors are written to be recovered from. "Error: no clause c27. Valid ids are: c1, c2, …" tells the agent how to fix its own mistake instead of leaving it to guess. Descriptions are prompts. Each says when to call the tool and what to read first, which is why agents chain them sensibly without being told the order. Stack: Express + vanilla JS, server-sent events for live sync across everyone reviewing, Upstash Redis in production so reviews survive serverless cold starts. Challenges we ran into Making the guarantee real rather than rhetorical. It isn't enough to omit an accept tool; the server also rejects any resolve request tagged via: "agent" , and phase changes are human-only. Defense in depth, so the claim survives a hostile reading. Writing tool descriptions models actually follow. Early versions had agents proposing edits to clauses they hadn't read. Explicitly saying so in the description fixed it. The WebMCP surface is still moving between document.modelContext and the earlier navigator.modelContext , so registration goes through a small adapter. What we learned Capability design is the safety story. Every guardrail you can move from the prompt into the tool surface is a guarantee instead of a hope — and phase-scoped registration means the answer to "what can this agent do right now?" is a list you can print, not a judgment call. What's next for Redline Upload your own contract; a shared playbook per organization so proposals match house style; multi-party review where the other side's agent negotiates back; and export to tracked-changes DOCX so the redlines land in the tools lawyers already use. A signed-in tab already carries every credential and permission the human has. If that tab registers tools with document.modelContext.registerTool , the agent gets a typed, narrow, inspectable interface to the site, and the vendor never has to ship a line of code. Adoption inverts. It no longer waits on ten thousand vendors. It happens one user, one tab, one reviewed pack at a time. What Agentready Network does Agentready Network is a local-first registry and runtime of reviewed action packs . A pack describes what a site can do, on exactly which origin, with which risk labels. The runtime turns that pack into live WebMCP tools inside the user's own signed-in session, then wraps every call in the safety the vendor never provided: Exact origins. A pack binds to one host and a reviewed fingerprint. Drift fails closed. Declarative, never executable. Manifests are JSON. The registry cannot ship code into your browser. Bounded, untrusted output. Results are allowlist-projected, byte-capped, and labeled contentTrust: "untrusted" . Tool output is content, not instruction. Human authority on consequences. Reads flow. Writes need a preview. Consequential actions need one exact, single-use approval bound to origin, tool, and normalized arguments. Change an argument and the grant is void. Verified closure. After a consequential action an inspector reads authoritative state and issues a receipt with no cookies, secrets, or raw bodies. Unknown outcome is reported as in_doubt , never as success. The human grants that exact action once, from a control the agent cannot reach. The agent executes once. The inspector verifies and issues one receipt. The incident is resolved . We poison the fingerprint. The pack refuses to run. We export the recorder draft. Every secret is gone before it can be published. Agents investigate. Humans authorize. Outcomes are verified. The user is the integration layer. Let an agent read freely and prepare freely, while every consequential step waits for a human decision the agent physically cannot forge. Hand a reviewer a receipt, not a screenshot, as proof of what changed. Teach a site once by observing its own traffic, then reuse that pack every session. WebMCP also gives us the one thing a bookmarklet never had: a standard discovery surface. Any agent that speaks document.modelContext finds our tools without knowing we exist. Reviewed adapters for Cal.com and Grafana were reverse-engineered on the wire, not from source. On the left there's a plan that looks like something an architect would hand you: grid, thick wall bands with the doors and windows cut out, swing arcs, dimensions. It can read the plan, ask where a bed would fit, place and move things, paint a wall, resize the room, cut a window, move the camera, or lay out a whole option. You can put rooms next to each other on one sheet, they snap edge to edge and refuse to overlap, and if you cut a doorway through a shared wall it opens in both rooms. How we built it It's a Vite + React + TypeScript app, no backend, three.js for the 3D through react-three-fiber. I built it in three days with an AI coding agent doing most of the typing and me doing most of the complaining. Underneath there's a plain TypeScript engine that doesn't know the UI exists. It computes footprints and overlaps, whether a door can swing, whether you can reach everything, walkway width on a 10 cm grid, a rough daylight model, budget, and it can invert every operation so undo is exact. Homes sit on top of that: snapping, overlap checks, and a doorway stored as one record that owns an opening in two rooms. The WebMCP part registers every tool on document.modelContext with a schema and a readOnlyHint where it applies. The selection tools live in their own group behind an AbortController, so when you click a different chair the old group is torn down and a new one registered. All the editing tools share one write path, which means they all answer the same way: what happened, what's now broken, the new numbers. That turned out to matter more than anything else I did. Since most browsers don't have document.modelContext yet, the app has a fake one built in and a dev panel (Cmd+Shift+D) where you can call any tool by hand. Honestly that's where I spent most of the time. There are 309 tests and a Playwright script that runs the real app through 44 screenshots. Challenges we ran into The first time I opened the DevTools WebMCP panel it was empty. Every tool registered without an error, and Chrome just didn't list any.
