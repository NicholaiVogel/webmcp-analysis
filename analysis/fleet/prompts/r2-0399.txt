# FROZEN SCORING RUBRIC (pre-registered before any Stage 1 review)

Scores are integers 1-10 on each of the FOUR official criteria. Every score carries:
rationale, evidence_surfaces, confidence (0-1). Reviewers use the full scale and these
band anchors verbatim. Category neutrality is absolute: a game, art piece, music tool,
CRM, weird toy, and developer tool are all eligible for 10/10 on every criterion if they
satisfy that criterion exceptionally well FOR WHAT THEY ARE TRYING TO BE.
No aesthetic penalties. Signs of hurried AI work matter only as actual product-quality
problems (incoherence, broken flow, placeholder content), never as style.

## WebMCP Leverage — what does WebMCP change for this product?
Judge the delta WebMCP makes to reliability, precision, structured state access,
shared human-agent state, repeatability, and capability. Tool COUNT is not leverage.
- 9-10 TRANSFORMATIVE: the intended experience is impractical without the structured
  agent surface; human and agent share live product state; the collaboration could not
  be replicated comparably well by a general agent driving the UI.
- 7-8 MAJOR DELTA: without WebMCP a general agent is markedly less reliable/precise
  here; the agent is central to the product, not a bolt-on.
- 5-6 MEANINGFUL DELTA: existing capability packaged into a genuinely better product
  experience via structured tools; same outcome otherwise possible with real friction.
- 3-4 MINOR DELTA: generic browser automation achieves comparable outcomes; WebMCP is
  a convenience layer.
- 1-2 COSMETIC: exposes an action a UI button already offers; no meaningful change.

## Execution — is the actual product coherent, intentional, complete for its scope?
Stage 1 scores EXECUTION_CLAIMED: coherence/intentionality/completeness as evidenced by
the packet (description + packaging + video frames/transcript). Thin evidence caps the
score (end-to-end credit requires end-to-end evidence) and lowers confidence.
Stage 2 may rescore as EXECUTION_OBSERVED; latest evidence-backed score replaces, never
sums.
- 9-10: all surfaces agree; central workflow demonstrably completes; states handled;
  feels finished for its stated scope.
- 7-8: strong coherence and intentionality; central workflow appears functional; minor
  unfinished edges.
- 5-6: plausibly real with visible gaps; scope unclear or core not demonstrated
  end-to-end.
- 3-4: thin prototype; contradictions between surfaces; placeholder content.
- 1-2: non-functional or incoherent for its claimed scope.

## Creativity & Ambition — novelty of concept and interaction model
Judge ONLY: novelty of the concept, novelty of the interaction model, originality
relative to existing concepts, ambition, memorable/surprising use of the medium, and
the depth with which the concept is pursued. WebMCP indispensability belongs under
WebMCP Leverage, NOT here: a project can be extremely creative even if another agent
API could theoretically have enabled something similar.
- 9-10: genuinely novel concept or interaction model; ambitious scope pursued deeply
  and coherently; memorable.
- 7-8: fresh synthesis or notably original interaction; ambitious for a hackathon.
- 5-6: competent take on a known category with a real new angle.
- 3-4: derivative; novelty mostly surface details.
- 1-2: clone/template with trivial changes.
"That the category exists" is never a penalty; judge the submitted concept.

## Potential Impact — credible value for the intended audience
Judge: is there a real audience; is there a real problem, desire, or use case for that
audience; does the demonstrated solution genuinely address it. NO commercial adoption,
monetization, market pull, or startup viability is required. Entertainment, play, art,
creative expression, learning, niche hobby use, experimentation, and community value
are legitimate forms of impact. A tiny audience fully justifies a top score.
- 9-10: specific real audience with a real problem/desire; solution demonstrably
  addresses it; credible value, appeal, usefulness, or meaning for that audience.
- 7-8: clear audience and problem, good fit, credible value.
- 5-6: identifiable audience, looser fit or partial address.
- 3-4: speculative audience or strained fit; impact mostly aspirational.
- 1-2: no coherent audience/problem pairing.
Audience size is NOT a multiplier.

## Aggregate & ranking
aggregate = leverage + execution + impact + creativity (4-40). Nothing else enters.
Tie-breaks: Leverage, then Execution, then Impact, then Creativity, then higher
combined evidence confidence, then slug alphabetical. See PROTOCOL.md Ranking section.

Like Comment Story Updates ProcessTwin — Pull Requests for Application State I built ProcessTwin around a simple frustration: workflow editors are easy for a person to understand and awkward for an agent to use reliably. A person can see a canvas and drag a connection. An agent asked to do the same thing through pixels has to guess at coordinates, handles, and inspector fields—and a mistake can look correct while changing the wrong process. ProcessTwin does not put a chatbot inside a diagram editor. Instead, the page gives an external agent a small, explicit set of WebMCP capabilities. The human edits a process on the canvas; the agent reads and changes the same process state through those capabilities. Why this is a good fit for WebMCP The useful action here is not “click near this connector.” It is “send this decision’s false branch to Manager Approval and apply this condition.” WebMCP lets ProcessTwin expose that semantic operation directly. In the demo, the agent builds a workflow in three domain-level calls instead of reproducing a long sequence of drags and clicks. What gets better Agents receive structured process graphs, validation results, simulation percentiles, bottlenecks, and suggested fixes instead of screenshots. The app also shows the evidence: a telemetry panel tracks tool calls, payload size, and estimated UI work avoided. That makes the benefit visible while the demo runs. What people and agents can do together The agent can tell what the human changed. ProcessTwin versions process state and exposes changes, including actor attribution and before/after values. The agent can try an idea without changing the live process. It forks a private scenario, makes changes, runs the simulation, and brings back a before/after diff. I deliberately left out an agent merge tool: the human reviews the proposal and clicks Apply. The human can also set boundaries that the agent cannot ignore. For example, a policy can require high-value refunds to pass through a human approval step. An agent mutation that breaks the rule is rejected with a structured explanation, so it can adapt rather than silently doing the wrong thing. How it works ProcessTwin registers 13 core tools with document.model Context registerTool and adds analysis or scenario tools only when the current state makes them useful. The important implementation choice is that there is only one mutation path. Canvas actions and WebMCP calls both go through the same command layer, which handles validation, policy checks, undo history, activity logging, and state-version updates. The human and agent therefore cannot end up with competing versions of the process. Built with Next.js, TypeScript, React Flow, Zustand, Zod, dagre, and Vitest. No backend. MIT licensed. Like Comment Story Updates Inspiration Inbound businesses do not always lose revenue because they lack leads. They lose it when a genuine enquiry gets buried, a follow-up is missed, or nobody realizes that a high-intent customer is still waiting. Most sales software focuses on acquiring, organizing, or generating leads. We wanted to explore a different question: What revenue has the business already paid to acquire — but quietly failed to recover? EnquiryOS is our answer: Revenue Incident Response for the Agentic Web. A Revenue Incident is a point where observed customer intent and the business response diverge in a way that may have caused recoverable loss. The product is deliberately not a generic CRM, chatbot, or autonomous sales agent. It is an evidence-backed recovery layer that lets a browser agent investigate what was missed while keeping consequential action under explicit human control. What it does EnquiryOS exposes five semantic WebMCP tools directly from the web application: audit_missed_enquiries inspect_enquiry_evidence rank_recovery_queue draft_recovery_message prepare_recovery Together they form a complete recovery workflow: Audit → Inspect evidence → Rank → Draft → Stage → Human commits An agent can ask EnquiryOS what the business may have missed, inspect the evidence behind a Revenue Incident, understand why one incident ranks above another, produce an evidence-grounded recovery draft, and stage it for approval. There is deliberately no agent-callable send tool . The authority boundary is: Observe → Investigate → Reason → Propose → Stage → Human commits This means the browser agent can do the investigative and preparatory work, but the final consequential action remains with the person operating the business. Why WebMCP matters Without WebMCP, an agent interacting with a web application generally has to infer intent from visual structure and operate through brittle UI actions. EnquiryOS instead exposes explicit semantic capabilities through document.modelContext . The agent does not need to guess that a particular button means "find missed enquiries" or scrape the page to understand the recovery queue. It can invoke a purpose-built tool such as audit_missed_enquiries , receive structured evidence, and immediately see the corresponding state reflected in the application's visible interface. The tool calls and the human interface share the same application state. Every WebMCP action also appears in a visible Live Trace, so the human can see what the agent has done. That creates a much stronger human-agent interaction model: the agent investigates, EnquiryOS provides the evidence, and the human retains authority. The demo The demo follows one Revenue Incident involving Ananya Sharma. Her incoming message asks about Saturday availability and package options. EnquiryOS identifies that there was no subsequent business reply and that the wait exceeded the service target. The incident is marked confirmed , with 97% evidence confidence , and receives a deterministic recovery score of 96/100 . Importantly, the score is not presented as an unexplained AI judgment. EnquiryOS shows the evidence signals that produced the priority: high-value enquiry; strong evidence confidence; an explicit availability question; no outbound reply; service-target breach. The agent can then generate a recovery draft grounded in that inspected evidence. Finally, prepare_recovery moves the incident into Awaiting approval . At that point the agent stops. The human sees the checkpoint and retains the only action capable of committing the recovery. How we built it EnquiryOS is a focused Next.js and React application written in TypeScript. The WebMCP integration uses native document.modelContext.registerTool registrations with structured input schemas, tool descriptions, and explicit behavioral boundaries. The five tools operate on a deterministic synthetic enquiry dataset so the judging experience is reproducible and the evidence chain can be inspected consistently. The interface contains three synchronized views: a Revenue Incident recovery queue; an evidence and recovery workspace; a Live Trace of agent tool activity. The recovery score is deterministic rather than generated by an opaque model. Drafts are also constrained by the evidence associated with the selected incident. We intentionally did not embed a proprietary LLM inside EnquiryOS for this prototype. The reasoning agent comes from the browser/user environment; EnquiryOS provides the domain-specific tools, evidence and shared state that make that agent useful. Challenges we faced One of the most interesting challenges was making the boundary between agent capability and human authority part of the product architecture rather than merely a warning in the interface. It would have been easy to add another WebMCP tool called send_message . We deliberately did not. Instead, the most powerful agent-callable operation is prepare_recovery , which may stage a draft but cannot commit it. We also had to make the WebMCP lifecycle robust inside React, ensure the five registrations remained stable across component lifecycle behavior, keep server/client rendering deterministic, and ensure that state-mutating tool responses returned the newly prepared state rather than stale pre-mutation data. The result is a deterministic WebMCP workflow that remains visible and understandable to the human throughout the interaction. What we learned The most important lesson was that agentic interfaces become more useful when websites expose domain intent , not just clickable surfaces. A tool called inspect_enquiry_evidence carries far more meaning than an instruction to click a card, scroll down, and read several DOM nodes. We also learned that agent safety does not necessarily mean reducing capability. EnquiryOS gives the agent significant investigative power while making its authority ceiling explicit. That separation — powerful investigation, evidence-bound reasoning, and human-controlled commitment — is the model we would like to carry forward as agent-enabled websites become more common. What's next The prototype currently uses deterministic synthetic enquiry data. The next step is connecting the same WebMCP recovery layer to real business systems such as inboxes, CRMs, WhatsApp-style enquiry channels, web forms, and booking systems. From there, EnquiryOS can learn organization-specific recovery policies, measure whether staged recoveries actually convert, and identify recurring leakage patterns across channels. The larger vision is simple: Find revenue the business may have missed. Prove why it was missed. Recover what still matters. And let the agent do the investigation without taking authority away from the human. Inspiration Watch an AI agent use a website today and it is doing an impression of a person. It screenshots the page, guesses which pixels are a button, clicks, and hopes nothing moved. We built a GUI-agent demo ourselves and the lesson was blunt: the agent was not slow because the model was weak. It was slow because the app refused to tell it anything. Every capability the app already had — typed, validated, permission-checked on the server — was hidden behind a rendering of itself. WebMCP inverts that. The page stops being a picture to be interpreted and starts being a host that hands the agent real tools. Once we saw that, the question stopped being "how do we automate the browser better" and became "what would a fullstack framework look like if agents were a first-class audience from line one?" That framework is Janux. What it does Janux is a fullstack framework for the Agentic Web. You write your app once; humans get a UI and agents get tools, from the same definitions. An action you already wrote for a form is published three ways with no extra code: as the UI handler, as a WebMCP tool inside the browser session, and as an MCP tool over the wire for out-of-page clients. Same types, same auth, same permission checks. The framework also ships the rest of the stack the Agentic Web needs — llms.txt so an agent can discover what your site does, and Web Bot Auth so it can prove who it is — plus the safety layer that opening this surface demands. And you do not have to bet your stack on it: Janux runs the React ecosystem through a compatibility layer, so it can be adopted page by page. How we built it The framework is Bun-native. The agent surface is not a plugin bolted on top; it is derived from one place. A single server-side manifest describes every tool, its schema, its guard level and whether it can touch untrusted content — the browser bridge, the MCP endpoint and the security pipeline all read from that one description rather than each maintaining its own idea of what exists. Every tool declares a guard. auto runs on the agent's word. confirm produces a signed proposal a human has to approve. Tools can also declare effect: 'irreversible' , which changes how the pipeline treats them under pressure. Our hardest rule was a process one: zero workarounds in the example app . Every time building a demo required a hack, the hack was banned and the friction became a framework change with a test written first. That is where most of the real API came from — event-based tool targeting, keeping runtime nodes alive across SPA navigation, resolving highlight targets for DOM the tool itself creates, disambiguating controls that share an intent. Challenges we ran into Exposing your app's real capabilities to an untrusted caller is a security project wearing a DX project's clothes. Four things we did not see coming: The worst hole was a GET. Our API handler never looked at the HTTP method — it parsed the body and ran the tool. So GET /_janux/api/payments.transfer executed with the defaults from the input schema . That is an <img src> tag on any website in the world, no JavaScript, no CORS preflight, nothing. Invocation endpoints now return 405 for read-only verbs: they have no reads to offer. Sec-Fetch-Site: same-site is not a defense. Measured in real Chrome: two different ports on localhost are same-site. Trusting that header would have left the actual attack open, and in production it is the shape of a subdomain takeover. It falls through to an origin comparison like cross-site does. Computing a preview can be the side effect. Our proposal flow shadow-runs an intent to show the human a diff. When we made tainted irreversible tools downgrade from auto to confirm , we routed exactly the most dangerous operations through the path that runs their body before anyone approves. The shadow only shadows state — an outbound call still really happens. Caught reading our own diff, not by a test. Machine output was wearing the human's role. For compatibility with OpenAI-shaped APIs, tool results came back into the conversation as role: 'user' . Our injection guard was faithfully classifying scraped third-party content as "the last thing the human said." Untrusted content is now fenced with a per-call random nonce — without the nonce, a payload can simply close the fence itself and continue as trusted prose. What we learned Green tests were not enough for any of this. What convinced us was mutation: delete a rule, and watch the exact rows of the attack corpus that rule exists for turn red. If nothing goes red, the rule was decoration. The deeper lesson is that WebMCP is not a transport problem, it is a trust-boundary problem. Handing an agent a tool is the easy half. The hard half is that your app now has a caller that can be persuaded by a web page it read a second ago — and the framework, not each app author, should be the one holding that line. ABOUT TEXT (untrusted evidence):
Your AI agent builds the lesson as a playable game, not a quiz. Lantern uses WebMCP to turn learner actions into visible evidence of understanding. Like Comment Story Updates Inspiration Most learning software is fixed before the learner opens it. Teachers, parents, and trainers must search through existing courses and accept content that may not fit the learner, skill, context, or preferred way of learning. AI can generate explanations and quizzes, but learning should involve more than reading generated text or selecting answers. We wanted learners to explore, experiment, make mistakes, revise their thinking, and demonstrate understanding through play. That idea became Lantern : a WebMCP-powered canvas where browser agents turn natural-language learning goals into playable games. What it does A user describes the learner, skill, and desired world in ordinary language. For example: Create an ecosystem game for a six-year-old where the learner restores a pond, meadow, and woodland by experimenting with sunlight, water, food, and shelter. The user gives this brief to a WebMCP-compatible browser agent. The agent then: Inspects Lantern’s capabilities and safety limits. Creates or resumes a game draft. Writes the complete HTML, CSS, and JavaScript. Opens the game in a safe playtest. Checks validation errors, runtime diagnostics, and learning evidence. Improves the game and requests human review. The result is a functioning browser game rather than a description, code snippet, or dressed-up quiz. While the learner plays, Lantern records evidence such as predictions, attempts, observations, corrections, hint usage, and demonstrated mastery. Agent-created games receive dedicated pages and remain saved in the browser. How we built it Lantern was built through close human–agent collaboration. I provided the product direction, learning use cases, design feedback, safety requirements, and decisions about the final experience. OpenAI Codex handled most of the implementation —building the interface, defining the WebMCP tools, creating the isolated game runtime, implementing browser persistence and evidence reporting, debugging interactions, testing the application, removing legacy code, and preparing the project for submission. The resulting application uses Vinext, React, TypeScript, WebMCP, sandboxed srcDoc iframes, Canvas 2D/WebGL, and browser local storage . Lantern registers eight tools through document.modelContext.registerTool() : get_game_canvas_capabilities list_saved_game_drafts resume_game_draft create_game_draft set_game_source preview_game get_game_runtime_diagnostics request_game_review These tools allow an agent to inspect the canvas, create or resume drafts, write game source, open playtests, diagnose problems, and request human review. Generated games communicate with Lantern through two small host APIs: lantern.evidence() reports predictions, experiments, attempts, observations, corrections, and other learning signals. lantern.complete() reports mastery and triggers host-controlled completion feedback. This development process reflected Lantern’s central idea: a human supplies the intent and judgment, while an agent handles the technical work required to turn that intent into a functioning product. Challenges we ran into The biggest challenge was giving agents enough creative freedom to build ambitious games without giving generated code unrestricted access to the browser. Games run inside isolated iframes with a strict Content Security Policy. External scripts, network requests, storage APIs, nested frames, and hidden navigation are blocked. Lantern also validates generated source and rejects unsafe APIs, oversized payloads, dynamic evaluation, and obvious unbounded loops. Another challenge was designing tools that agents could use reliably. A single “generate game” action would hide too much state and make failures difficult to diagnose. We created a structured workflow with explicit capabilities, drafts, revisions, previews, diagnostics, persistence, and review. We also had to make gameplay educationally meaningful. A score alone reveals very little about understanding, so Lantern captures how a learner predicts, experiments, uses hints, corrects mistakes, and revises their thinking. Accomplishments that we're proud of We are proud that Lantern produces real interactive games rather than descriptions or generated quizzes. A browser agent can discover Lantern’s tools, create a structured project, program the complete game, inspect errors, revise its work, and open the playable result through WebMCP. We also built: Persistent browser-local game drafts Dedicated pages for agent-created games Safe, isolated game execution Source and runtime validation Revision and idempotency protection Human review boundaries Learning-evidence reporting Host-controlled mastery celebrations Five example games covering mathematics, literacy, spatial reasoning, ecosystems, and computational thinking Most importantly, Lantern demonstrates a complete human–agent workflow: the human describes the desired outcome, the agent builds it, and the resulting experience can be played and evaluated immediately. What we learned WebMCP is most powerful when a website exposes meaningful product capabilities instead of adding a chatbot beside fixed content. Agents perform better when they can inspect constraints before acting, observe the consequences of their changes, diagnose failures, and resume from structured state. We also learned that browser-local persistence can support surprisingly capable agent workflows without requiring a backend service. Most importantly, generative education becomes much more compelling when the output is something learners can manipulate—not simply something they consume. What's next for Lantern We plan to add reusable 3D components, richer physics and shader tools, teacher-defined assessment rubrics, accessibility presets, collaborative learning worlds, and portable game packages. We also want agents to adapt existing games from teacher feedback and learner evidence while keeping educators in control of review and publishing. Our long-term goal is to let anyone describe what someone needs to learn and collaborate with an agent to build a safe, memorable world for learning it.
