=== RUBRIC (frozen) ===
# FROZEN SCORING RUBRIC (pre-registered before any Stage 1 review)

Scores are integers 1-10 on each of the FOUR official criteria. Every score carries:
rationale, evidence_surfaces, confidence (0-1). Reviewers use the full scale and these
band anchors verbatim. Category neutrality is absolute: a game, art piece, music tool,
CRM, weird toy, and developer tool are all eligible for 10/10 on every criterion if they
satisfy that criterion exceptionally well FOR WHAT THEY ARE TRYING TO BE.
No aesthetic penalties. Signs of hurried AI work matter only as actual product-quality
problems (incoherence, broken flow, placeholder content), never as style.

## WebMCP Leverage — what does WebMCP change for this product?
Judge the delta WebMCP makes to reliability, precision, structured state access,
shared human-agent state, repeatability, and capability. Tool COUNT is not leverage.
- 9-10 TRANSFORMATIVE: the intended experience is impractical without the structured
  agent surface; human and agent share live product state; the collaboration could not
  be replicated comparably well by a general agent driving the UI.
- 7-8 MAJOR DELTA: without WebMCP a general agent is markedly less reliable/precise
  here; the agent is central to the product, not a bolt-on.
- 5-6 MEANINGFUL DELTA: existing capability packaged into a genuinely better product
  experience via structured tools; same outcome otherwise possible with real friction.
- 3-4 MINOR DELTA: generic browser automation achieves comparable outcomes; WebMCP is
  a convenience layer.
- 1-2 COSMETIC: exposes an action a UI button already offers; no meaningful change.

## Execution — is the actual product coherent, intentional, complete for its scope?
Stage 1 scores EXECUTION_CLAIMED: coherence/intentionality/completeness as evidenced by
the packet (description + packaging + video frames/transcript). Thin evidence caps the
score (end-to-end credit requires end-to-end evidence) and lowers confidence.
Stage 2 may rescore as EXECUTION_OBSERVED; latest evidence-backed score replaces, never
sums.
- 9-10: all surfaces agree; central workflow demonstrably completes; states handled;
  feels finished for its stated scope.
- 7-8: strong coherence and intentionality; central workflow appears functional; minor
  unfinished edges.
- 5-6: plausibly real with visible gaps; scope unclear or core not demonstrated
  end-to-end.
- 3-4: thin prototype; contradictions between surfaces; placeholder content.
- 1-2: non-functional or incoherent for its claimed scope.

## Creativity & Ambition — novelty of concept and interaction model
Judge ONLY: novelty of the concept, novelty of the interaction model, originality
relative to existing concepts, ambition, memorable/surprising use of the medium, and
the depth with which the concept is pursued. WebMCP indispensability belongs under
WebMCP Leverage, NOT here: a project can be extremely creative even if another agent
API could theoretically have enabled something similar.
- 9-10: genuinely novel concept or interaction model; ambitious scope pursued deeply
  and coherently; memorable.
- 7-8: fresh synthesis or notably original interaction; ambitious for a hackathon.
- 5-6: competent take on a known category with a real new angle.
- 3-4: derivative; novelty mostly surface details.
- 1-2: clone/template with trivial changes.
"That the category exists" is never a penalty; judge the submitted concept.

## Potential Impact — credible value for the intended audience
Judge: is there a real audience; is there a real problem, desire, or use case for that
audience; does the demonstrated solution genuinely address it. NO commercial adoption,
monetization, market pull, or startup viability is required. Entertainment, play, art,
creative expression, learning, niche hobby use, experimentation, and community value
are legitimate forms of impact. A tiny audience fully justifies a top score.
- 9-10: specific real audience with a real problem/desire; solution demonstrably
  addresses it; credible value, appeal, usefulness, or meaning for that audience.
- 7-8: clear audience and problem, good fit, credible value.
- 5-6: identifiable audience, looser fit or partial address.
- 3-4: speculative audience or strained fit; impact mostly aspirational.
- 1-2: no coherent audience/problem pairing.
Audience size is NOT a multiplier.

## Aggregate & ranking
aggregate = leverage + execution + impact + creativity (4-40). Nothing else enters.
Tie-breaks: Leverage, then Execution, then Impact, then Creativity, then higher
combined evidence confidence, then slug alphabetical. See PROTOCOL.md Ranking section.

A WebMCP design studio where agent actions arrive as editable ChangeSets you can preview, amend and undo before the canvas changes. Like Comment Story Updates How it works—agent intent→one editable ChangeSet→human negotiates→receipt & history. Atelier is the product Redini is the transaction layer Inspiration Most AI agent demos mutate your app silently. You ask for a change, it happens, you hope it was right. Approval dialogs are binary — yes or no — while real design work is never binary. I wanted the opposite: what if every agent action arrived as a set of related, negotiable changes you can actually see together? A title, a background, a logo position — proposed at once, previewed as one coherent poster, edited one by one. That question became Atelier, powered by Redini . ## What it does Atelier is a WebMCP design editor where agent actions arrive as editable ChangeSets instead of silent mutations. ONE agent intent → ONE WebMCP call (design_update) → ONE ChangeSet → derived preview → amend / skip → atomic commit → receipt → Undo ↔ Redo One call can propose 3 or 6 operations at once. Before anything touches the live canvas you can preview the full result, amend any parameter with typed controls, skip any operation, and commit only the accepted subset. The receipt preserves the distance between what the agent intended and what you authorized: INTENDED / AMENDED BY YOU / SKIPPED BY YOU / APPLIED — STATE v0 → v2 Redini is the small, app-agnostic transaction layer underneath. Every commit, undo and redo advances the version — \( v_{n+1} > v_n \) — so a proposal that was staged on an old state becomes stale instead of being silently applied elsewhere. Live: https://redini-atelier.netlify.app — Try ?clean=1 for the focused agent path. ## How we built it Stack: TypeScript + Vite, native DOM/CSS, Netlify static hosting. No framework, no snapshots — forward operations + compensating inverses only. WebMCP: We use the native surface — document.modelContext.registerTool() , getTools() , executeTool(tool, JSON.stringify(args)) . The public app exposes 5 focused tools: design_update , list_templates , get_current_design , filter_templates , get_vendor_content . design_update stays pending while you negotiate and resolves with a concise result like {"status":"committed","appliedCount":2} . Undo/Redo are intentionally human-side only. Verified like a product: 104 tests, tsc --noEmit clean, production builds, and a real Chrome 149+ E2E that drives the true executeTool path — including PREVIEW — not applied yet , amend, skip, Commit N changes , receipt, Undo, Redo, keyboard ⌘Z / ⇧⌘Z , and stale proposals. Deployed with explicit Origin-Agent-Cluster: ?1 and Permissions-Policy: tools=(self) so the experimental browser gate is unambiguous. ## Challenges we ran into WebMCP is still experimental. executeTool expects the tool object + JSON string , Chrome needs --enable-features=WebMCP , and documents must be origin-keyed or registerTool must reject with SecurityError . We found all three with a real Puppeteer spike on Chrome 149. Product challenges were harder: Preview without double-render: simulate() already gave the right derived state, but we rendered it as a translucent overlay on top of the committed poster. Fix: hide the committed canvas while previewing. Honest atomicity: if runtime.apply() mutates then throws without returning an inverse, the world may be partially changed. Redini never claims “nothing happened” in that case — it surfaces stateUncertain instead. Derived bounds: move {x:640,y:400} passes a per-field check but puts the logo fully outside the 640×400 canvas. Bounds are now validated on the sequential derived logo state and re-validated at commit. ## Accomplishments that we're proud of A complete, demo-ready loop that holds together under verification: agent proposes 6 changes in one call → you amend one, skip one, commit 2 of 6 → receipt → Undo → Redo → provenance shows Agent proposed vs You staged Template "..." , all with 0 console errors . We are proud of the small things judges can falsify: the badge names the proposal that owns the preview, Commit is disabled at 0 ops, templates are real <button> s, and the 104-test suite includes the exact repros from our external audits. And it works inside the real ChatGPT in-app browser — our final smoke was there, on the Netlify URL, with frame-ancestors allowlisted for it. ## What we learned A transaction layer does not need snapshots. Set-semantics + idempotent inverses are enough, and the hardest part is not the algorithm but making the UI tell the truth. We also learned to keep the agent surface small — 5 tools and one negotiable ChangeSet are more legible than a toolbox of approvals. Built solo for my first hackathon, it was also a crash course in reading specs before building. ## What's next for Redini-Atelier Redini stays a small, reusable guard. Next we want to make it a tiny npm module with a cleaner validate(op, priorOps?) contract, add opt-in persistence for history, and try the same pattern on other domains — dashboards, forms, planning tools — where one agent intent naturally touches several related fields. Code is MIT at https://github.com/cioffiAI/redini-atelier — v1.0.0-challenge is frozen, main stays open. Like Comment Story Updates Labled Layers Packed, labeled Empty Closed Duffel bag Backpack Inspiration I always procrastinate packing until the very last minute, then struggle to find what belongs where even if I already have a packing list in mind. After I guess where everything should go and then rearrange my suitcase a dozen times, I finally come to find that it doesn't fit. With AI becoming good at understanding what we need through context and chatlogs, I wanted to create a tool for ChatGPT to express a simple request into something physical and visual that we could actually use. What it does Paqly turns any packing list into a 3D packing plan that you can breakdown into clearly labeled layers. Instead of organizing and planning what to put and where, you can simply tell ChatGPT what you want to bring or even just ask ChatGPT to create a list for you. ChatGPT would then communicate with Paqly directly through WebMCP and create a 3D visual based on the specified suitcase and items. With Paqly, you are able to see where each item should go, which layer it belongs in, the order in which each item should be packed, how much of the suitcase is filled and whether the packing list actually fits. How I built it The core of Paqly is a WebMCP integration that exposes packing functionality directly to AI agents. Rather than requiring an agent to navigate the website like a human and click through forms, Paqly exposes structured tools that allow ChatGPT to send packing information directly to the application. From there, the packing system takes the item dimensions and suitcase constraints, determines an arrangement, groups the result into packing layers, validates the placement, and passes that result into the interactive 3D visualisation. A lot of the work also went into making the output understandable. A mathematically valid arrangement is not very useful if a person cannot tell what they are looking at, so the 3D view includes item labels, separated layers, a re-playable packing order and an interactive suitcase view. Challenges I ran into The hardest part was making the packing plan physically believable rather than just visually impressive. Early versions could technically place objects inside the scene while still producing obviously bad results: items could overlap, float, extend outside the suitcase or leave large amounts of wasted space. This meant I had to treat validation separately from visual rendering and continuously check that the generated arrangement actually made sense. Another challenge was the WebMCP integration itself. I wanted the AI to interact with Paqly through structured WebMCP tools rather than simply using browser automation to click around the interface. Finally, 3D packing is much harder than displaying a normal list. Different objects have different dimensions, orientations and levels of compressibility, while the final result still needs to be simple enough for someone to reproduce with a real suitcase. Accomplishments that I'm proud of I'm most proud that Paqly goes beyond giving packing advice. You can describe what you are trying to pack like thinking aloud and get back an actual spatial plan showing where everything should go, and the combination of AI, WebMCP and interactive 3D makes the result easier to obtain and follow. I'm also proud of how much the physical packing and visualisation improved throughout development. What started as basic object placement became a layered system with packing order, fit validation and a much clearer 3D representation of the suitcase. What I learned The biggest thing I learned is that giving AI tools is much more interesting than simply putting an AI chatbot inside a website. WebMCP lets the AI interact with the actual functionality of Paqly. The AI handles the vibes, while Paqly handles the specialised packing logic and visualisation. I also learned how quickly a 3D system can look correct while being physically wrong. Building this forced me to think much more carefully about constraints, validation and the difference between generating something that looks good and generating something that actually works. What's next for Paqly The next step is making Paqly understand more about the real world. I want it to support a much larger library of real items, better modelling of soft and compressible objects, different suitcase types and more advanced physical optimisation to better simulate a real packing environment. I want it to be able to mathematically find not just the optimal placement for each item, but the optimal fold, the optimal angle, and other features that make a paq perfect. A user-created future floor plan, edited through WebMCP. Export the current floor plan exactly as it appears. Inspect, propose, validate, and apply floor-plan changes. Inspiration When I first used a computer as a child, there was something I wanted even more than a game: software that would let me design my own floor plans. I dreamed of moving walls, expanding rooms, and placing devices that did not yet exist—as naturally as drawing ideas on paper. Today, many floor-planning tools are available. However, I wondered whether designing a home could become more accessible and imaginative if people could simply describe what they wanted instead of learning specialized controls. That idea inspired me to build HABITAT//NULL, a future floor-plan simulator powered by WebMCP. What It Does HABITAT//NULL is a floor-plan simulator where users and AI collaboratively imagine future living spaces. Users can describe changes in natural language, such as: “Make the kitchen larger.” “Split this room at a 60/40 ratio.” “Turn this area into a parking space.” Through WebMCP, the AI can inspect the current floor plan, propose a change, validate it, and apply it to the design. The project is not limited to conventional furniture. It is also designed to let users invent and place their own futuristic tools, devices, and living systems. Completed floor plans can be saved as PNG or PDF files exactly as they appear on the screen. Rather than simply optimizing existing homes, HABITAT//NULL aims to turn imagined ways of living—things that do not yet exist—into visible spaces. How I Built It The floor plan is managed as structured data representing rooms, walls, openings, furniture, and equipment. The visual floor plan is generated from this underlying data. I created WebMCP tools that allow AI to inspect the current layout, propose modifications, validate the result, and apply an approved proposal. This allows the AI to understand and manipulate the structure of the floor plan instead of relying on screen coordinates or button positions. Changes are first created as proposals rather than being applied immediately. After the proposal has been reviewed, the exact change can be committed to the current floor plan. The visual design combines understated science fiction with the nostalgic feeling of a hand-drawn architectural plan. I wanted the interface to suggest a quiet, deteriorated future where advanced equipment still survives among worn materials and old records. Challenges The most difficult challenge was translating ambiguous natural-language requests into precise floor-plan operations. For example, “Make the kitchen larger” raises several questions: Which wall should move? How much space should be taken from the neighboring room? What should happen to doors, furniture, and connected spaces? Changing a room’s size requires more than updating its dimensions. The relationships among walls, openings, routes, and placed objects must remain consistent. Another challenge was making the entire workflow possible through WebMCP. Creating a proposal was not enough—the AI also needed to inspect, validate, and apply the change without requiring unnecessary manual interaction. It was also important to avoid adding too many unrelated features. I continually returned to the project’s essential experience: letting users reshape a floor plan through conversation. What I Learned This project taught me that WebMCP is more than a way to automate browser interactions. It can become a structured communication layer between an AI agent and a web application. When the AI understands the meaning of rooms, walls, and openings—not just their appearance on the screen—users can express their intentions naturally without learning specialized software controls. I also learned that giving an AI freedom is not enough to create a trustworthy experience. Users need to see proposed changes, verify the results, and clearly understand what will be applied. A proposal-and-approval workflow makes collaboration with AI both safer and easier to understand. What’s Next Next, I want to give users more freedom to create and place their own furniture, machines, and imagined future systems. I would also like to support multi-story buildings and unconventional environments such as fantasy homes, spacecraft, and underground shelters. My goal is to develop HABITAT//NULL into more than a tool for selecting or editing existing floor plans. I want it to become a place where users and AI can invent new ways of living together. Make music together with your AI agent. Start with a melody, then let your agent add instruments, respond to your changes, and shape the song alongside you in a shared live studio. Like 2 Comment Story Updates The studio — full five-instrument arrangement, Musical Contract panel, 14 live tools, and the timeline. Performance mode — the swapped toolset (7 performance tools), energy, live meters, and a timeline of the entire agent build. Contract approval Share Inspiration Most people have a tune in their head that never becomes a song. The melody is the easy part. The hard part is everything around it: drums, bass, chords, and knowing how to put them together. Most AI music tools solve this with a text box: describe a song, get a song. I wanted the musician to stay in charge. You play the idea, you keep the final say, and an AI agent joins as a bandmate, not a ghostwriter. WebMCP makes that possible. Instead of building a chatbot into a music app, Duet lets the agent you already use work inside the same live studio, with real tools and clear limits. What it does Duet is a browser music studio shared by a human and an AI agent. The human starts by drawing a melody on a piano roll. It lives only in that browser tab; nothing is uploaded. Then they hand the rest to their agent with an ordinary request, for example to keep the notes as they are and build a cinematic synthwave arrangement around them. The agent reads the exact live composition through WebMCP, adds drums, bass and atmospheric chords, shapes the groove, and performs the result. Every agent action appears immediately in the interface, is attributed in a visible timeline (HUMAN / AGENT / SYSTEM), and can be heard through the browser. The musician can switch back to Compose mode at any point and change whatever they like, and the agent can respond to those changes. Both work on one composition, and the human keeps the creative direction. Before the agent touches anything, the musician sets a Musical Contract : the melody is locked to the human; the agent may edit drums, bass, pad, keys and the mix; energy is capped; tempo and key can be locked. These terms are live state that every WebMCP tool enforces. If the agent tries to change the locked melody, its tool call pauses and Duet shows the musician the exact proposed change to approve or decline. The agent only proceeds with permission. When the song is ready, it can be shared. Publishing always pauses for the musician to approve the title, then produces a backend-free remix link with the whole composition encoded in the URL. Anyone who opens it can play the song, remix it, and invite their own agent to join the band. Duet contains no embedded AI model and needs no AI API key. The user brings a WebMCP-compatible agent, and WebMCP gives it structured, scoped access to the studio. How I built it Duet is built around one shared musical state used by both the visual interface and its WebMCP tools. Whether the musician edits a note by hand or the agent writes a drum pattern, both go through the same engine, bump the same version, and land in the same timeline. The page exposes a small, typed set of tools: read the session, add and remove instruments, edit each instrument, adjust tempo, key and groove, and publish. The toolset is contextual. Adding a drum machine registers drums_edit ; removing it withdraws the tool. Compose mode exposes composition tools; entering Performance mode withdraws them and registers a separate set: play, stop, energy, track mix, breakdown. The registration adapter targets Chrome's document.modelContext implementation, feature-detects the navigator.modelContext proposal as a fallback, and was verified against Chrome's real WebMCP runtime on the deployed site. Playback is phrase-aware rather than a bare loop: patterns run as a four-bar phrase (main, main, variation, fill), notes carry velocity and small timing offsets, and deterministic humanization and swing keep the groove alive without randomness. The audio engine is built on Tone.js with five synthesized instruments, per-voice filter envelopes, 32 presets, kick-driven ducking and a proper master chain. It reads the shared state on every tick, so an agent edit is audible on the very next step. The key architectural decision was to keep the model outside the product. Duet is the instrument; WebMCP is the collaboration layer that connects it to whichever agent the musician brings. The app is a static React + TypeScript build with no backend, deployed on Vercel. Challenges I ran into The hardest part was designing agent tools that are expressive enough for real musical collaboration without exposing one enormous "make a song" command. Each instrument got a single tool with a small set of enumerated operations, strict validation, and structured responses that tell the agent exactly what changed. Preserving human authorship was a design problem as much as a technical one. The answer became the Musical Contract: constraints the human sets in the interface and the tools enforce, including the pause-and-ask flow for the locked melody. The agent should respond to the musician's ideas, not replace them. Beyond that: keeping manual edits and agent actions perfectly in sync, accurate musical timing in the browser, and making every agent action understandable and reversible. 116 automated tests cover the tool sets, mode transitions, contract enforcement, sharing and undo. Accomplishments that I'm proud of Duet makes human–agent collaboration both visible and audible. Nobody has to imagine what the agent is doing: you watch instruments and tools appear, see every move attributed in the timeline, and hear the arrangement grow. The Musical Contract turns WebMCP's human-in-the-loop story into something a musician actually wants: not a permissions dialog, but a bandmate asking before stepping on your solo. And Duet works without a chatbot or a single model tied to it. The same studio can be played by any WebMCP-compatible agent. WebMCP isn't an add-on here; it's what lets the musician and the agent share one creative environment. Everything you create is remixable and can be passed from person to person, each inviting their own agent to join the band. What I learned WebMCP becomes powerful when a website behaves like a shared instrument rather than a collection of forms. Structured musical operations are far more reliable and expressive than asking an agent to click coordinates or edit raw files, and contextual tools help the agent understand what is possible right now. Making the human's constraints part of the tool contract, rather than hoping the agent behaves, is what made the collaboration feel trustworthy. Human–agent interaction feels right when the result is immediately visible, reversible, and, in Duet's case, audible. What's next for Duet A song mode that arranges the loop into a full two-minute structure (intro, build, drop, breakdown, outro) using the same mute, energy and phrase machinery, so an agent can arrange as well as play. MIDI keyboard input, so the tune in your head goes straight from your fingers to the piano roll. Variation and fill slots for every instrument. And richer real-time improvisation between musician and agent. Like Comment Story Updates Overview Debugging electronic circuits is often a slow and fragmented process, especially when the problem is not immediately obvious or the person working on it does not have deep experience with the specific hardware. Finding the cause can involve jumping between schematics, measurements, datasheets, simulation tools, and external help. AI can assist with the diagnosis, but the usual workflow of sending screenshots, measurements, and explanations back and forth through chat quickly becomes confusing and loses context. empeirik was built to bring that process into one shared workspace. The circuit, measurements, hypotheses, diagnostic steps, and proposed fixes all remain visible in the same environment while the AI works directly with the current state of the problem. It can inspect the circuit, decide which measurements are useful, narrow down likely causes, propose repairs or directly implement them, and verify them in simulation instead of repeatedly reconstructing the situation from a conversation. The project is built on top of CircuitJS1 for circuit rendering and simulation, with an additional diagnostic layer for measurements, hypotheses, repair testing, a shared activity timeline, and WebMCP tools that let the AI interact directly with the workspace. One of the main challenges was turning a traditional circuit simulator into a structured environment that both the user and the AI can operate at the same time while keeping every diagnostic step synchronized and visible. Learning and building electronics made it quickly clear how much context is lost when AI assistance is limited to a separate chat window. Giving the agent direct access to the same tools, state, and visual environment as the user makes the debugging process much more continuous and practical.
