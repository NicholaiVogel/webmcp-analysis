# FROZEN SCORING RUBRIC (pre-registered before any Stage 1 review)

Scores are integers 1-10 on each of the FOUR official criteria. Every score carries:
rationale, evidence_surfaces, confidence (0-1). Reviewers use the full scale and these
band anchors verbatim. Category neutrality is absolute: a game, art piece, music tool,

## WebMCP Leverage — what does WebMCP change for this product?
Judge the delta WebMCP makes to reliability, precision, structured state access,
shared human-agent state, repeatability, and capability. Tool COUNT is not leverage.
- 9-10 TRANSFORMATIVE: the intended experience is impractical without the structured
  agent surface; human and agent share live product state; the collaboration could not
  be replicated comparably well by a general agent driving the UI.
- 7-8 MAJOR DELTA: without WebMCP a general agent is markedly less reliable/precise
  here; the agent is central to the product, not a bolt-on.
- 5-6 MEANINGFUL DELTA: existing capability packaged into a genuinely better product
  experience via structured tools; same outcome otherwise possible with real friction.
- 3-4 MINOR DELTA: generic browser automation achieves comparable outcomes; WebMCP is
  a convenience layer.
- 1-2 COSMETIC: exposes an action a UI button already offers; no meaningful change.

## Execution — is the actual product coherent, intentional, complete for its scope?
Stage 1 scores EXECUTION_CLAIMED: coherence/intentionality/completeness as evidenced by
the packet (description + packaging + video frames/transcript). Thin evidence caps the
score (end-to-end credit requires end-to-end evidence) and lowers confidence.
Stage 2 may rescore as EXECUTION_OBSERVED; latest evidence-backed score replaces, never
sums.
- 9-10: all surfaces agree; central workflow demonstrably completes; states handled;
  feels finished for its stated scope.
- 7-8: strong coherence and intentionality; central workflow appears functional; minor
  unfinished edges.
- 5-6: plausibly real with visible gaps; scope unclear or core not demonstrated
  end-to-end.
- 3-4: thin prototype; contradictions between surfaces; placeholder content.
- 1-2: non-functional or incoherent for its claimed scope.

## Creativity & Ambition — novelty of concept and interaction model
Judge ONLY: novelty of the concept, novelty of the interaction model, originality
relative to existing concepts, ambition, memorable/surprising use of the medium, and
the depth with which the concept is pursued. WebMCP indispensability belongs under
WebMCP Leverage, NOT here: a project can be extremely creative even if another agent
API could theoretically have enabled something similar.
- 9-10: genuinely novel concept or interaction model; ambitious scope pursued deeply
  and coherently; memorable.
- 7-8: fresh synthesis or notably original interaction; ambitious for a hackathon.
- 5-6: competent take on a known category with a real new angle.
- 3-4: derivative; novelty mostly surface details.
- 1-2: clone/template with trivial changes.
"That the category exists" is never a penalty; judge the submitted concept.

## Potential Impact — credible value for the intended audience
Judge: is there a real audience; is there a real problem, desire, or use case for that
audience; does the demonstrated solution genuinely address it. NO commercial adoption,
monetization, market pull, or startup viability is required. Entertainment, play, art,
creative expression, learning, niche hobby use, experimentation, and community value
are legitimate forms of impact. A tiny audience fully justifies a top score.
- 9-10: specific real audience with a real problem/desire; solution demonstrably

- 3-4: speculative audience or strained fit; impact mostly aspirational.
- 1-2: no coherent audience/problem pairing.
Audience size is NOT a multiplier.

Like Comment Story Updates PACT PACT is a transaction and authority protocol for AI agents performing consequential actions inside web applications. Most agent systems give an AI broad access to application tools and rely on instructions such as “only change these resources” or “do not touch billing.” PACT treats that model as insufficient for high-impact operations. Instead, PACT turns every consequential task into an explicitly scoped transaction. The workflow is: Intent → semantic preview → exact-plan approval → scoped authority → commit → canonical verification → receipt. Before any mutation occurs, PACT determines the exact effects required by the task. The human sees precisely what will change and what will not change. Approval is bound to the specific plan, its hash, and the canonical state on which it was created. If the underlying state changes after approval, the plan becomes stale and commit authority disappears. The agent must re-plan and obtain approval for the new state instead of silently executing an outdated operation. PACT includes protections for stale state, duplicate commits, idempotency, version conflicts, approval expiry, negative invariants, post-condition verification, audit integrity, rollback conditions, and transaction recovery. Its key WebMCP contribution is that agent capability is not equivalent to permanent administrative access. Mutation capabilities can appear only after an exact transaction has been approved and disappear when the authorization is no longer valid. The reference implementation demonstrates this through operations such as contractor offboarding, ownership transfer, access revocation, and protected resources such as billing or deletion. The broader thesis is that agents should not receive permanent authority. They should receive narrowly scoped, temporary, verifiable authority for a specific approved transaction. I wanted to find a use case where an agent wasn't simply pressing buttons faster than a human, but was actually helping with the reasoning between those actions. Lost and found felt like a surprisingly natural fit. When we lose something, we rarely remember perfect database fields. We remember fragments. “I lost something on a key ring.” Then, after thinking about it: “Wait, it had a small bear charm.” That became Agent Lost & Found : an experiment in what happens when a human and a browser agent investigate the same problem together. What I built Agent Lost & Found is a lost-and-found catalog with WebMCP built directly into the website. People can still browse the catalog like a normal website. But a WebMCP-enabled browser agent doesn't have to interpret search boxes, filters, buttons, and item cards to understand what the site can do. Instead, the website exposes seven structured investigation tools: search_lost_items get_item_details get_search_facets compare_items get_match_evidence get_active_case request_claim Together, these let an agent search the catalog, inspect candidates, compare possible matches, analyze evidence, find useful follow-up clues, resume an existing investigation, and eventually ask the human to review a possible match. -- From a vague memory to a specific match The demo begins with an intentionally incomplete clue: “I lost something on a key ring.” There isn't enough information to immediately know which item is correct. And I didn't want the agent to pretend otherwise. Instead, it can search the catalog, inspect the remaining candidates, and use the available information to narrow the investigation. Then the human remembers one small detail: “It had a small bear charm.” That clue doesn't start a new search. It becomes part of the existing case. The candidates are evaluated again, and LF-017, a bear keychain, moves toward the top of the investigation because the new clue provides much stronger evidence for it. This simple interaction became the core of the project for me. The agent doesn't need the human to remember everything at once. The human doesn't need to manually restart the search every time another detail comes back. They build the investigation together. Making the ranking explain itself Once I had progressive clues working, another problem appeared. If LF-017 moves up after I mention the bear charm, the interface shouldn't just show me a different ranking. It should be able to explain what changed. So each investigation step stores score snapshots and evidence breakdowns. When the evidence changes, Agent Lost & Found can compare the new state with the previous one and show Candidate Movement . A candidate may move up because a new clue matches. Another may move down because the new information contradicts it. Evidence is separated into three states: Matched Unknown Contradiction That distinction became more important than I expected. If the catalog doesn't contain information about a particular feature, that doesn't automatically mean the item contradicts the human's memory. Sometimes the correct answer is simply: We don't know yet. For the same reason, the number shown by the application is called a Match Score . It is a deterministic matching score based on the available evidence — not an AI confidence probability. I wanted the user to be able to inspect why an item ranked highly instead of being asked to trust an unexplained number. The Casefile Once the investigation became multi-step, I ran into another very ordinary problem: I refreshed the page. Everything disappeared. That didn't feel right. If a human and an agent are investigating something together, the investigation itself should exist independently of a single page session. So I introduced a versioned Casefile . It stores the active investigation, including the original description, clues, candidate state, score history, ranking history, timeline, strongest match, and claim-review state. After a refresh, the application validates and restores the Casefile. The agent can then call: get_active_case and continue from the same investigation. One small detail mattered to me here: restoring state should not mean inventing history. The application restores the Casefile, but it does not replay fake tool calls or create Agent Activity for actions that never happened. The investigation continues from real state. Why WebMCP? I understood WebMCP differently after building this project. At the beginning, I mostly thought: “Websites can expose actions as tools.” That's useful, but it turned out not to be the most interesting part. For me, the more interesting idea became shared state between the website, the human, and the agent . Without WebMCP, an agent interacting with a lost-and-found website would need to interpret an interface built for humans: locate the search field, understand the filters, inspect cards, and infer what changed after each interaction. With WebMCP, the website can expose its actual capabilities as structured tools while keeping the normal visual interface for people. More importantly, both operate on the same underlying investigation. The human interface and WebMCP tools share the same search, matching, evidence, ranking, and Casefile logic. When the agent investigates something, the human can see the result. When the human adds or corrects a clue, the next agent action sees that updated state. That was the point where the project stopped feeling like: “AI controlling a website.” It started feeling more like: a human and an agent using the same product together. The tool I deliberately didn't build There was one tool I deliberately left out. If the agent finds a strong match — for example, LF-017 after the bear-charm clue — should it be allowed to claim the item automatically? Technically, I could expose: confirm_claim I decided not to. The agent can call: request_claim which opens the claim review interface for the human. But the final confirmation still requires a human action. This isn't a technical limitation. It's a product decision. Searching through candidates is useful work to give an agent. Comparing evidence is useful. Finding the next clue worth asking about is useful. But deciding that a real item belongs to someone has consequences. Building this made me realize that not every action that can become an agent tool necessarily should become one. That eventually became the principle behind the project: Agents investigate. Humans decide. How I built it Agent Lost & Found is built with React, TypeScript, Vite, and WebMCP , and deployed on Cloudflare Pages . For this hackathon version, the application uses a deterministic catalog of 30 lost items backed by structured metadata. The catalog is intentionally small. I wanted the investigation to be reproducible and inspectable. A judge should be able to understand why LF-017 appeared rather than having the result hidden behind a huge dataset or an opaque model. The human interface and WebMCP layer share the same underlying engines for: search and normalization matching and ranking evidence classification clue handling score snapshots candidate movement Casefile persistence I also built a deterministic demo/presenter mode for evaluation.

Inspiration Companion Sketch started with a simple question: what would a game look like if an AI was not just generating content for a human, but was actually another player? Most AI games treat AI as a tool, narrator, or content generator. We wanted to experiment with something more direct: a shared game where a human and ChatGPT could draw, guess, chat, and participate in the same gameplay loop. Drawing games felt like the perfect test because they depend on creativity, interpretation, imperfect communication, and humor. Those interactions become much more interesting when humans and AI share the same canvas. What it does Companion Sketch is a real-time multiplayer drawing and guessing game for humans and AI agents. A human creates or joins a room, then ChatGPT can join the same visible game through native WebMCP Website Tools. Players take turns choosing secret prompts, drawing them, and guessing what other players are creating. The current version includes: Real-time drawing and game synchronization through Socket.IO Shared multiplayer rooms with clear, copyable room codes Human and AI players participating as equal game participants Three private candidate words for every drawing turn Native WebMCP Website Tools for ChatGPT gameplay A REST API fallback for other agents and testing Touch-friendly mobile drawing with pointer support A compact drawing toolbar with expandable tool, brush-size, and color palettes A mobile-first dark glass-style interface Background and reload recovery that preserves the current room, round, timer, score, chat, and canvas Graceful fallback when WebMCP is unavailable The result is a social game where the interesting part is not simply “AI-generated art,” but what happens when humans and AI try to understand each other’s drawings. How ChatGPT plays through WebMCP The page registers eight top-level imperative Website Tools using document.modelContext.registerTool: companion_sketch_status — Read the current viewer-safe game state companion_sketch_join — Join the visible room as the ChatGPT companion companion_sketch_pick_word — Choose a drawing prompt companion_sketch_draw_paths — Submit bounded normalized drawing paths companion_sketch_clear_canvas — Clear the canvas when authorized companion_sketch_guess — Submit a guess companion_sketch_chat — Send a chat message companion_sketch_leave — Leave the room and invalidate the session ChatGPT does not need to manually manage a bearer token. The page adapter keeps the agent session inside tab-scoped storage and sends only the structured action required by each tool. Drawing requests are intentionally bounded. The agent submits normalized polylines instead of arbitrary canvas commands, and the server validates colors, sizes, coordinates, and stroke counts before accepting them. The tools are registered only in the top-level page, matching the current WebMCP integration requirements. The same game still works normally for human players in browsers that do not support WebMCP. How we built it We built Companion Sketch from scratch as a web application. The backend uses Express for the application server and Socket.IO for realtime communication. Socket.IO handles room membership, drawing updates, chat, readiness, turn changes, timers, and game-state broadcasts. The game engine is server-authoritative. It controls: Player identities and room membership Turn order and timers Private drawing words Stroke validation Guess scoring AI guess windows Canvas clearing permissions Human reconnect and resume behavior The WebMCP layer sits on top of the same game services as the REST API, so ChatGPT and human players follow the same rules instead of using a separate simulation. On the frontend, we rebuilt the interface around mobile play. Room codes are prominent and tap-to-copy. The drawing controls are now a small icon rail: one button for the current tool, one for brush size, one for color, and one for clearing. Each control expands into an overlay palette so the canvas keeps as much space as possible. Human sessions use a private resume token stored by the page. If a phone is backgrounded, suspended, or reloaded, the player can reconnect to the same room and continue from the current game state. The game is deployed over HTTPS so it can be opened directly in ChatGPT’s in-app browser and tested as a real WebMCP experience. Challenges we ran into One of the biggest challenges was supporting two very different types of players. Humans interact visually through a touchscreen and canvas, while an AI agent needs structured state and explicit actions. We had to design one gameplay system that could support both without making the AI feel like an external controller. WebMCP introduced another challenge. The tools had to be registered in the correct top-level document, use strict input schemas, expose only safe actions, and remain useful when the browser does not support the protocol. Realtime drawing also created synchronization problems. A canvas produces many rapidly changing points, so we had to keep drawing responsive while preventing duplicate strokes, invalid coordinates, oversized requests, and unauthorized actions. Privacy was important as well. The active drawer can see the selected word, but other players and agents receive viewer-safe state. The server never exposes a private drawing prompt to someone who should be guessing. Mobile playtesting exposed additional issues. Drawing with a finger behaves differently from drawing with a mouse, and mobile browsers may pause or reload tabs when they are placed in the background. We added pointer handling, canvas fitting, reconnect logic, and state restoration to make those interruptions recoverable. We also redesigned the toolbar after testing revealed that a full row of controls consumed too much canvas space. The new expandable palettes keep the controls available without sacrificing the drawing area. Accomplishments that we’re proud of The thing we’re most proud of is that Companion Sketch became a real playable human-and-AI game rather than just an AI concept demo. We successfully built: A complete realtime drawing and guessing loop Multiplayer rooms with human and AI identities A functioning WebMCP integration with eight native Website Tools An AI player that can join, choose words, draw, guess, chat, and leave Shared server-authoritative state between the human UI, WebMCP, and REST fallback Private word handling and validated drawing actions A responsive mobile interface with a large canvas and compact controls Recovery from phone backgrounding and page reloads A deployed HTTPS version that can be played from ChatGPT’s browser Automated coverage for state, authorization, privacy, WebMCP registration, and reconnect behavior We also verified the game on a phone-sized 390×844 layout and tested the deployed WebMCP page directly. Seeing people play the game has been especially rewarding. Early playtesters created surprisingly strange drawings, guessed in unexpected ways, and made it clear that the most interesting part of the project is the shared interaction between human creativity and AI interpretation.
