# FROZEN SCORING RUBRIC (pre-registered before any Stage 1 review)

Scores are integers 1-10 on each of the FOUR official criteria. Every score carries:
rationale, evidence_surfaces, confidence (0-1). Reviewers use the full scale and these
band anchors verbatim. Category neutrality is absolute: a game, art piece, music tool,
CRM, weird toy, and developer tool are all eligible for 10/10 on every criterion if they
satisfy that criterion exceptionally well FOR WHAT THEY ARE TRYING TO BE.
No aesthetic penalties. Signs of hurried AI work matter only as actual product-quality
problems (incoherence, broken flow, placeholder content), never as style.

## WebMCP Leverage — what does WebMCP change for this product?
Judge the delta WebMCP makes to reliability, precision, structured state access,
shared human-agent state, repeatability, and capability. Tool COUNT is not leverage.
- 9-10 TRANSFORMATIVE: the intended experience is impractical without the structured
  agent surface; human and agent share live product state; the collaboration could not
  be replicated comparably well by a general agent driving the UI.
- 7-8 MAJOR DELTA: without WebMCP a general agent is markedly less reliable/precise
  here; the agent is central to the product, not a bolt-on.
- 5-6 MEANINGFUL DELTA: existing capability packaged into a genuinely better product
  experience via structured tools; same outcome otherwise possible with real friction.
- 3-4 MINOR DELTA: generic browser automation achieves comparable outcomes; WebMCP is
  a convenience layer.
- 1-2 COSMETIC: exposes an action a UI button already offers; no meaningful change.

## Execution — is the actual product coherent, intentional, complete for its scope?
Stage 1 scores EXECUTION_CLAIMED: coherence/intentionality/completeness as evidenced by
the packet (description + packaging + video frames/transcript). Thin evidence caps the
score (end-to-end credit requires end-to-end evidence) and lowers confidence.
Stage 2 may rescore as EXECUTION_OBSERVED; latest evidence-backed score replaces, never
sums.
- 9-10: all surfaces agree; central workflow demonstrably completes; states handled;
  feels finished for its stated scope.
- 7-8: strong coherence and intentionality; central workflow appears functional; minor
  unfinished edges.
- 5-6: plausibly real with visible gaps; scope unclear or core not demonstrated
  end-to-end.
- 3-4: thin prototype; contradictions between surfaces; placeholder content.
- 1-2: non-functional or incoherent for its claimed scope.

## Creativity & Ambition — novelty of concept and interaction model
Judge ONLY: novelty of the concept, novelty of the interaction model, originality
relative to existing concepts, ambition, memorable/surprising use of the medium, and
the depth with which the concept is pursued. WebMCP indispensability belongs under
WebMCP Leverage, NOT here: a project can be extremely creative even if another agent
API could theoretically have enabled something similar.
- 9-10: genuinely novel concept or interaction model; ambitious scope pursued deeply
  and coherently; memorable.
- 7-8: fresh synthesis or notably original interaction; ambitious for a hackathon.
- 5-6: competent take on a known category with a real new angle.
- 3-4: derivative; novelty mostly surface details.
- 1-2: clone/template with trivial changes.
"That the category exists" is never a penalty; judge the submitted concept.

## Potential Impact — credible value for the intended audience
Judge: is there a real audience; is there a real problem, desire, or use case for that
audience; does the demonstrated solution genuinely address it. NO commercial adoption,
monetization, market pull, or startup viability is required. Entertainment, play, art,
creative expression, learning, niche hobby use, experimentation, and community value
are legitimate forms of impact. A tiny audience fully justifies a top score.
- 9-10: specific real audience with a real problem/desire; solution demonstrably
  addresses it; credible value, appeal, usefulness, or meaning for that audience.
- 7-8: clear audience and problem, good fit, credible value.
- 5-6: identifiable audience, looser fit or partial address.
- 3-4: speculative audience or strained fit; impact mostly aspirational.
- 1-2: no coherent audience/problem pairing.
Audience size is NOT a multiplier.

## Aggregate & ranking
aggregate = leverage + execution + impact + creativity (4-40). Nothing else enters.
Tie-breaks: Leverage, then Execution, then Impact, then Creativity, then higher
combined evidence confidence, then slug alphabetical. See PROTOCOL.md Ranking section.

It's a point and click adventure, with incredible humour and often tough puzzles. I wanted to explore whether I could let an agent play a game via WebMCP and I wanted to see whether I could make that happen using Codex. I also wanted a fun way to test WebMCP beyond a conventional website. If WebMCP can describe a web application to an agent, could it describe a 35-year-old game running inside WebAssembly? What it does The applications runs the original DOS VGA release of The Secret of Monkey Island in a browser using ScummVM compiled to WebAssembly. Players provide their own game files, which stay inside the browser and are never uploaded. Once loaded, the game exposes seven WebMCP tools. An AI agent can observe the current room, see characters and objects, inspect inventory, walk to destinations, use the game’s verbs, choose dialogue responses, start a new game, wait for actions to complete, and save progress. In my experience, the agent mostly plays through WebMCP, though I have observed it take screenshots once in a while to see where they are in the game. How I built it ChatGPT and I started with ScummVM and a fork that exposes semantic information from its SCUMM engine. I pinned that source revision and maintain my browser-specific changes as a series of patches. I compiled the SCUMM engine to WebAssembly using Emscripten. A C++ bridge exposes observations and actions from inside the running engine, while a TypeScript controller manages revisions, asynchronous actions, errors, cancellation, and traces. A WebMCP adapter registers the seven tools with the browser. I built the surrounding application with TypeScript and Vite. It validates user-selected game files before mounting them into ScummVM’s in-memory filesystem. The browser shell supports both the File System Access API and a directory-input fallback for embedded browsers. Saved games are written through ScummVM and synchronize them to IndexedDB using Emscripten’s IDBFS. The hosted version runs as a ChatGPT Site, while the complete ScummVM and WebAssembly toolchain can also be built locally from source. Challenges I ran into The largest challenge was connecting a synchronous, frame-driven game engine to asynchronous browser tools. Walking, conversations, scripts, and room transitions do not finish when a command is accepted, so I needed an explicit action lifecycle and a wait_for_game tool that observes the engine rather than relying on fixed delays. Dialogue was a pain. The original SCUMM interface reuses numbered verb slots for conversation choices, including hidden and consumed entries. I had to ensure the choices reported through WebMCP exactly matched those the engine would accept. In early testing the agent would choose an old option and exit conversations when it meant to continue learning information. Persistence was even more fun. Calling ScummVM’s save function directly from JavaScript re-entered WebAssembly while Emscripten’s Asyncify machinery had already suspended the game loop, producing an out-of-bounds memory failure. I fixed this by scheduling saves and restores through the normal game loop, polling for native completion, and synchronizing browser storage only afterward. Browser file permissions were another challenge. I could not bundle or redistribute the game data, ScummVM is GPL3.0, but the game remains under copyright. I wanted to use the browser File APIs to maintain consistent access to a directory with user permission, but the built-in ChatGPT browser doesn't support this API. Chrome still should, so loading the game files once should make it possible to continue when revisiting later. Accomplishments I’m proud of I completed a real playthrough from a new game to the SCUMM Bar, the town on Melee Island and beyond using only WebMCP tools after the initial game file selection. The agent navigated between rooms and held multi-step conversations with an ordinary pirate in the SCUMM Bar, the important-looking pirates, and even with a dog. The agent set off to master the three trials to become a pirate, selecting swordsmanship as its first challenge. I'm genuinely proud to have worked with Codex to build an application that I wouldn't know where to start with (patching the 25 year old C++ in the ScummVM) while also learning how WebMCP works and connecting the two. All done while reviving my love for grog. What I learned I learned that old games contain a rich internal world. Rooms, actors, objects, verbs, inventory, dialogue, and scripts are all structured inside the engine; the difficult part is exposing that structure safely and consistently to an agent. I also learned that observation is as important as action. Agents perform much more reliably when every action is based on a fresh revision and the environment provides an explicit signal that an asynchronous action has finished. Most importantly, I learned that WebMCP can make an interactive experience agent-accessible without reducing it to coordinates and screenshots. The agent can reason about “talk to the pirate” or “open the door” rather than guessing where those things are on a canvas. What’s next for SCUMMWebMCP I would like to see whether an agent can play the entire game through on their own. Plus, I love the game, so I'd like to play it with an agent. I have experimented with voice mode in ChatGPT to direct the game, which was good, but I couldn't get ChatGPT to put on a pirate accent while playing. It did drop some extra "Arrrr" into it's vocabulary though. One thing that didn't work initially was audio. I would like to get the audio back as the soundtrack to the game is as captivating as the pirate banter. My next step is to broaden the experiment beyond one release of one game. I would like to support more SCUMM titles and game variants while keeping their engine-specific semantics behind a consistent WebMCP interface. I would also like to build some explicit memory into the game for agents that are playing. Ensuring they know what they are holding and what they are currently trying to achieve would give them a better chance to win the game. Ultimately, I would like to learn the real secret of Monkey Island. Like Comment Story Updates Civium offers two ways to try out the experience. on the demo web, the companion chatbot that does the clicks for you. on the demo web, the findings are presented inline for your approval. on the demo web, once posted, civium will remember. on GitHub with extension installed, the companion box shows on PRs page. on GitHub with extension installed, the PR details page presents a pointer to all the findings. on GitHub with extension installed, the findings are displayed inline on the changes page. Inspiration Code review is one of the highest-leverage parts of software development, but it also demands sustained attention. Reviewers have to understand context, trace impact, spot risks, and decide what actually matters. AI can reduce the mechanical work in that first pass. But replacing the reviewer with a bot creates a different problem: noisy comments, shallow conclusions, and unclear human ownership. Civium Systems for Code Review is built around a simple idea: let the agent prepare the review, and let the reviewer make the judgment. What it does Civium Systems for Code Review turns the pull-request queue into a shared workspace for a reviewer and an agent. The agent can: review one pull request or the entire open queue; surface pull requests that still need a decision; and organize the queue so reviewers can focus on the changes that deserve attention. The reviewer sees the results inside the normal review flow. Pull requests with no findings are clearly marked, so they can be skipped without opening the diff. Findings appear directly below the relevant changed lines, with the surrounding code still visible. Every finding starts as a private draft. The reviewer decides whether to dismiss it or approve it. Nothing is published simply because the agent found it. For this demo, the review engine intentionally uses one narrow, deterministic rule: it finds snake_case variable declarations on changed JavaScript and TypeScript lines. This keeps the behavior repeatable and the scope honest. The goal is not to present a complete review engine, but to demonstrate a better workflow for people and agents to review code together. WebMCP makes that workflow practical for an agent. Instead of inspecting page markup and guessing which controls to use, the agent gets a small set of meaningful actions exposed directly by the application. The same workflow still works manually when WebMCP is unavailable. How we built it The application uses: React 19 and Next.js-compatible routing via Vinext WebMCP tool registration through document.modelContext Babel's TypeScript-aware parser for deterministic AST inspection Node's built-in test runner for fixture, state, scanner, session, and comment-equivalence tests The pull-request queue exposes two WebMCP tools: review_pull_request accepts one validated pull-request number and starts its review. set_pull_request_filter accepts a constrained (demo only) mode for showing all pull requests, reviews needing action, or pull requests from a known author. Both tools update the same React state used by the visible interface, so agent actions and manual actions stay in sync. Review requests go through server routes backed by Cloudflare D1. Review projects are deduplicated by repository, pull-request number, and head SHA. The scanner parses JavaScript and TypeScript declarations, evaluates only added lines, and maps persisted findings back to the exact file and line in the diff. The on-page Civium Companion uses the same underlying operations. It maps user's semantic requests to the same actions exposed through WebMCP. Challenges we ran into The hardest design question was deciding where the agent should stop. Once an agent can inspect code and propose findings, it is tempting to let it publish those findings automatically. But that would weaken the human ownership Civium is designed to preserve. We chose a strict boundary: the agent can organize and prepare the review, but the reviewer decides what gets published. Another challenge was keeping the demo realistic without overstating what we built. We intentionally limited the review rule, constrained API inputs, and scoped GitHub publication to a small set of frozen example pull requests. We also had to make agent actions feel like part of the product rather than a separate automation layer. Using the same application state for both WebMCP actions and manual interactions helped keep the experience consistent. Accomplishments that we're proud of One natural-language request can start a structured review across the queue. The agent can narrow the queue to the pull requests that still need human attention. Findings persist across navigation and appear at the exact lines that triggered them. The scanner inspects parsed declarations rather than matching arbitrary text. Only added lines are evaluated. Repeated reviews of the same pull-request version do not create duplicate work. Agent and manual actions operate on the same application state. The workflow remains understandable and usable without WebMCP. Most importantly, the agent can take on repetitive review work without taking ownership of the final decision away from the reviewer. What we learned WebMCP is most useful when an application exposes meaningful work rather than every individual UI control. Civium gives the agent only two tools, but those tools are enough to review and organize an entire queue. The agent does not need to understand every button on the page to be useful. We also learned that agent actions need visible consequences. Reviewers should be able to see when a pull request enters review, when results arrive, and which findings still need a decision. That visibility matters because trust does not come from the agent simply saying that work was done. It comes from being able to see what it did and make the final judgment yourself. What's next for Civium Systems for Code Review The demo proves the workflow. The next step is turning it into something real engineering teams can use every day. That means supporting real repositories and organizations, production-grade authentication and permissions, and reliable handling of changing pull requests. The review engine also needs to grow beyond one fixed rule. It should understand more kinds of code issues, use repository and team context, and let teams decide what the agent should look for. As the product scales, Civium will also need stronger infrastructure around security, reliability, auditability, and background processing. The core idea stays the same: let agents do the repetitive review work, while people keep control of the decisions that matter. Instead of stopping at a conversational recommendation, I wanted users to move from a business problem to measurable assumptions, an opportunity assessment, and a proposed implementation plan—with results they could inspect. What it does AIMEC Alpha evaluates a business automation opportunity using five diagnostics: AI return on investment AI operating costs Data readiness Agentic automation readiness Overall AI readiness Two complementary specialists then help interpret the findings: The AI Opportunity Analyst combines diagnostic results with the supplied business context to assess the opportunity, identify risks, and recommend whether to proceed, pilot, or hold.
