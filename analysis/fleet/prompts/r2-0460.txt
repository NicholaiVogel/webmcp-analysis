# FROZEN SCORING RUBRIC (pre-registered before any Stage 1 review)

Scores are integers 1-10 on each of the FOUR official criteria. Every score carries:
rationale, evidence_surfaces, confidence (0-1). Reviewers use the full scale and these
band anchors verbatim. Category neutrality is absolute: a game, art piece, music tool,
CRM, weird toy, and developer tool are all eligible for 10/10 on every criterion if they
satisfy that criterion exceptionally well FOR WHAT THEY ARE TRYING TO BE.
No aesthetic penalties. Signs of hurried AI work matter only as actual product-quality
problems (incoherence, broken flow, placeholder content), never as style.

## WebMCP Leverage — what does WebMCP change for this product?
Judge the delta WebMCP makes to reliability, precision, structured state access,
shared human-agent state, repeatability, and capability. Tool COUNT is not leverage.
- 9-10 TRANSFORMATIVE: the intended experience is impractical without the structured
  agent surface; human and agent share live product state; the collaboration could not
  be replicated comparably well by a general agent driving the UI.
- 7-8 MAJOR DELTA: without WebMCP a general agent is markedly less reliable/precise
  here; the agent is central to the product, not a bolt-on.
- 5-6 MEANINGFUL DELTA: existing capability packaged into a genuinely better product
  experience via structured tools; same outcome otherwise possible with real friction.
- 3-4 MINOR DELTA: generic browser automation achieves comparable outcomes; WebMCP is
  a convenience layer.
- 1-2 COSMETIC: exposes an action a UI button already offers; no meaningful change.

## Execution — is the actual product coherent, intentional, complete for its scope?
Stage 1 scores EXECUTION_CLAIMED: coherence/intentionality/completeness as evidenced by
the packet (description + packaging + video frames/transcript). Thin evidence caps the
score (end-to-end credit requires end-to-end evidence) and lowers confidence.
Stage 2 may rescore as EXECUTION_OBSERVED; latest evidence-backed score replaces, never
sums.
- 9-10: all surfaces agree; central workflow demonstrably completes; states handled;
  feels finished for its stated scope.
- 7-8: strong coherence and intentionality; central workflow appears functional; minor
  unfinished edges.
- 5-6: plausibly real with visible gaps; scope unclear or core not demonstrated
  end-to-end.
- 3-4: thin prototype; contradictions between surfaces; placeholder content.
- 1-2: non-functional or incoherent for its claimed scope.

## Creativity & Ambition — novelty of concept and interaction model
Judge ONLY: novelty of the concept, novelty of the interaction model, originality
relative to existing concepts, ambition, memorable/surprising use of the medium, and
the depth with which the concept is pursued. WebMCP indispensability belongs under
WebMCP Leverage, NOT here: a project can be extremely creative even if another agent
API could theoretically have enabled something similar.
- 9-10: genuinely novel concept or interaction model; ambitious scope pursued deeply
  and coherently; memorable.
- 7-8: fresh synthesis or notably original interaction; ambitious for a hackathon.
- 5-6: competent take on a known category with a real new angle.
- 3-4: derivative; novelty mostly surface details.
- 1-2: clone/template with trivial changes.
"That the category exists" is never a penalty; judge the submitted concept.

## Potential Impact — credible value for the intended audience
Judge: is there a real audience; is there a real problem, desire, or use case for that
audience; does the demonstrated solution genuinely address it. NO commercial adoption,
monetization, market pull, or startup viability is required. Entertainment, play, art,
creative expression, learning, niche hobby use, experimentation, and community value
are legitimate forms of impact. A tiny audience fully justifies a top score.
- 9-10: specific real audience with a real problem/desire; solution demonstrably
  addresses it; credible value, appeal, usefulness, or meaning for that audience.
- 7-8: clear audience and problem, good fit, credible value.
- 5-6: identifiable audience, looser fit or partial address.
- 3-4: speculative audience or strained fit; impact mostly aspirational.
- 1-2: no coherent audience/problem pairing.
Audience size is NOT a multiplier.

## Aggregate & ranking
aggregate = leverage + execution + impact + creativity (4-40). Nothing else enters.
Tie-breaks: Leverage, then Execution, then Impact, then Creativity, then higher
combined evidence confidence, then slug alphabetical. See PROTOCOL.md Ranking section.

That is a genuinely different mechanism from generating an image, and right now it is much worse at it. It is also version one of something I have not seen anyone try properly: generative AI that draws rather than generates. The picture is a document the model and I are both editing. Prompted image generation started out worse than this and got where it is because enough people agreed it was worth pushing on. I think this is worth pushing on for a different reason: I want a picture I can reach into and argue with, and a bitmap has never once let me do that, and I have fun drawing with my artistic friend. Like Comment Story Updates Five eligibility claims cross two WebMCP sites. Birth date, exact GPA, transcript, address, student ID, and name stay in the wallet. Inspiration Browser agents can now work across websites. That creates a privacy problem: proving one fact often means copying an entire private record into the agent conversation. A fellowship may need to know that an applicant is over 18, enrolled, studying an eligible field, above a GPA threshold, and resident in an eligible region. It does not need the person's birth date, student ID, exact GPA, transcript, home address, or name. Proof Courier started with one question: can the agent carry the answer without carrying the record? What it does Proof Courier is a working two-site WebMCP workflow backed by three independent production artifacts. The fellowship verifier publishes the five claims it needs, along with the audience, purpose, expiry limit, fresh nonce, prohibited fields, and a rule that only the person may submit. The private wallet checks that request before preparing anything. Its Request Firewall blocks an unsafe audience, purpose, nonce, or request for automatic submission. If a site asks for raw records, extra claims, or a longer lifetime, the wallet releases nothing and returns a machine-readable counterproposal containing only the five derived claims and a ten-minute lifetime. Once the verifier accepts that smaller request, the wallet prepares it for visible review. The agent still cannot export anything. The person sees what will be shared, who will receive it, why it is needed, and when it expires. Only the person's approval registers wallet_export_proof . The tool works once and then disappears. The agent carries the proof to the verifier, which checks it and prepares the application. There is no agent tool for final submission. The result is simple: five eligibility claims cross between the sites. The private source values stay inside the wallet. Why WebMCP This experience depends on structured tools and live page state. The verifier gives the agent a precise request instead of making it guess from pixels. The wallet turns consent into real capability control. Before approval, export does not exist. After approval, it appears for one use. After that use, it is replaced by a read-only receipt. That 0 to 1 to 0 capability lifecycle means the person and agent see the same authority state. It also gives the agent a safe recovery path when a website asks for too much. The wallet and verifier run on separate origins. They do not share browser storage or fetch data from each other. In the tested journey, the proof returned by one WebMCP tool and passed to the other is the only bridge. How we built it Both pages register their own tools with document.modelContext.registerTool . The synthetic proof bundle uses Web Crypto. A P-256 issuer signature authenticates the credential metadata. Merkle inclusion paths reveal only the requested claims. A holder signature binds the audience, purpose, nonce, expiry, disclosure set, approval time, and one-use consent grant. The verifier rejects an untrusted issuer, wrong audience or purpose, expired proof, repeated nonce, missing or extra claim, altered disclosure, forged signature, or consent dated in the future. The wallet, verifier, and showcase deploy as independent artifacts. Each canonical origin serves only its own routes and entry chunk. The 21-check production matrix returns 200 for the owning route and chunk, and 404 for every peer role route and peer entry chunk. Release checks also keep wallet signing fixtures out of verifier and showcase artifacts, remove source maps, scan for raw synthetic record values, and verify six security headers on every role. Challenges The hardest part was separating preparation from permission. A confirmation message was not enough. The agent's actual tool inventory had to change after the visible approval and change again after the permitted export. An early version also included linkable identity metadata in otherwise safe receipts. We removed it and added checks around every agent-facing result. Privacy here is not a promise in the interface. It is a boundary tested in the data path. The Request Firewall introduced a second challenge. Refusing a bad request is easy; helping the agent continue safely is harder. The wallet now explains why the request failed and, when possible, proposes the smallest acceptable replacement. What we learned Tool schemas are part of the trust model. A useful WebMCP tool needs clear inputs, bounded output, honest annotations, and a lifecycle that matches its authority. We also learned where the human should remain in control. The agent is good at reading requirements, comparing policies, and carrying a proof. The person should approve disclosure and make the final consequential decision. WebMCP connects the two by allowing an AI agent to directly use tools such as analyze_situation, search_laws, get_next_steps, draft_demand_letter, and find_help through the live website without requiring separate APIs or integrations. WebMCP also makes Justice Portal interactive by allowing an agent to analyze situations, populate forms, navigate the site, identify relevant laws, determine missing information, create action plans and evidence checklists, draft demand letters, and connect users with appropriate legal-help organizations. Multiple tools can be combined to address issues such as eviction procedures, deposit limits, and notice requirements. In this way, Justice Portal transforms from a passive legal information website into an interactive legal assistant where people and AI agents work together to understand, organize, and respond to everyday legal problems while keeping the user involved and in control. How we built it Justice Portal is a frontend-only React 19 + TypeScript application built with Vite and Tailwind CSS. It has no backend, database, or API keys, so the legal data, case analysis, and generated documents run entirely in the user's browser, keeping sensitive information local and making the system easy to audit. The application is built around a single tool layer that powers both the user interface and AI agents. It contains a structured Ugandan legal dataset, a referral directory, and a deterministic matching engine that analyzes a user's problem, identifies relevant laws, rights, remedies, evidence, next steps, and legal-help services. Because the engine is rule-based, users can see why particular laws were matched instead of relying on a black-box model. WebMCP exposes these capabilities through 13 tools, including analyzing situations, searching laws, retrieving cases, generating action plans and demand letters, finding legal help, filling forms, and navigating the application. Both the UI and AI agents use the same tool handlers, meaning agent actions immediately update what the user sees. A built-in WebMCP Inspector allows developers to view tool schemas, test tools, inspect results and errors, measure execution time, and view the history of UI and agent calls. This makes Justice Portal both an accessible legal-information platform and a practical demonstration of how WebMCP enables AI agents to interact directly with web applications. Challenges we ran into One of our biggest challenges was understanding WebMCP and how it actually fits into the interaction between a website and an AI agent. At first, it was difficult to understand why WebMCP was necessary when an agent could already interact with a website through its normal user interface. We gradually learned that the real value is in allowing a website to expose meaningful, structured capabilities that an agent can directly use instead of relying only on visual interaction. Another challenge was ideation . We wanted to build something where WebMCP was genuinely useful rather than simply adding it to an unrelated application. We therefore had to think carefully about problems that involve multiple steps and where an AI agent could meaningfully collaborate with a human. Finally, designing the application around a frontend-only architecture with mock data required us to focus on the core experience and demonstrate the concept without spending too much time building infrastructure that was not essential to the WebMCP demonstration. Accomplishments that we're proud of We are proud of turning an initial idea into a working concept that demonstrates how AI agents and web applications can work together through WebMCP . One of our biggest accomplishments was understanding and implementing the idea of exposing application capabilities as WebMCP tools. Instead of treating WebMCP as another interface element, we designed tools around meaningful actions that an agent could perform within Justice Portal. Another accomplishment is keeping the human in the loop . Justice Compass is designed to assist users rather than make consequential legal decisions on their behalf. Important outputs remain reviewable and editable before the user decides what to do. Most importantly, we are proud that the project allowed us to explore a new technology while applying it to a problem that has real-world relevance. What we learned We learned that WebMCP is more than a mechanism for an AI agent to search or navigate a website. We learned how a website can expose structured tools that allow an AI agent to interact with the application's capabilities directly. This helped us understand the difference between an agent simply navigating a user interface and an agent being able to use meaningful application-level operations. We also learned how multiple WebMCP tools can be combined into a larger workflow. For example, an agent can search for relevant information, identify missing facts, create or update a case, organize evidence, and prepare a document as part of one broader task. The project also taught us the importance of designing around the actual problem being solved rather than simply choosing a technology and trying to find somewhere to use it.
