# FROZEN SCORING RUBRIC (pre-registered before any Stage 1 review)

Scores are integers 1-10 on each of the FOUR official criteria. Every score carries:
rationale, evidence_surfaces, confidence (0-1). Reviewers use the full scale and these
band anchors verbatim. Category neutrality is absolute: a game, art piece, music tool,
CRM, weird toy, and developer tool are all eligible for 10/10 on every criterion if they
satisfy that criterion exceptionally well FOR WHAT THEY ARE TRYING TO BE.
No aesthetic penalties. Signs of hurried AI work matter only as actual product-quality
problems (incoherence, broken flow, placeholder content), never as style.

## WebMCP Leverage — what does WebMCP change for this product?
Judge the delta WebMCP makes to reliability, precision, structured state access,
shared human-agent state, repeatability, and capability. Tool COUNT is not leverage.
- 9-10 TRANSFORMATIVE: the intended experience is impractical without the structured
  agent surface; human and agent share live product state; the collaboration could not
  be replicated comparably well by a general agent driving the UI.
- 7-8 MAJOR DELTA: without WebMCP a general agent is markedly less reliable/precise
  here; the agent is central to the product, not a bolt-on.
- 5-6 MEANINGFUL DELTA: existing capability packaged into a genuinely better product
  experience via structured tools; same outcome otherwise possible with real friction.
- 3-4 MINOR DELTA: generic browser automation achieves comparable outcomes; WebMCP is
  a convenience layer.
- 1-2 COSMETIC: exposes an action a UI button already offers; no meaningful change.

## Execution — is the actual product coherent, intentional, complete for its scope?
Stage 1 scores EXECUTION_CLAIMED: coherence/intentionality/completeness as evidenced by
the packet (description + packaging + video frames/transcript). Thin evidence caps the
score (end-to-end credit requires end-to-end evidence) and lowers confidence.
Stage 2 may rescore as EXECUTION_OBSERVED; latest evidence-backed score replaces, never
sums.
- 9-10: all surfaces agree; central workflow demonstrably completes; states handled;
  feels finished for its stated scope.
- 7-8: strong coherence and intentionality; central workflow appears functional; minor
  unfinished edges.
- 5-6: plausibly real with visible gaps; scope unclear or core not demonstrated
  end-to-end.
- 3-4: thin prototype; contradictions between surfaces; placeholder content.
- 1-2: non-functional or incoherent for its claimed scope.

## Creativity & Ambition — novelty of concept and interaction model
Judge ONLY: novelty of the concept, novelty of the interaction model, originality
relative to existing concepts, ambition, memorable/surprising use of the medium, and
the depth with which the concept is pursued. WebMCP indispensability belongs under
WebMCP Leverage, NOT here: a project can be extremely creative even if another agent
API could theoretically have enabled something similar.
- 9-10: genuinely novel concept or interaction model; ambitious scope pursued deeply
  and coherently; memorable.
- 7-8: fresh synthesis or notably original interaction; ambitious for a hackathon.
- 5-6: competent take on a known category with a real new angle.
- 3-4: derivative; novelty mostly surface details.
- 1-2: clone/template with trivial changes.
"That the category exists" is never a penalty; judge the submitted concept.

## Potential Impact — credible value for the intended audience
Judge: is there a real audience; is there a real problem, desire, or use case for that
audience; does the demonstrated solution genuinely address it. NO commercial adoption,
monetization, market pull, or startup viability is required. Entertainment, play, art,
creative expression, learning, niche hobby use, experimentation, and community value
are legitimate forms of impact. A tiny audience fully justifies a top score.
- 9-10: specific real audience with a real problem/desire; solution demonstrably
  addresses it; credible value, appeal, usefulness, or meaning for that audience.
- 7-8: clear audience and problem, good fit, credible value.
- 5-6: identifiable audience, looser fit or partial address.
- 3-4: speculative audience or strained fit; impact mostly aspirational.
- 1-2: no coherent audience/problem pairing.
Audience size is NOT a multiplier.

## Aggregate & ranking
aggregate = leverage + execution + impact + creativity (4-40). Nothing else enters.
Tie-breaks: Leverage, then Execution, then Impact, then Creativity, then higher
combined evidence confidence, then slug alphabetical. See PROTOCOL.md Ranking section.

Use LLM-powered WebMCP agents to discover and prepare tours directly with local operators—cutting out marketplace middlemen while keeping payment human-controlled. Like Comment Story Updates Booking Interface Codex with WebMCP Inspiration Tour bookings are often controlled by large marketplaces that sit between travelers and local operators. These intermediaries can add commissions, weaken the direct customer relationship, and make small operators dependent on centralized platforms. Our inspiration was to use WebMCP to create a more direct model. A traveler can ask an LLM what they want in natural language, and the agent can interact directly with tools published by the tour operator's own website—without scraping the page or routing the customer through a marketplace. The agent automates repetitive steps such as discovering tours, comparing departures, checking current capacity, and preparing the visible cart. The operator retains ownership of its inventory, brand, and customer relationship, while the traveler remains in control of checkout, payment, and final confirmation. What it does Tours Booking WebMCP lets an AI agent work with the same inventory and cart that the traveler sees. It exposes four WebMCP tools: search_departures searches the page's current inventory snapshot. get_departure refreshes and re-checks the capacity, price, and transfer options for a specific departure. prepare_booking validates a complete selection against refreshed capacity and atomically replaces the visible cart. get_booking_summary reports the cart contents, total price, estimated authorization amount, editability, and recommended next action. An agent can help travelers discover suitable tours, verify that enough spaces remain, and prepare a cart for review. It cannot collect contact or billing details, handle cards, authorize payment, reserve inventory, or confirm a booking. Those actions remain human-controlled in the visible booking experience. The normal interface continues to work when WebMCP is unavailable, making the integration a progressive enhancement. How we built it We built a framework-independent TypeScript adapter around WebMCP's imperative document.modelContext.registerTool() API. The host booking page supplies normalized inventory and cart-state callbacks. The adapter owns the tool descriptions, strict JSON schemas, matching runtime validation, freshness rules, response formatting, cancellation, lifecycle cleanup, and safe cart-preparation protocol. For an exact departure check or cart preparation, the adapter asks the host to refresh inventory instead of presenting cached data as current. prepare_booking validates the complete replacement cart, checks the page state before and after the asynchronous refresh, and performs exactly one atomic commit. If validation or a state check fails, the existing cart remains unchanged. Inventory-derived results are marked as untrusted content. Read-only tools are annotated accordingly, while the cart-mutating tool is explicitly identified as non-read-only. The repository also includes a backend-free Vite demo with deterministic in-memory inventory, a fake document.modelContext test harness, documentation, and agent-evaluation scenarios. Challenges we ran into One challenge was designing around an experimental browser API whose availability and behavior may change. The application still needed to remain usable in browsers without WebMCP. Freshness was another major challenge. Availability can change between an agent's search and its attempt to prepare a cart, so saved inventory cannot be presented as current. We added explicit freshness metadata, forced refreshes for exact checks, and capacity validation immediately before committing cart state. Asynchronous refreshes also introduced race conditions. Checkout could begin, another browser tab could become active, or an invocation could be cancelled while capacity was being refreshed. We addressed these cases with before-and-after state checks, serialized preparation, abort-signal propagation, and an atomic commit boundary. Finally, we wanted useful agent capabilities without quietly turning a cart tool into a payment or reservation tool. Identity, billing, cards, payments, reservations, and confirmations therefore remain completely outside the tool contracts. Accomplishments that we're proud of Built a coherent four-tool WebMCP experience rather than a single demonstration call. Connected agent actions to the same visible cart controlled by the traveler. Implemented fresh-capacity validation and all-or-nothing cart preparation. Preserved a strict human boundary around personal data, payment, reservation, and confirmation. Added 21 deterministic tests covering schemas, freshness, time zones, capacity, atomicity, concurrency, cancellation, lifecycle cleanup, and safety boundaries. Defined nine agent-evaluation scenarios covering tool selection, argument accuracy, ambiguity, unavailable data, and unsafe payment requests. Kept search responses within a compact 1,536-byte target. Created an independently runnable demo requiring no credentials, customer data, private API, or payment provider. Passed the complete formatting, testing, TypeScript build, demo build, and package-verification pipeline. What we learned We learned that designing WebMCP tools is as much a product and security exercise as it is an API exercise. Tool names, descriptions, schemas, annotations, and results collectively shape how reliably an agent behaves. We also learned that freshness must be represented explicitly. "Available" is not meaningful unless the agent and traveler know whether the information came from a current refresh or a saved snapshot. Most importantly, we learned that useful agent assistance does not require handing over the entire user journey. Agents can reduce discovery and coordination work while preserving meaningful human control over sensitive and consequential actions. What's next for Tours Booking WebMCP Next, we plan to: Deploy the demo with the browser headers and origin-trial configuration required by the active WebMCP implementation. Connect the adapter to normalized production inventory through the existing host callbacks without exposing private booking endpoints. Run the evaluation suite across additional WebMCP-capable agents and browsers. Add more inventory scenarios, accessibility testing, and localized user-facing explanations. Continue refining consent and review UX while keeping payment and confirmation human-controlled. Like Comment Story Updates WebMCP-powered inference routing lab comparing Round Robin with capacity- and load-aware routing on heterogeneous workers Inspiration AI inference infrastructure is increasingly heterogeneous: workers can have very different capacities, yet simple strategies such as Round Robin still treat them as interchangeable. A slow worker can build a large queue while faster capacity remains available elsewhere. At the same time, browser agents often interact with applications indirectly by inspecting interfaces, finding controls, and deciding what to click. For infrastructure software, however, operations like comparing routing policies or inspecting a bottleneck have much richer semantics than the buttons that trigger them. ThermalMesh Lab grew from one question: What if an AI agent could understand and operate inference infrastructure through domain-level tools instead of automating a dashboard by sight? What it does ThermalMesh Lab is a browser-based simulator for heterogeneous AI inference clusters. Users can configure workers with different abstract capacities, generate deterministic workloads, and compare two routing strategies: Round Robin — equal distribution without considering capacity or load. Inference-Aware (ThermalMesh) — a simplified strategy using capacity, load, and estimated completion time. The simulator exposes TTFT, queue latency, throughput, completed and unfinished requests, worker utilization, and queue depth. The same application can also be operated by an AI agent through WebMCP Site Tools. An agent can configure the cluster and workload, run benchmarks, compare policies, inspect bottlenecks, and apply a routing strategy. Humans and agents share the same application state, so every agent action is immediately reflected in the dashboard. ThermalMesh Lab is a browser-based simulation, not a hardware benchmark. How we built it ThermalMesh Lab is built with React, TypeScript, and Vite and runs entirely in the browser. The simulation engine generates a deterministic seeded request trace and gives both routing policies the same workload, making comparisons reproducible. WebMCP is integrated through document.modelContext.registerTool() . The application exposes seven domain-level Site Tools: get_cluster_state configure_cluster configure_workload run_benchmark compare_routing_policies inspect_bottlenecks apply_routing_policy The WebMCP layer does not maintain separate agent state. Site Tools and the visible React interface call the same validated application/store operations. We also implemented a state-dependent tool lifecycle. After a valid comparison, ThermalMesh Lab dynamically exposes an eighth tool: apply_winning_configuration If the cluster or workload changes, the comparison becomes stale and that tool is removed. The application requires no backend, database, authentication, or paid API and is deployed as a static site on Cloudflare Pages. Challenges we ran into The biggest challenge was making WebMCP a real application interface rather than a collection of tools that simply mimic button clicks. Operations such as compare_routing_policies and inspect_bottlenecks needed to represent actual infrastructure semantics while keeping the human UI and agent perfectly synchronized. Dynamic registration also introduced lifecycle problems. We had to prevent duplicate or stale registrations, ensure tools always operated on current state, and remove the winning-policy tool immediately when its comparison became invalid. The simulator itself required careful handling as well. Both policies needed identical request traces, overloaded scenarios had to remain numerically safe, and unfinished requests could not appear as misleading zero-latency samples. One of our first live agent tests produced an unexpected result: the agent realized its initial workload had completely saturated the cluster, reduced the workload, reran the comparison, and then selected a better routing policy. The state-dependent lifecycle also works end to end: 7 base tools → comparison → 8 tools → invalidation → 7 tools We are also proud that the simulator does not artificially make Inference-Aware routing win. Blindspot asks the opposite question: what should a good tool contract deliberately withhold? get_bomb_status reports the countdown, strikes, module types, and progress, then explicitly lists the visual facts it refuses to return. The action tools can cut, press, hold, and release, but they never reveal the bomb's appearance. This makes least-privilege interface design playable. If one tool leaks a wire colour or keypad symbol, the co-op game collapses because the agent no longer needs its human partner. This is a tool-contract demonstration, not a claim that WebMCP is a browser sandbox. An agent separately granted vision or DOM access could inspect the rendered page. The mission prompt therefore asks the agent to use only Blindspot's WebMCP tools, making the seven contracts the complete interface for the run. The human-agent experience The agent calls get_bomb_status and get_manual . It asks the human for only the visual facts required by a rule. It acts through typed WebMCP calls while both participants watch every call and result appear live. On the button module, the agent holds the button, a strip lights up only in the human view, the human reports its colour, and the agent schedules the correct release digit. A successful run ends in a mission report with time left, strikes, and tool-call count. The page includes a physical bomb-console interface, responsive human and agent views, an enlarged handoff state, opt-in synthesized sound, accessible redundant colour labels, and a replayable deterministic judge seed. Implementation Blindspot is intentionally small: vanilla HTML, CSS, and JavaScript with no runtime dependencies or build step. src/tools.js declares the seven real contracts and registers each one with document.modelContext ?? navigator.modelContext , including name , description , JSON inputSchema , annotations, and execute . The same instrumented handlers update the live call feed. A fallback console is generated from those schemas only when native WebMCP is unavailable. Native sessions keep the executable controls with the connected agent. The public repo includes an MIT license, complete run instructions, a deterministic judge walkthrough, and 26 headless checks. The suite covers rules, clock throttling, all seven registration descriptors, partial registration reporting, hostile call-feed input, audio opt-in, schema-driven fallback accessibility, and bomb-appearance leak probes across fresh seeded games. GitHub Actions runs the suite and syntax checks on every push. Verified live I tested the production deployment in ChatGPT's in-app browser. It registered all seven tools from the production origin, completed the deterministic bomb through native WebMCP, and ended DEFUSED with zero strikes.
