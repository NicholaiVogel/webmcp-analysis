=== RUBRIC (frozen) ===
# FROZEN SCORING RUBRIC (pre-registered before any Stage 1 review)

Scores are integers 1-10 on each of the FOUR official criteria. Every score carries:
rationale, evidence_surfaces, confidence (0-1). Reviewers use the full scale and these
band anchors verbatim. Category neutrality is absolute: a game, art piece, music tool,
CRM, weird toy, and developer tool are all eligible for 10/10 on every criterion if they
satisfy that criterion exceptionally well FOR WHAT THEY ARE TRYING TO BE.
No aesthetic penalties. Signs of hurried AI work matter only as actual product-quality
problems (incoherence, broken flow, placeholder content), never as style.

## WebMCP Leverage — what does WebMCP change for this product?
Judge the delta WebMCP makes to reliability, precision, structured state access,
shared human-agent state, repeatability, and capability. Tool COUNT is not leverage.
- 9-10 TRANSFORMATIVE: the intended experience is impractical without the structured
  agent surface; human and agent share live product state; the collaboration could not
  be replicated comparably well by a general agent driving the UI.
- 7-8 MAJOR DELTA: without WebMCP a general agent is markedly less reliable/precise
  here; the agent is central to the product, not a bolt-on.
- 5-6 MEANINGFUL DELTA: existing capability packaged into a genuinely better product
  experience via structured tools; same outcome otherwise possible with real friction.
- 3-4 MINOR DELTA: generic browser automation achieves comparable outcomes; WebMCP is
  a convenience layer.
- 1-2 COSMETIC: exposes an action a UI button already offers; no meaningful change.

## Execution — is the actual product coherent, intentional, complete for its scope?
Stage 1 scores EXECUTION_CLAIMED: coherence/intentionality/completeness as evidenced by
the packet (description + packaging + video frames/transcript). Thin evidence caps the
score (end-to-end credit requires end-to-end evidence) and lowers confidence.
Stage 2 may rescore as EXECUTION_OBSERVED; latest evidence-backed score replaces, never
sums.
- 9-10: all surfaces agree; central workflow demonstrably completes; states handled;
  feels finished for its stated scope.
- 7-8: strong coherence and intentionality; central workflow appears functional; minor
  unfinished edges.
- 5-6: plausibly real with visible gaps; scope unclear or core not demonstrated
  end-to-end.
- 3-4: thin prototype; contradictions between surfaces; placeholder content.
- 1-2: non-functional or incoherent for its claimed scope.

## Creativity & Ambition — novelty of concept and interaction model
Judge ONLY: novelty of the concept, novelty of the interaction model, originality
relative to existing concepts, ambition, memorable/surprising use of the medium, and
the depth with which the concept is pursued. WebMCP indispensability belongs under
WebMCP Leverage, NOT here: a project can be extremely creative even if another agent
API could theoretically have enabled something similar.
- 9-10: genuinely novel concept or interaction model; ambitious scope pursued deeply
  and coherently; memorable.
- 7-8: fresh synthesis or notably original interaction; ambitious for a hackathon.
- 5-6: competent take on a known category with a real new angle.
- 3-4: derivative; novelty mostly surface details.
- 1-2: clone/template with trivial changes.
"That the category exists" is never a penalty; judge the submitted concept.

## Potential Impact — credible value for the intended audience
Judge: is there a real audience; is there a real problem, desire, or use case for that
audience; does the demonstrated solution genuinely address it. NO commercial adoption,
monetization, market pull, or startup viability is required. Entertainment, play, art,
creative expression, learning, niche hobby use, experimentation, and community value
are legitimate forms of impact. A tiny audience fully justifies a top score.
- 9-10: specific real audience with a real problem/desire; solution demonstrably
  addresses it; credible value, appeal, usefulness, or meaning for that audience.
- 7-8: clear audience and problem, good fit, credible value.
- 5-6: identifiable audience, looser fit or partial address.
- 3-4: speculative audience or strained fit; impact mostly aspirational.
- 1-2: no coherent audience/problem pairing.
Audience size is NOT a multiplier.

## Aggregate & ranking
aggregate = leverage + execution + impact + creativity (4-40). Nothing else enters.
Tie-breaks: Leverage, then Execution, then Impact, then Creativity, then higher
combined evidence confidence, then slug alphabetical. See PROTOCOL.md Ranking section.

The page opens the application and fills in your contact information, eligibility, and work history from a saved profile. Two-Pass Input: A profile cannot answer everything, and it shouldn't guess. When the form asks "Why this role, and why now?" or asks about relocation, the agent moves to that field, highlights it in amber on screen, and asks you out loud. You answer, it writes down what you said, and the highlight clears. Spoken Corrections: Say "My salary expectation went up to $130,000." Shop for PC parts with an AI assistant that shares your live build, understands your goals, explains compatibility and trade-offs, and keeps you in control of every choice. Like Comment Story Updates Browse PC parts while the shopper and AI assistant share the same live build and goals. AI recommendations appear directly in the storefront with goal impact, compatibility, and trade-offs. Agent-proposed changes are staged as a preview. Only the shopper can approve and apply them. Inspiration Choosing PC parts is difficult, especially for people who do not already understand hardware. There are three problems at the same time: Compatibility: Will this CPU work with this motherboard? Will this GPU fit in the case? Is the power supply sufficient? Understanding specs: Numbers like VRAM, wattage, clock speed, and dimensions do not directly tell a shopper what they mean for the games they want to play. Trade-offs: Every choice affects performance, budget, aesthetics, and the parts that can be selected next. Traditional compatibility checkers can tell you whether parts work together, but they do not feel like shopping with a knowledgeable person who understands what you are trying to achieve. We wanted to explore a different experience: what if the AI assistant you already use could shop with you , directly inside the storefront? What it does PART / NER is a PC parts storefront where a shopper and their AI assistant work on the same live build . The shopper can browse and select products normally. The AI assistant can: understand the current PC build and the shopper's goals recommend products directly inside the storefront explain expected game performance in terms of the shopper's FPS targets explain compatibility problems and trade-offs compare options based on performance, budget, and aesthetics prepare multi-part build changes as a preview The shopper always remains in control. The AI cannot directly apply a staged build change. Agent-proposed changes appear as a before/after preview, and only the shopper can apply them by clicking Approve & apply preview on the page. A key WebMCP interaction is that the shopper can ignore the AI's recommendation, manually choose a different part, and the assistant can continue from that updated live build on the next turn. This is not an AI that shops for you. It is an AI that shops with you. How we built it PART / NER is a frontend-only React application. The React UI and WebMCP tools operate on the same shared Zustand store, so human actions and agent actions always refer to the same build state. The app exposes WebMCP tools for: reading the current build setting shopping goals finding products displaying recommendations in the storefront staging build changes resolving compatibility issues For example, recommend_products ranks and renders product candidates without selecting anything, preview_build_changes stages multi-part changes for human review, and resolve_build_issues is dynamically registered only while a compatibility error exists. The application owns deterministic domain logic for: compatibility rules budget calculations fictional benchmark estimates goal evaluation recommendation ranking The AI does not invent prices, compatibility facts, or FPS numbers. Those values come from the application. There is no backend, database, authentication system, retailer API, or model API in the app itself. The catalog contains 30 fictional PC products and fictional games so the demo is reproducible and does not depend on third-party product data. Challenges we ran into One of the biggest challenges was deciding where the boundary between the AI and the application should be. It would have been easy to let the model reason about compatibility or generate performance claims, but that would make the experience unreliable. Instead, the application owns the facts and deterministic rules, while the AI focuses on understanding intent, comparing options, and explaining trade-offs. Another challenge was making WebMCP visibly useful rather than simply exposing CRUD-style tools. The key was treating the live build and storefront UI as a shared workspace between the shopper and the assistant. We also deliberately removed the agent's ability to apply build changes directly. An assistant can stage a preview, but only the shopper can approve it on the page. This made the human-agent boundary much clearer. Accomplishments that we're proud of We are especially proud that the demo shows a genuine collaborative loop between a person and an AI assistant. The AI can recommend the best-value GPU, but the shopper can choose a different GPU for aesthetic reasons. On the next turn, the assistant reads the updated build and continues naturally from the shopper's decision. Recommendations are rendered directly in the storefront instead of existing only as chat text. Compatibility, game-performance goals, budget impact, and trade-offs are presented together so the shopper can make the final decision with useful context. We also implemented versioned previews so an old agent proposal cannot overwrite a newer human choice. What we learned The most interesting part of WebMCP is not automating a website. It is enabling a human and an agent to collaborate through the same interface while each uses what they are good at. The AI can process constraints and explain options quickly. The human can make subjective decisions about appearance, priorities, and preferences. The web application becomes the shared workspace connecting the two. We also learned that meaningful WebMCP tools should expose semantic actions and live application state, rather than reproducing low-level UI automation. What's next for PART / NER A real version could connect to retailer catalogs and verified benchmark sources, support more PC component categories, and incorporate live inventory and pricing. It could also use the shopper's existing AI context and preferences, when available, to make the experience more personalized without requiring the user to start from scratch in every shopping session. The same interaction model could extend beyond PC parts to other purchases where products depend on one another, such as cameras and lenses, home networking equipment, audio systems, or DIY electronics. Every wrong guess costs somebody a failed checkout or a broken signup. Toolsmith closes that gap without asking the site owner to write code, and without ever letting an agent approve its own work. \n\n What it does \n\n Paste a URL. Toolsmith reads the page, proposes one WebMCP tool per form, drafts a webmcp.js file, and waits. Nothing activates until a person clicks Approve . A receipt comes back, and the file downloads. \n\n There is no language model anywhere in the loop. Every generated line traces to a real field on a real page. \n\n The five tools, registered on document.modelContext : \n\n \n profile_site reads the public structure of one page: title, headings, forms and their fields, top-level navigation. \n propose_tools turns the profile into candidate tools. GET forms become read-only tools. POST forms are marked consequential and get a confirm gate. \n draft_manifest emits the webmcp.js source for the chosen candidates, plus the SHA-256 of that exact text. \n activate_manifest shows the approval card and returns a pending request. This tool cannot approve anything on its own. \n verify_manifest_activation reads back the receipt and checks its digest against the manifest stored in the browser. \n \n\n How we built it \n\n The drafting logic is a deterministic template over detected forms, not a model call. The five tools are registered natively on document.modelContext with AbortSignal cleanup. \n\n The only thing that can activate a manifest is a click on the Approve card. The page checks two properties of that event before it writes a receipt: \n\n \n event.isTrusted must be true, so a synthetic click from script does not count. \n navigator.userActivation.isActive must be true, so the click happened inside a real user gesture. \n \n\n The generated file carries the same discipline forward. Every consequential tool it registers wraps its submit in a browser confirm dialog, so an agent using the output still cannot post a form without a person in the loop. \n\n Challenges we ran into \n\n The hard part was proving the approval boundary still holds when the tool itself produces tools. It was not enough to gate Toolsmith's own Approve card. The output had to inherit the same rule, so every consequential tool in a generated file got its own confirm gate. \n\n Accomplishments that we're proud of \n\n A site owner can read the template and trace every output line to a form on their page. The approval boundary generalized from gating one action on one page to gating every action a generated file can take. And the whole loop runs with zero metered calls. \n\n What we learned \n\n A readable template earns more trust than a model nobody can inspect. One human click is enough to gate an entire generated interface, as long as the gate checks the properties of the click and not just its presence. \n\n What's next for Toolsmith \n\n Point it at a whole site and get one manifest back.
