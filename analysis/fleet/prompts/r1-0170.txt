# FROZEN SCORING RUBRIC (pre-registered before any Stage 1 review)

Scores are integers 1-10 on each of the FOUR official criteria. Every score carries:
rationale, evidence_surfaces, confidence (0-1). Reviewers use the full scale and these
band anchors verbatim. Category neutrality is absolute: a game, art piece, music tool,
CRM, weird toy, and developer tool are all eligible for 10/10 on every criterion if they
satisfy that criterion exceptionally well FOR WHAT THEY ARE TRYING TO BE.
No aesthetic penalties. Signs of hurried AI work matter only as actual product-quality
problems (incoherence, broken flow, placeholder content), never as style.

## WebMCP Leverage — what does WebMCP change for this product?
Judge the delta WebMCP makes to reliability, precision, structured state access,
shared human-agent state, repeatability, and capability. Tool COUNT is not leverage.
- 9-10 TRANSFORMATIVE: the intended experience is impractical without the structured
  agent surface; human and agent share live product state; the collaboration could not
  be replicated comparably well by a general agent driving the UI.
- 7-8 MAJOR DELTA: without WebMCP a general agent is markedly less reliable/precise
  here; the agent is central to the product, not a bolt-on.
- 5-6 MEANINGFUL DELTA: existing capability packaged into a genuinely better product
  experience via structured tools; same outcome otherwise possible with real friction.
- 3-4 MINOR DELTA: generic browser automation achieves comparable outcomes; WebMCP is
  a convenience layer.
- 1-2 COSMETIC: exposes an action a UI button already offers; no meaningful change.

## Execution — is the actual product coherent, intentional, complete for its scope?
Stage 1 scores EXECUTION_CLAIMED: coherence/intentionality/completeness as evidenced by
the packet (description + packaging + video frames/transcript). Thin evidence caps the
score (end-to-end credit requires end-to-end evidence) and lowers confidence.
Stage 2 may rescore as EXECUTION_OBSERVED; latest evidence-backed score replaces, never
sums.
- 9-10: all surfaces agree; central workflow demonstrably completes; states handled;
  feels finished for its stated scope.
- 7-8: strong coherence and intentionality; central workflow appears functional; minor
  unfinished edges.
- 5-6: plausibly real with visible gaps; scope unclear or core not demonstrated
  end-to-end.
- 3-4: thin prototype; contradictions between surfaces; placeholder content.
- 1-2: non-functional or incoherent for its claimed scope.

## Creativity & Ambition — novelty of concept and interaction model
Judge ONLY: novelty of the concept, novelty of the interaction model, originality
relative to existing concepts, ambition, memorable/surprising use of the medium, and
the depth with which the concept is pursued. WebMCP indispensability belongs under
WebMCP Leverage, NOT here: a project can be extremely creative even if another agent
API could theoretically have enabled something similar.
- 9-10: genuinely novel concept or interaction model; ambitious scope pursued deeply
  and coherently; memorable.
- 7-8: fresh synthesis or notably original interaction; ambitious for a hackathon.
- 5-6: competent take on a known category with a real new angle.
- 3-4: derivative; novelty mostly surface details.
- 1-2: clone/template with trivial changes.
"That the category exists" is never a penalty; judge the submitted concept.

## Potential Impact — credible value for the intended audience
Judge: is there a real audience; is there a real problem, desire, or use case for that
audience; does the demonstrated solution genuinely address it. NO commercial adoption,
monetization, market pull, or startup viability is required. Entertainment, play, art,
creative expression, learning, niche hobby use, experimentation, and community value
are legitimate forms of impact. A tiny audience fully justifies a top score.
- 9-10: specific real audience with a real problem/desire; solution demonstrably
  addresses it; credible value, appeal, usefulness, or meaning for that audience.
- 7-8: clear audience and problem, good fit, credible value.
- 5-6: identifiable audience, looser fit or partial address.
- 3-4: speculative audience or strained fit; impact mostly aspirational.
- 1-2: no coherent audience/problem pairing.
Audience size is NOT a multiplier.

## Aggregate & ranking
aggregate = leverage + execution + impact + creativity (4-40). Nothing else enters.
Tie-breaks: Leverage, then Execution, then Impact, then Creativity, then higher
combined evidence confidence, then slug alphabetical. See PROTOCOL.md Ranking section.

Procedures that people and agents write, run and improve together. When a step no longer matches reality, the run records it, and the agent turns that into a fix. Like Comment Story Updates A procedure held as structured data. Each step carries an instruction, a check saying what proof looks like, and an optional condition. Inspiration We run a small agency and a SaaS product on our own infrastructure, so we live on written procedures. Onboarding a client, restoring a backup, rotating a credential. They get written once, carefully, and then the software underneath them changes. A tab is renamed, a field moves, a step becomes somebody else's job. Within months a good portion of the document is wrong. What struck us is why that happens. The moment you discover a procedure is wrong is precisely the moment you cannot fix it. You are mid-task, under time pressure, and correcting the document is somebody else's job. So you work around it silently and move on, and the next person hits the same wall. WebMCP suggested a way out. If the procedure is something an agent can read and edit while you are standing in front of it, then finding the error and fixing it stop being separate jobs. What it does Runbook Studio holds a procedure as structured data rather than prose. Every step has a title, an instruction, an optional check stating what proof looks like, and an optional condition that sends the run to a different step when something is already true. Because the steps are data, three things become possible in one place. Write it with the agent. Ask it to split a vague step, add checks, reorder, or add a condition, and watch the document rearrange. Every change the agent makes is marked on the page. Run it with the agent. A run walks the steps in order, honouring conditions. At each step you confirm the check, or you record a blocker saying what you found instead. Improve it from the run. The blocker is pinned to the step that failed. Afterwards, the agent reads it and amends that step to match reality. That loop is the point. Documentation that gets better every time somebody uses it. The model is domain-neutral. Three examples ship with the app: onboarding a client into a CRM, restoring a database from a backup, and a cooking recipe. How we built it Next.js and TypeScript, client-side state, no database, no authentication. Self-hosted on our own infrastructure behind nginx-proxy. The only server-side component is an optional route that proxies to an OpenAI-compatible endpoint for the built-in agent, rate limited and turn capped. 13 tools are registered on document.modelContext, covering the full lifecycle: reading the runbook, editing steps, setting checks and conditions, running, recording blockers, and turning blockers into amendments. Design decisions worth naming: Tool descriptions are the interface: they are written for a model to read, not a developer. We tested selection accuracy by giving the raw getTools() output to models with no other context and checking which tools they chose for real instructions. Descriptions were tuned until selection was reliable across independent models. The annotations are used properly. readOnlyHint on read tools, untrustedContentHint wherever user-written content flows back to the model. Chrome's budgets are respected: names under 30 characters, descriptions under 500, parameter descriptions under 150, and an output guard that trims oversized responses with a hint to use a narrower tool instead. Tools stay same-origin. exposedTo is unset, so no cross-origin frame can call them. Registration takes an AbortSignal: the execution-time signal is treated as optional, since it is not supplied in the Chrome build we tested against. The app also ships with its own agent, so it needs no browser flag and no ChatGPT to try. A built-in harness discovers the page's tools with getTools() and runs them with executeTool(), either manually or by sending a sentence to a model that chooses the tools itself. With no model configured, manual mode still works with zero setup. Verification was done against Chrome for Testing with native WebMCP, driving executeTool through Playwright and asserting on the resulting DOM, so no tool is reported working on the basis of code inspection alone. Challenges we ran into The API is new and the documentation moves. Older material uses navigator.modelContext; current Chrome uses document.modelContext. The API is undefined outside a secure context, which makes several obvious testing approaches fail silently rather than loudly. Steering a model reliably. Given only tool descriptions, a model would sometimes re-read every step, propose a change instead of making it, or keep editing after finishing. We fixed this with server-side guards rather than by making the tools more forgiving, because the tools have to behave correctly for any agent, not only ours. We could not test in ChatGPT's in-app browser. Our development machine is old enough that it is capped at a Chrome version predating WebMCP, and it cannot run the ChatGPT desktop app at all. So we never saw our own app inside the environment the judges will use. We compensated rather than hoped. Every tool was verified against Chrome for Testing 152 with native WebMCP, headless on our server, driving executeTool through Playwright and asserting on the resulting DOM. We separately tested tool-selection accuracy by giving the raw getTools() output to models with no other context, checking they chose the right tools for real instructions before we relied on the descriptions. The constraint turned out to be productive. Because we could not lean on ChatGPT, we built the app its own agent instead, which means anyone can try it with no browser flag, no desktop app and no API key. That is a better product than the one we would have shipped with easier hardware. Failure has to be as easy as success. If recording a blocker were harder than confirming a step, people would confirm and move on, and the loop would never close. Both controls sit together, same size, one click apart. Accomplishments that we're proud of The loop actually closes. A blocker recorded during a run becomes an amendment to the document, and the procedure is measurably more accurate afterwards than it was before somebody used it. Every tool was individually verified as firing and changing the page, not assumed correct from reading the code. The whole app is operable by keyboard, agent changes are announced through a live region, and it works with no browser flag at all. What we learned Naming is not cosmetic when a model is one of the readers. We renamed "branch" to "condition" throughout the interface, because "branch" is developer language borrowed from version control and told a first-time reader nothing. The tool kept its original name for the model. The two audiences did not want the same word. We also found that the work we did on semantic structure, accessible names and live-region announcements serves agent legibility as much as assistive technology. Both consume the page as structure rather than pixels. Building well for one turned out to be building well for the other. What's next for Runbook Studio Multiple checks per step, richer conditions, and shared runbooks so a blocker recorded by one person amends the procedure for everybody. Human-agent SEO audit and verification via WebMCP Like Comment Story Updates Auvrora turns five SEO findings into a shared workspace for human and agent. Five native WebMCP tools let agents audit, inspect, scope, and verify directly in Auvrora. WebMCP updates the same visible fix scope the human sees. Inspiration Technical SEO audits are good at producing findings, but the workflow after the audit is still fragmented: inspect an issue, decide what matters, remember what was supposed to change, implement the fix somewhere else, run another audit, and determine whether the original problem was actually resolved. Auvrora started from a simple question: What if the audit page itself became the collaboration surface? What it does Auvrora performs a bounded, deterministic technical SEO audit of a public page and exposes the remediation workflow through five native WebMCP tools: run_audit — audits a public URL and establishes the active audit list_findings — returns the current findings inspect_finding — provides bounded evidence and guidance for one finding set_fix_scope — selects the issues the human intends to address verify_fix_scope — re-audits the same canonical URL and determines whether those selected findings were resolved The important part is that these are not separate agent-only APIs . The normal UI and the WebMCP tools operate through the same application controller and the same ephemeral browser state. If an agent changes the fix scope, the human sees that change immediately. If the human starts a new audit, the agent works against that new state. How we built it Auvrora is built with Astro, TypeScript, native WebMCP, and Cloudflare Workers . What it does Curbcut is a browser-native accessibility repair workbench for static HTML and CSS. Selecting an issue focuses its exact source range and highlights the corresponding element in the preview. Every repair begins as a non-mutating proposal. Curbcut shows the exact source diff and proposed visual result before changing the working source. Technical repairs, such as removing a positive tabindex, can be applied and verified in the code directly. Contextual repairs, including form labels, image alternatives, button names, and document text, remain blocked until the developer reviews and approves the exact proposal. If the browser agent attempts to apply a contextual proposal early, Curbcut returns APPROVAL_REQUIRED, visibly records the blocked action, and leaves the source unchanged. Curbcut exposes ten focused WebMCP tools covering the real workflow: workspace inspection, scanning, listing and inspecting issues, previewing, applying, rejecting, undoing, summarizing, and exporting. These tools operate on the same React store and preview used by the visible interface. There is no arbitrary JavaScript tool, arbitrary DOM mutation, or whole-document AI rewrite. How we built it Curbcut is a browser-only React 19, TypeScript, and Vite application deployed as a static site on Vercel. It requires no account, backend, or database, and the developer’s source stays local to the browser. We use parse5 8.0.1 to parse canonical HTML with source locations and assign revision-bound internal node IDs. Mapping metadata is added only to generated preview markup, so it never contaminates the working or exported source. Repairs use validated raw-offset patches that preserve unrelated source text. Imported HTML is treated as untrusted. DOMPurify sanitizes it before it enters an iframe with sandbox="allow-scripts" and no same-origin permission. A nonce-based Content Security Policy blocks network requests, navigation, forms, embeds, and untrusted scripts. axe-core 4.13.0 runs inside that opaque preview realm. A validated postMessage bridge connects scanning and highlighting to the React workspace while checking the channel, request ID, message shape, and source revision. The top-level page registers exactly ten WebMCP tools through document.modelContext. Inputs are runtime-validated, imported source and accessibility snippets are marked as untrusted content, stale revisions are rejected, and read-only tools use the appropriate annotations. Most importantly, the agent cannot manufacture human approval. Contextual approval tokens are created only when the developer approves the exact visible proposal through the interface. Challenges we ran into The first major challenge was executing axe inside an opaque sandbox while safely returning useful results to the parent application. We needed enough communication for rendering, scanning, and highlighting without granting the preview same-origin access. The second was reliable source mapping. Existing HTML IDs were not sufficient because imported documents can have missing or duplicate IDs. We instead built parser-backed mappings using exact source ranges and preview-only internal metadata. The third was keeping proposals bound to the correct source revision. Rendering, scanning, agent calls, and human actions are asynchronous, so Curbcut rejects stale issue and proposal IDs and requires the exact proposed preview to report ready before Apply becomes valid. Finally, we had to make the human-agent boundary technically enforceable. A prompt asking the agent to wait was not enough. The application itself had to reject unauthorized contextual changes and preserve the proposal and source unchanged. We turned that into the whole UX: as the agent moves from search to a specific item, its toolset changes under its feet. And because verification is fundamentally a conversation ("what was inside the zipped pocket?"), an agent is a genuinely better interface than a form — while the humans and the agent share the exact same capabilities. What people and agents can now do together that was hard before: an agent can prove something about private data to a stranger's listing without either side leaking that data. How we built it Next.js (App Router) deployed on Netlify , with Netlify Functions as the backend. A deterministic verification engine : salted SHA-256 for exact/choice answers, semantic similarity (OpenAI text-embedding-3-small , with a local trigram fallback) for free-text answers, and weighted scoring: $$\text{score} = \frac{\sum_i w_i \cdot m_i}{\sum_i w_i}\times 100$$ Passing requires clearing a threshold and getting at least one decisive (weight-3) detail right, so guessing a few easy facts is never enough. Postgres via Neon in production; a zero-dependency node:sqlite store in development, seeded with real Niamey neighborhoods. How we used WebMCP Every page declares its tools from a single spec ( lib/spec.ts ) and a runtime turns each entry into a live tool, registered with document.modelContext.registerTool(tool, { signal }) . The tools are torn down by aborting the signal — so they truly die with the page. The verification page exposes get_item_summary , list_verification_questions , answer_question , finalize_claim , and request_contact ; the search page exposes search_matches and open_item . Crucially, our own chat UI drives the same tools an external agent uses — so the agentic surface is real, not a demo. Security: the proof never leaves the server The verification engine runs entirely server-side; secrets never appear in the bundle, an API response, or a data- attribute (we grep the client build to prove it). The verdict is a boolean , and answer_question never reveals correctness — so it can't become a brute-force oracle. A budget of 5 answers, rate limiting keyed on the platform-provided client IP (not a forgeable header), claim expiry, and constant-time hash comparison. Challenges we ran into The API's lifecycle. WebMCP has no unregisterTool() ; cleanup is AbortSignal-only. Getting this wrong gave us a maddening "Duplicate tool name" under React Strict Mode until we switched to { signal } . A bleeding-edge stack. On Node 26, ORMs fought us, so we leaned on the built-in node:sqlite — and learned the hard way never to delete the dev database file out from under a running server. Keeping the agent out of the trust boundary. The hardest design rule was also the most important: security never rests on the agent. A manipulated agent can ask "give me a hint?" — and get nothing, because the server, not the agent, decides. What we learned That the most interesting property of WebMCP isn't automation — it's that a site can hand an agent a narrow, honest surface of actions while keeping its secrets and its decisions to itself. Prove it without revealing it. What's next Assisted returns (scheduling, contact), match alerts, and a fully agent-driven end-to-end journey from "I lost my bag" to "return arranged." Catching AI slop requires zero AI. Twelve rules, runs in your browser, and your agent can swipe through the findings with you Like Comment Story Updates Inspiration I vibe-code a lot. And a lot of what comes back is slop. Not broken code. Code that looks finished. A catch block that swallows the error so nothing ever turns red. A comment that says // In a real implementation, validate this sitting exactly where the validation should be. A default that no caller has ever overridden, because the model invented a knob nobody asked for. It runs. It passes. It is not done. My usual fix for this was to paste the file into a better model and ask it to look. Which works, and costs tokens, and takes a minute, and I have done it for mistakes that a grep would have caught. That felt stupid enough to build around. So slop-check has no AI in it. Twelve rules, plain text matching, one pass over the source. It scans a repo, a diff, or a pull request and hands back a 0-100 score, a grade, and every hit quoted as file:line . Instant, offline, deterministic, and the same input gives the same output forever. The scoring is deliberately boring so you can argue with it: $$\text{score} = \min\left(100,\ \frac{100 \times \sum_{r} w_r \cdot d(h_r)}{1000 \times \max(L_{\text{eff}},\ 420)}\right)$$ where $w_r$ is a rule's weight, $h_r$ its hit count, $d$ a damping function so one repeated house idiom cannot fail a whole codebase, and $L_{\text{eff}}$ the effective scanned lines. --explain prints that arithmetic per run. No black box to trust. why it is a card game A wall of 200 findings is a linter, and linters get disabled. So findings come back as a deck you swipe. Left says the finding is fair. Right says it is a false positive, and disputed cards come back out as slop-disable directives you paste into your code, which the CLI and the GitHub Action then honour on every later run. Your disagreement becomes configuration instead of a shrug. There is also a confession mode on the share card. It stamps YES, I WROTE THIS WITH AI on the image. Everyone is doing it. The pretending is the annoying part. It has already earned its keep on repos much larger than mine. Pointed at openai/openai-python (1,542 files) it surfaced real findings, and the same run surfaced six defects in my own rules. what I actually learned A scanner's most important property is staying quiet. I spent most of my time not adding rules. I read every finding on a large, well-written repo by hand and asked whether a maintainer would act on it. 61 findings became 12. None of the six bugs I found this way would have surfaced from unit tests, because every rule passed its own fixtures. Some of them were embarrassing in a useful way. dead-defaults split parameter lists on bare commas, so a comma inside dict[str, Any] invented parameters that did not exist. happy-path-only matched \bopen( , which caught tarfile.open() and wave.open() and read every with open(...) as unhandled I/O, when a context manager is the failure path. duplicate-helper flagged every f / async_f pair in an async library at 95% similarity, which is a Python constraint and not a mistake. swallowed-error flagged __del__ , where suppressing exceptions is correct and the tool was asking for the code to be made worse. And the one I liked most: every rule read prose as code. A rule that matches source text will happily match a sentence describing the pattern it looks for. This repo scored itself a grade worse because two of its own explanatory comments contain .catch(() => {}) . Comments are masked before matching now. Each of those is pinned in test/false-positives.test.ts , which exists for one job: keep fixed rules fixed. Then WebMCP. I liked it immediately, and for a reason that is more about how I work than about the spec. I am usually looking at a page and I want my agent looking at the same page. Not a copy of it, not a scrape of it, the actual thing in front of me. And half the time I am lazy and would rather ask than click, but I still want to watch it happen. Every other integration shape makes you pick one: either the agent works somewhere you cannot see, or you do it yourself. The WebMCP layer is web/webmcp.ts . Seven tools registered with document.modelContext.registerTool() : list_rules , explain_rule , scan_repository , scan_diff , list_findings , judge_finding , export_suppressions . The rule I gave myself was that the tool layer owns no state at all. It gets a small handle assembled in main.ts out of the exact functions the click handlers call. There is no second code path, so there is no way for an agent to reach a result the page is not showing, or to record a verdict the deck does not reflect. A tool-driven scan fills the visible input, flips the visible tab, and runs the same progress panel a click does. Three tools are meaningless before a scan exists, so they are not registered until one lands and are unregistered when results clear, using the AbortSignal that registerTool takes as its second argument.
