=== RUBRIC (frozen) ===
# FROZEN SCORING RUBRIC (pre-registered before any Stage 1 review)

Scores are integers 1-10 on each of the FOUR official criteria. Every score carries:
rationale, evidence_surfaces, confidence (0-1). Reviewers use the full scale and these
band anchors verbatim. Category neutrality is absolute: a game, art piece, music tool,
CRM, weird toy, and developer tool are all eligible for 10/10 on every criterion if they
satisfy that criterion exceptionally well FOR WHAT THEY ARE TRYING TO BE.
No aesthetic penalties. Signs of hurried AI work matter only as actual product-quality
problems (incoherence, broken flow, placeholder content), never as style.

## WebMCP Leverage — what does WebMCP change for this product?
Judge the delta WebMCP makes to reliability, precision, structured state access,
shared human-agent state, repeatability, and capability. Tool COUNT is not leverage.
- 9-10 TRANSFORMATIVE: the intended experience is impractical without the structured
  agent surface; human and agent share live product state; the collaboration could not
  be replicated comparably well by a general agent driving the UI.
- 7-8 MAJOR DELTA: without WebMCP a general agent is markedly less reliable/precise
  here; the agent is central to the product, not a bolt-on.
- 5-6 MEANINGFUL DELTA: existing capability packaged into a genuinely better product
  experience via structured tools; same outcome otherwise possible with real friction.
- 3-4 MINOR DELTA: generic browser automation achieves comparable outcomes; WebMCP is
  a convenience layer.
- 1-2 COSMETIC: exposes an action a UI button already offers; no meaningful change.

## Execution — is the actual product coherent, intentional, complete for its scope?
Stage 1 scores EXECUTION_CLAIMED: coherence/intentionality/completeness as evidenced by
the packet (description + packaging + video frames/transcript). Thin evidence caps the
score (end-to-end credit requires end-to-end evidence) and lowers confidence.
Stage 2 may rescore as EXECUTION_OBSERVED; latest evidence-backed score replaces, never
sums.
- 9-10: all surfaces agree; central workflow demonstrably completes; states handled;
  feels finished for its stated scope.
- 7-8: strong coherence and intentionality; central workflow appears functional; minor
  unfinished edges.
- 5-6: plausibly real with visible gaps; scope unclear or core not demonstrated
  end-to-end.
- 3-4: thin prototype; contradictions between surfaces; placeholder content.
- 1-2: non-functional or incoherent for its claimed scope.

## Creativity & Ambition — novelty of concept and interaction model
Judge ONLY: novelty of the concept, novelty of the interaction model, originality
relative to existing concepts, ambition, memorable/surprising use of the medium, and
the depth with which the concept is pursued. WebMCP indispensability belongs under
WebMCP Leverage, NOT here: a project can be extremely creative even if another agent
API could theoretically have enabled something similar.
- 9-10: genuinely novel concept or interaction model; ambitious scope pursued deeply
  and coherently; memorable.
- 7-8: fresh synthesis or notably original interaction; ambitious for a hackathon.
- 5-6: competent take on a known category with a real new angle.
- 3-4: derivative; novelty mostly surface details.
- 1-2: clone/template with trivial changes.
"That the category exists" is never a penalty; judge the submitted concept.

## Potential Impact — credible value for the intended audience
Judge: is there a real audience; is there a real problem, desire, or use case for that
audience; does the demonstrated solution genuinely address it. NO commercial adoption,
monetization, market pull, or startup viability is required. Entertainment, play, art,
creative expression, learning, niche hobby use, experimentation, and community value
are legitimate forms of impact. A tiny audience fully justifies a top score.
- 9-10: specific real audience with a real problem/desire; solution demonstrably
  addresses it; credible value, appeal, usefulness, or meaning for that audience.
- 7-8: clear audience and problem, good fit, credible value.
- 5-6: identifiable audience, looser fit or partial address.
- 3-4: speculative audience or strained fit; impact mostly aspirational.
- 1-2: no coherent audience/problem pairing.
Audience size is NOT a multiplier.

## Aggregate & ranking
aggregate = leverage + execution + impact + creativity (4-40). Nothing else enters.
Tie-breaks: Leverage, then Execution, then Impact, then Creativity, then higher
combined evidence confidence, then slug alphabetical. See PROTOCOL.md Ranking section.

Like Comment Story Updates The consent gate: the agent asked for three passages, the reviewer chooses which cross. Registered tools listed on the right. Manuscript open: eight tools exposed. The reviewer's draft, written in-page and exported as Markdown. Home page where you can upload documents. Inspiration Peer reviewers are bound by confidentiality. ICMJE, Elsevier, COPE and NIH all restrict putting a manuscript under review into an AI service, because doing so transmits a confidential document to a third party. Reviewing is slow, detailed, unpaid work that AI is genuinely good at assisting with and reviewers are told they can't use it. That conflict has no resolution today. Reviewers either break confidentiality quietly, or do without. ReviewGate resolves it architecturally instead of by policy: the manuscript never leaves the browser, and the agent only ever sees what the reviewer explicitly hands it. What it does Open a manuscript in .txt, .md or PDF and it is parsed and stored entirely in your browser's IndexedDB. The page exposes eight WebMCP tools to your agent. Every tool that would return manuscript prose pauses and asks you first, showing exactly which sentences are about to cross, with per-passage checkboxes. You release some, withhold others, or deny outright. Every decision is written to an append-only disclosure log you can export as Markdown for the editor: which tool asked, why, how many passages were requested, released and withheld. "I used AI" stops being a confession and becomes an auditable statement. Why this had to be WebMCP Every other way of giving an agent access to a manuscript involves sending the manuscript somewhere. A server MCP has to transmit it. An API integration has to transmit it. Copy-paste transmits it. WebMCP tools execute in the page , in the reviewer's own session, on data that is already in the browser. The agent operates on a document that never moves. Rebuilt as a server MCP this project doesn't get worse but stops being possible. The architecture is the confidentiality argument. How I built it React 19, Vite 8, Tailwind 4, IndexedDB via idb , and a lazily-imported PDF.js for text extraction. No backend, by necessity rather than convenience. Design decisions worth naming: Consent is the return value, not a notification. execute() is async, so the tool simply doesn't resolve until the human decides. No browser confirmation API is needed which also means nothing breaks as the spec moves. A refusal reads as a refusal. Deny a request and the agent receives an explicit statement: "the reviewer declined to release any text; do not infer anything about the manuscript's content from this refusal." An agent that reads an empty result as absence of evidence would conclude a withheld section was blank. Verified live: it replies "I can't assess it yet: access to the Methods section was declined." Graduated disclosure. check_stats_reporting returns the numeric values reported in a paper without the sentences around them which provides a lower-disclosure path for a common review task. The tool surface follows application state. One tool is registered before a manuscript is open, eight after, one again on close. Standing grants are scoped to the sentence. "Release for session" never silently widens to neighbouring passages, and grants die with the manuscript. The claim is enforced, not promised. The deployed CSP sets connect-src 'none' , so the browser refuses every outbound request the page could make. A live counter in the header shows the count, and it reads zero. Challenges I ran into requestUserInteraction() doesn't exist. Several third-party guides document it; Chrome's shipped imperative API doesn't have it. The whole design rested on a human-in-the-loop pause. It turned out not to be needed at all ,an async execute() already makes the agent wait and owning the dialog means owning what it shows. Registering once is wrong. I checked for document.modelContext at mount. An embedding browser installs the API after first paint, so the check found nothing, registered nothing, and never retried, indistinguishable from the site not supporting WebMCP. It now watches for the API and registers when it appears. The sentence splitter broke on decimals. A naive regex split "p<0.05" into "(p<0" and "05).", showing reviewers meaningless fragments in the consent dialog. Embedded browsers block blob downloads silently. The export appeared to succeed while nothing reached the reviewer. Exports now render on screen with copy and download beside them. Real documents don't look like test fixtures. My heading detection only understood ALL-CAPS headings, so a report using "Chapter 3 : Problem Statement" parsed into six wrong sections with the title page promoted to structure. It now handles numbered, chapter and roman-numeral headings, drops contents-page entries, and filters repeated running headers. What I learned That a permission model is more interesting than a capability. WebMCP's obvious use is letting agents do more; the more interesting question is what it lets you safely refuse . And that the failure modes worth engineering against are semantic, not technical, an agent misreading silence as absence would have been far more damaging here than any crash. What's next Per-section rather than per-sentence grants for long manuscripts, a "what the agent has seen this session" view distinct from the chronological log, and redaction; releasing a sentence with figures or identifiers masked. Honest limitations Claim detection is regex pattern matching, not semantic analysis; the tool descriptions tell the agent so. The network counter is a demo instrument while the real guarantee is the CSP plus having no server to send anything to. No journal endorses this tool; it helps a reviewer comply with policy and documents what happened. Like Comment Story Updates The problem nobody has budget for Every operations team lives inside sites that will never ship an API for them. Monitoring in Grafana. Customer commitments in Cal.com. Payer portals, vendor consoles, admin dashboards. The person holding the session can see everything and change anything, but their agent can do nothing except screenshot the screen and guess where to click. WebMCP fixes this for sites that adopt it. Today that is roughly none of them. We measured three real open-source products, 522k lines of code, 429 places where a tool obviously belongs, and zero WebMCP registrations. So we asked a different question. What if the user adopts WebMCP for the site? The reframe WebMCP turns the login you already have into an API. A signed-in tab already carries every credential and permission the human has. If that tab registers tools with document.modelContext.registerTool , the agent gets a typed, narrow, inspectable interface to the site, and the vendor never has to ship a line of code. Adoption inverts. It no longer waits on ten thousand vendors. It happens one user, one tab, one reviewed pack at a time. What Agentready Network does Agentready Network is a local-first registry and runtime of reviewed action packs . A pack describes what a site can do, on exactly which origin, with which risk labels. The runtime turns that pack into live WebMCP tools inside the user's own signed-in session, then wraps every call in the safety the vendor never provided: Exact origins. A pack binds to one host and a reviewed fingerprint. Drift fails closed. Declarative, never executable. Manifests are JSON. The registry cannot ship code into your browser. Bounded, untrusted output. Results are allowlist-projected, byte-capped, and labeled contentTrust: "untrusted" . Tool output is content, not instruction. Human authority on consequences. Reads flow. Writes need a preview. Consequential actions need one exact, single-use approval bound to origin, tool, and normalized arguments. Change an argument and the grant is void. Verified closure. After a consequential action an inspector reads authoritative state and issues a receipt with no cookies, secrets, or raw bodies. Unknown outcome is reported as in_doubt , never as success. One concrete job, end to end The demo is not a feature tour. It is one incident. An alert fires in Grafana. Customer bookings in Cal.com are about to be affected. Neither site has WebMCP. Like Comment Story Updates Inspiration Group scheduling is a negotiation over everyone's constraints - and it's still done by making humans click checkboxes in polls. When WebMCP launched, we realized the missing piece was never a smarter poll: it was letting each person's AI agent join the negotiation itself . Quorum is our answer: scheduling, negotiated by your agent - approved by you. What it does Quorum is a meeting scheduler where humans and agents are equal participants in the same live session. Humans get a normal UI: candidate times, yes/maybe/no voting, counter-proposals. Agents get seven WebMCP tools registered by the page itself - they can read the event state, join on their human's behalf, vote with reasons, counter-propose new times, and rank the slots. Why this is a strong fit for WebMCP Scheduling is a multi-step, stateful interaction - exactly where screen-scraping agents fail. With WebMCP, the page tells agents what's possible via typed, self-describing tools. In testing, an agent that had never seen the app correctly chained four tools on the first try (read state -> join -> vote with a conflict note -> report the winner) purely from the tool descriptions. What people and agents can do together that wasn't possible before A mixed negotiation - some parties human, some agents - on the same page, in real time, with full mutual visibility. Before WebMCP, agents couldn't reliably operate voting UIs built for humans, and sites had no way to declare what actions they support. Quorum shows the new shape of the open web: agents handle the tedium, the audit trail keeps trust, and humans keep the final word. How we built it Vanilla JS frontend + Express backend, live-synced over server-sent events.
