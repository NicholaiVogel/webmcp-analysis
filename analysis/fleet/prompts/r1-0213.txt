# FROZEN SCORING RUBRIC (pre-registered before any Stage 1 review)

Scores are integers 1-10 on each of the FOUR official criteria. Every score carries:
rationale, evidence_surfaces, confidence (0-1). Reviewers use the full scale and these
band anchors verbatim. Category neutrality is absolute: a game, art piece, music tool,
CRM, weird toy, and developer tool are all eligible for 10/10 on every criterion if they
satisfy that criterion exceptionally well FOR WHAT THEY ARE TRYING TO BE.
No aesthetic penalties. Signs of hurried AI work matter only as actual product-quality
problems (incoherence, broken flow, placeholder content), never as style.

## WebMCP Leverage — what does WebMCP change for this product?
Judge the delta WebMCP makes to reliability, precision, structured state access,
shared human-agent state, repeatability, and capability. Tool COUNT is not leverage.
- 9-10 TRANSFORMATIVE: the intended experience is impractical without the structured
  agent surface; human and agent share live product state; the collaboration could not
  be replicated comparably well by a general agent driving the UI.
- 7-8 MAJOR DELTA: without WebMCP a general agent is markedly less reliable/precise
  here; the agent is central to the product, not a bolt-on.
- 5-6 MEANINGFUL DELTA: existing capability packaged into a genuinely better product
  experience via structured tools; same outcome otherwise possible with real friction.
- 3-4 MINOR DELTA: generic browser automation achieves comparable outcomes; WebMCP is
  a convenience layer.
- 1-2 COSMETIC: exposes an action a UI button already offers; no meaningful change.

## Execution — is the actual product coherent, intentional, complete for its scope?
Stage 1 scores EXECUTION_CLAIMED: coherence/intentionality/completeness as evidenced by
the packet (description + packaging + video frames/transcript). Thin evidence caps the
score (end-to-end credit requires end-to-end evidence) and lowers confidence.
Stage 2 may rescore as EXECUTION_OBSERVED; latest evidence-backed score replaces, never
sums.
- 9-10: all surfaces agree; central workflow demonstrably completes; states handled;
  feels finished for its stated scope.
- 7-8: strong coherence and intentionality; central workflow appears functional; minor
  unfinished edges.
- 5-6: plausibly real with visible gaps; scope unclear or core not demonstrated
  end-to-end.
- 3-4: thin prototype; contradictions between surfaces; placeholder content.
- 1-2: non-functional or incoherent for its claimed scope.

## Creativity & Ambition — novelty of concept and interaction model
Judge ONLY: novelty of the concept, novelty of the interaction model, originality
relative to existing concepts, ambition, memorable/surprising use of the medium, and
the depth with which the concept is pursued. WebMCP indispensability belongs under
WebMCP Leverage, NOT here: a project can be extremely creative even if another agent
API could theoretically have enabled something similar.
- 9-10: genuinely novel concept or interaction model; ambitious scope pursued deeply
  and coherently; memorable.
- 7-8: fresh synthesis or notably original interaction; ambitious for a hackathon.
- 5-6: competent take on a known category with a real new angle.
- 3-4: derivative; novelty mostly surface details.
- 1-2: clone/template with trivial changes.
"That the category exists" is never a penalty; judge the submitted concept.

## Potential Impact — credible value for the intended audience
Judge: is there a real audience; is there a real problem, desire, or use case for that
audience; does the demonstrated solution genuinely address it. NO commercial adoption,
monetization, market pull, or startup viability is required. Entertainment, play, art,
creative expression, learning, niche hobby use, experimentation, and community value
are legitimate forms of impact. A tiny audience fully justifies a top score.
- 9-10: specific real audience with a real problem/desire; solution demonstrably
  addresses it; credible value, appeal, usefulness, or meaning for that audience.
- 7-8: clear audience and problem, good fit, credible value.
- 5-6: identifiable audience, looser fit or partial address.
- 3-4: speculative audience or strained fit; impact mostly aspirational.
- 1-2: no coherent audience/problem pairing.
Audience size is NOT a multiplier.

## Aggregate & ranking
aggregate = leverage + execution + impact + creativity (4-40). Nothing else enters.
Tie-breaks: Leverage, then Execution, then Impact, then Creativity, then higher
combined evidence confidence, then slug alphabetical. See PROTOCOL.md Ranking section.

Your AI agent reviews an employment contract through tools the page registers in your browser and the blurred document snaps into clarity as you decide each redline. Like Comment Story Updates Inspiration You get an offer. The salary is great — and the contract is 22 pages of fine print with a 48-hour signature deadline. About 30% of US workers are bound by non-competes, and most people sign without understanding what they're signing. A lawyer costs $400/hour. The alternative used to be asking an AI "is this enforceable?" and getting a confident essay you then had to reconstruct into something actionable. Redline is for the person who actually has: a document, a deadline, and an AI agent they already use. What it does Paste an employment contract (or open one of three deliberately overreaching samples) and it loads blurred — the way a 22-page contract actually feels. Your AI agent then works inside the page: it reads every clause, flags the risky ones with severity and grounded reasons, and proposes concrete redline language. Each proposal lands as a decision card under the exact sentence it modifies. You decide each one — Keep as-is, or Redline it — and accepted changes roll up into a negotiation email, Current vs Proposed side by side, one click to copy. As the agent assesses each clause, it snaps into clarity — but clarity is computed, not decorative: a clause only clears once it's assessed and every redline on it is decided. Reset a verdict and it re-flags. The blur is the progress bar. Why this is a strong fit for WebMCP A contract-review tool needs an agent to see inside the document, clause by clause, and write back into it. That is exactly what WebMCP enables: Redline registers seven tools on document.modelContext — get_sample_contract , get_document_state , extract_clauses , get_clause_text , assess_clause , get_enforceability_context , export_negotiation_email . Two-phase dynamic registration: at boot only a get_sample_contract tool exists; once a document is loaded, the app retires it via WebMCP's signal-based lifecycle and registers the six analysis tools. The tool list always matches what is meaningful right now. Grounded analysis, not vibes: get_enforceability_context hands the agent real data — non-compete enforceability across 20 US states (earnings thresholds, void-by-default rules, notice requirements), training-repayment-agreement traps in 5 states, and heuristic families for NDAs, arbitration, and IP assignment. When Redline says a five-year worldwide non-compete is high-severity in Washington, it quotes RCW 49.62. No API keys, no backend, no scraping. The page is the tool provider and the browser is the whole stack (static assets on Cloudflare Workers). What people and agents can do together now The old pattern: ask an AI, get an essay, copy fragments into a text editor, lose the structure. Here the agent proposes and the human decides — and the boundary is enforced by the data flow itself: agent tools write proposals and assessments into clause threads, but redline verdicts can only come from the UI. What was six screens of copy-paste is now one grounded pass with the human keeping the pen on every change. Because all state is derived (blur, completion, the email draft are computed from assessments and verdicts — never stored as flags), the UI can't claim progress the human didn't actually make. How we built it React 19 + TypeScript (strict), Vite, Cloudflare Workers static assets — no backend, no keys. All tool reads go through a live session ref (tools never read stale React state); writes flow actions → store → UI in one direction. The email composer is deterministic: the page and export_negotiation_email emit byte-identical output. The decision UI (FlagBadge, RedlineCard, ClauseChat) renders proposals inline in the document; a 500ms transition snaps each clause from blurred to clear as decisions land. Challenges we ran into WebMCP's registration lifecycle has no unregister API — tools are retired through abort signals, which shaped our two-phase registration design. Making blur honest took a derived rule: early versions cleared clauses on assessment alone; the shipped version requires every redline on the clause to be decided. Keeping page and tool output identical forced us to make the email composer a pure function both paths call. Live agent testing caught real bugs (duplicate redline proposals on re-assessment, chat auto-expand behavior) that we fixed with a pure redline-planning function and better thread state. What we learned The WebMCP model inverts the usual architecture: the app doesn't call the model, the agent calls the app. Designing tools for another developer (the agent platform) forced us to make state readable, actions explicit, and UI claims derived from data — which made the app better for humans too. What's honest about this demo The samples are drafted overbroad — that's the test. But nothing is scripted: the app ships data and heuristics, not verdicts, and accepts whatever a competent model actually produces through the tools. Different model, different analysis — same structure to hold it. ABOUT TEXT (untrusted evidence):
The visual website builder where humans and AI agents collaboratively create, edit, validate, and publish agent-ready websites through WebMCP. Like Comment Story Updates demo vedio : https://drive.google.com/drive/folders/1OA56ZB6VfMFG1dIzqcJiOLojGptgHV85?usp=sharing Inspiration The web was built for humans, but the next generation of the web will also be used by AI agents. We were inspired by a simple question: What if an AI agent could work inside the same visual workspace as a human, instead of trying to control a website by guessing clicks, reading screenshots, or manipulating the DOM? Existing AI website builders mostly follow a prompt → generate → review workflow. We wanted something more collaborative. That led us to WEAVE : a visual website editor where humans and AI agents can work on the same live website together. Humans provide creative direction and visual judgment, while agents handle structured editing and repetitive work through WebMCP. Our goal was to explore what an agent-native creative tool could look like. What it does WEAVE is a collaborative, agent-native website builder. A human can visually create and edit a website using the canvas, while an AI agent can understand the same project and make changes through structured WebMCP tools. Instead of asking an agent to "click this button" or figure out a website from screenshots, WEAVE exposes meaningful capabilities such as: weave_get_context — understand the current project, page, sections, elements, and selection weave_get_selection — understand the element currently selected by the human weave_add_section — add a new website section weave_update_element — modify content or properties of an element weave_move_element — reorganize elements weave_delete_element — remove elements weave_publish_site — request publication of the website This allows an agent to perform workflows such as: "Add a pricing section below the testimonials, change the hero headline, and move the CTA above the footer." The agent first reads the structured state of the editor, chooses the appropriate WebMCP tools, and performs the requested mutations. Most importantly, human and agent edits go through the same validated mutation pipeline , so the agent is not given a separate backdoor into the editor. For high-consequence actions such as publishing, WEAVE keeps the human in control. The agent can request publication, but the final action requires human approval. How we built it WEAVE is built around a structured representation of the visual editor. Instead of exposing the DOM or screenshots to the agent, we maintain a semantic CanvasNode model representing the pages, sections, elements, content, and relationships inside the project. We then expose this model and the editor's capabilities through WebMCP. The architecture is roughly: AI Agent → WebMCP → WEAVE tools → validation → mutation queue → editor/source → live canvas The WebMCP layer registers WEAVE's tools through the browser's model context interface when available. For read operations, the agent can request a bounded snapshot of the current editor state. For write operations, tool inputs are validated before being passed into the existing mutation system. This gives us one important property: Human edits and agent edits use the same underlying mutation pipeline. We also maintain a context version so that the agent can detect when the editor has changed and retrieve fresh context rather than blindly acting on stale information. The project is built with a TypeScript/React-based web stack and integrates WebMCP directly into the browser application. Challenges we ran into Making agent actions reliable We did not want the agent to manipulate the UI through arbitrary clicks or DOM operations. We needed a structured representation that an agent could understand reliably. This led us to build the CanvasNode representation and expose semantic operations instead of low-level browser actions. Keeping human and agent edits consistent A major challenge was ensuring that an agent edit behaved exactly like a human edit. We solved this by routing agent mutations through the same validated mutation queue and editor pipeline rather than creating a separate agent-only editing path. Handling stale context A human can modify the canvas while an agent is reasoning about a previous state. To address this, WEAVE tracks a contextVersion and allows the agent to retrieve the latest structured context before performing subsequent operations. Safety and human control Giving an agent the ability to modify a website also creates the possibility of unintended destructive actions. We therefore separated read, write, destructive, and high-consequence capabilities and kept publication behind an explicit human approval step. Accomplishments that we're proud of We are most proud that WebMCP is not just an additional feature in WEAVE — it is part of the core interaction model. We built a system where: Agents can understand a structured visual website rather than relying on screenshots. Agents can perform meaningful website operations through typed WebMCP tools. Human and agent changes use the same mutation pipeline. The agent can react to the human's current selection and project state. Stale context can be detected and refreshed. Destructive and high-consequence operations are handled with additional safety. Publishing remains under human control. WebMCP activity can be observed as part of the editor workflow. We are also proud of turning a traditional visual website editor into something that can be treated as an agent-operable creative workspace . What we learned We learned that making a website "AI-enabled" is very different from making it agent-native . An agent does not necessarily need more UI access. It needs better capabilities and context . Instead of exposing hundreds of low-level browser interactions, a small set of well-designed semantic tools can give an agent much more reliable control. We also learned that human-agent collaboration works best when both sides share the same source of truth. The human should not have to work around the agent, and the agent should not need to simulate a human. WebMCP gave us a way to experiment with this model directly inside the web application. What's next for WEAVE Our next goal is to make WEAVE a broader platform for agent-native websites and creative applications . We want to explore: More powerful agent workflows for complete website creation Better multi-step planning and execution Richer structured context for complex projects More granular human approval and collaboration controls Version history and agent action rollback Multiple specialized agents working on different parts of a project Exporting websites with their capabilities and agent-facing metadata Making published WEAVE websites themselves more discoverable and usable by agents Ultimately, we want WEAVE to explore a future where websites are not just pages that agents can read, but interactive environments with structured capabilities that agents can understand and use alongside humans.
