# FROZEN SCORING RUBRIC (pre-registered before any Stage 1 review)

Scores are integers 1-10 on each of the FOUR official criteria. Every score carries:
rationale, evidence_surfaces, confidence (0-1). Reviewers use the full scale and these
band anchors verbatim. Category neutrality is absolute: a game, art piece, music tool,
CRM, weird toy, and developer tool are all eligible for 10/10 on every criterion if they
satisfy that criterion exceptionally well FOR WHAT THEY ARE TRYING TO BE.
No aesthetic penalties. Signs of hurried AI work matter only as actual product-quality
problems (incoherence, broken flow, placeholder content), never as style.

## WebMCP Leverage — what does WebMCP change for this product?
Judge the delta WebMCP makes to reliability, precision, structured state access,
shared human-agent state, repeatability, and capability. Tool COUNT is not leverage.
- 9-10 TRANSFORMATIVE: the intended experience is impractical without the structured
  agent surface; human and agent share live product state; the collaboration could not
  be replicated comparably well by a general agent driving the UI.
- 7-8 MAJOR DELTA: without WebMCP a general agent is markedly less reliable/precise
  here; the agent is central to the product, not a bolt-on.
- 5-6 MEANINGFUL DELTA: existing capability packaged into a genuinely better product
  experience via structured tools; same outcome otherwise possible with real friction.
- 3-4 MINOR DELTA: generic browser automation achieves comparable outcomes; WebMCP is
  a convenience layer.
- 1-2 COSMETIC: exposes an action a UI button already offers; no meaningful change.

## Execution — is the actual product coherent, intentional, complete for its scope?
Stage 1 scores EXECUTION_CLAIMED: coherence/intentionality/completeness as evidenced by
the packet (description + packaging + video frames/transcript). Thin evidence caps the
score (end-to-end credit requires end-to-end evidence) and lowers confidence.
Stage 2 may rescore as EXECUTION_OBSERVED; latest evidence-backed score replaces, never
sums.
- 9-10: all surfaces agree; central workflow demonstrably completes; states handled;
  feels finished for its stated scope.
- 7-8: strong coherence and intentionality; central workflow appears functional; minor
  unfinished edges.
- 5-6: plausibly real with visible gaps; scope unclear or core not demonstrated
  end-to-end.
- 3-4: thin prototype; contradictions between surfaces; placeholder content.
- 1-2: non-functional or incoherent for its claimed scope.

## Creativity & Ambition — novelty of concept and interaction model
Judge ONLY: novelty of the concept, novelty of the interaction model, originality
relative to existing concepts, ambition, memorable/surprising use of the medium, and
the depth with which the concept is pursued. WebMCP indispensability belongs under
WebMCP Leverage, NOT here: a project can be extremely creative even if another agent
API could theoretically have enabled something similar.
- 9-10: genuinely novel concept or interaction model; ambitious scope pursued deeply
  and coherently; memorable.
- 7-8: fresh synthesis or notably original interaction; ambitious for a hackathon.
- 5-6: competent take on a known category with a real new angle.
- 3-4: derivative; novelty mostly surface details.
- 1-2: clone/template with trivial changes.
"That the category exists" is never a penalty; judge the submitted concept.

## Potential Impact — credible value for the intended audience
Judge: is there a real audience; is there a real problem, desire, or use case for that
audience; does the demonstrated solution genuinely address it. NO commercial adoption,
monetization, market pull, or startup viability is required. Entertainment, play, art,
creative expression, learning, niche hobby use, experimentation, and community value
are legitimate forms of impact. A tiny audience fully justifies a top score.
- 9-10: specific real audience with a real problem/desire; solution demonstrably
  addresses it; credible value, appeal, usefulness, or meaning for that audience.
- 7-8: clear audience and problem, good fit, credible value.
- 5-6: identifiable audience, looser fit or partial address.
- 3-4: speculative audience or strained fit; impact mostly aspirational.
- 1-2: no coherent audience/problem pairing.
Audience size is NOT a multiplier.

## Aggregate & ranking
aggregate = leverage + execution + impact + creativity (4-40). Nothing else enters.
Tie-breaks: Leverage, then Execution, then Impact, then Creativity, then higher
combined evidence confidence, then slug alphabetical. See PROTOCOL.md Ranking section.

One verified chessboard. Two native interfaces. Humans and AI agents share every turn through WebMCP. Like Comment Story Updates Opening Board Human-agent exchange WebMCP explainer Legal-move guidance Why I built it I wanted to test WebMCP on a task where success could not be hidden behind a convincing-looking interface. Chess is a good test because every action has an objectively correct result. An agent must know where every piece is, whose turn it is, which side it controls and which moves are legal. Misreading one square can invalidate the entire game. A normal chess website is designed for human eyes and a mouse. An agent using that interface must reconstruct 64 squares from pixels and translate its move back into screen coordinates. That is fragile and unnecessary—the page already knows the exact position and legal actions. Obsidian Chess explores a different model: give the person and the agent interfaces designed for their individual strengths while connecting both to the same game. What it does The person plays through a visual chessboard. The agent plays through four page-defined WebMCP tools. For example, the person can select Take White and move the pawn from e2 to e4 . An agent waiting through wait_for_turn wakes up and receives the updated FEN, move history, role ownership, game status and exact legal moves. The agent selects one of those moves and calls move_piece , optionally adding an explanation such as “I’m contesting the centre.” The move immediately appears on the board. The SAN history, FEN, turn indicator and Human + Agent Trace all update from the same position. This is a complete playable experience rather than a mocked tool demo. A person can claim either colour, full chess rules are enforced, games can be reset, promotions are supported and invalid actions recover safely. The board works with a mouse, keyboard or touch. Why WebMCP is a strong fit Most agent tools perform one action and finish. A chess match is different: it is a long-running collaboration where control alternates between a person and an agent. A person might think for five seconds or five minutes. Without WebMCP, the agent must repeatedly inspect the screen and guess whether anything changed. With wait_for_turn , it can wait, wake when the person moves and receive the new authoritative state. WebMCP also lets the application expose precise capabilities without giving up control of its rules. The agent can inspect the position and submit a move, but the page still verifies side ownership, turn ownership and chess legality before changing anything. If the agent acts out of turn, submits an illegal move or moves before the person has chosen a side, the request is rejected without changing the board. The response includes a stable error code, the unchanged state and a specific recovery instruction. The person keeps the expressive interface that makes chess understandable. The agent receives structured state, exact legal actions and explicit permissions. Neither participant operates on a copy. Who this helps Obsidian Chess is immediately a playable human-agent game, but it is also a reference for developers building applications where people and agents share responsibility. The same pattern appears in purchase approvals, document reviews, scheduling negotiations, incident response and simulations. A person reviews information and makes a decision; the agent waits, observes the resulting state and continues from an explicitly permitted set of actions. These workflows often rely on screen interpretation, repeated polling or separate custom integrations. Obsidian Chess demonstrates another approach: the website exposes its existing state and domain actions directly while retaining control over validation and permissions. Chess makes this pattern easy to verify. A move is legal or illegal, turn ownership is unambiguous and every transition can be compared with the visible board. This is also what makes the project different from a typical AI chess demo. It does not add a chatbot beside a board or connect a hidden chess engine. The agent discovers the page’s tools, reads the shared state, chooses an action, explains it and waits for the person. How I implemented WebMCP The application is built with React and Vinext. chess.js is the authority for legal moves, check, checkmate, draws, castling, en passant and promotion. The page registers four tools through the browser’s model context: join_game announces the agent and returns the collaboration contract. get_board returns the authoritative position, ownership, history and legal actions. move_piece submits one schema-checked, turn-gated and rules-validated move. wait_for_turn waits for the agent to gain control of the next turn. Every response includes a protocol version, success state, stable status code, suggested next action and current game state. This gives the agent enough information to continue or recover without guessing. The waiting system uses a bounded long poll tied to a session identifier and state version. Duplicate waits are rejected, and resetting the game invalidates older waits so a stale call cannot act inside a new match. The visible Human + Agent Trace shows tool discovery, board inspection, waiting, moves, resets and errors. I also published evaluation.json and llms.txt guides so automated evaluators can follow a deterministic testing path. Challenges and lessons The hardest part was keeping two different interfaces synchronized across human thinking time. An early bug allowed the person to claim White but left pawn interaction in the wrong state. Fixing it required treating side ownership, turn ownership and the chess.js position as one transition instead of separate UI events. I added regression tests for opening pawn moves with humans playing either colour, alongside tests for wrong turns, promotions, castling, en passant, checkmate and resets. Reset behaviour was another subtle problem. A waiting call started during one match must never wake up and act in a different match. Session and version checks now return an explicit reset result to stale calls. I also learned that agent interfaces must explain failure as carefully as success. Returning only “illegal move” leaves the agent guessing. Returning ILLEGAL_MOVE , the unchanged position and a specific next action lets it recover safely. The result is one person, one agent and two purpose-built interfaces sharing one precise, verifiable game. A GitHub for molecular-dynamics runs: every number traces to a file, and your agent gets seventeen calibrated scientific operations through WebMCP. The agent reasons, Runcard checks, a person approves Like Comment Story Updates Main page System 1 System 2 Inspiration I had somehow come across the field of Molecular Dynamics through a summer project, and gained experience with AMBER through building an LLM orchestration engine around it. The biggest thing on my mind was just the lack of guardrails that this software had. It was like working with Assembly. It got me thinking a lot more about the experiences of those working in MD, and how I can build software that lowers the barrier to entry. What it does Sharing a MD simulation with someone usually means just giving your entire directory. I guess it works, but it's very hard to decode, and with hundreds of literal text files as the input, it's just not a very friendly way to share results. Runcard is essentially GitHub for MD simulations. A prepared system is like a repo, a run is like a commit, and users can "fork" runs, adding their own changes to someone else's work. Runcard turns your MD directory into a shareable, validated record, with analysis tools not just invocable by users, but directly invocable by agents through WebMCP. How we built it I was already quite familiar with building AMBER validators, and I used that knowledge to process .in files to ensure that the records were physically sound. That's the "validated record" part of things. I knew along the way that I wanted to do something analogous to Github, which helped with a lot of design work. If a run was a commit, and a rerun was a fork, many more things became clear. Proposals became like pull requests, which had to be approved by the user. Primarily, I had to work on creating functions that converted the raw files into the data presented in the website, and the analysis tools that drove both user/agent interaction. One table drives both users and agent interactions, so both sides are getting the exact same functions. At this point, most of the work was done, the rest just went into presenting it in a way that was intuitive to the user. Challenges we ran into Some of the things we have agents watch out for (like assuming spread as error) were lessons I had learned when generating the bundles in the first place. Some of the runs were with different engines (PMEMD vs Sander), and also had different total times (2 ps vs 30 ps) yet we treated them the same. AMBER is just very tricky to get right, so many of the challenges we had, despite my previous experience, were with honest our results were. Accomplishments that we're proud of To be honest, I'm just proud that I was able to finish this as a final product. Getting to the finish line means a lot to me, since I've never done hackathons all the way through before. On the product side, I was really proud of how the agent performed given these tools. I first asked it to check the math of a calculation with the tools, and it insisted on refusing to give an error score, because only 2 of 3 matched runs exist. Then, I asked it after to consider the system with 310K temperature instead of 300. It not only did that experiment correctly, but noticed that the missing 3rd matched run in the original run was also missing in this 310K run, and automatically proposed a fix. The tools made this possible, and agents kind of did the rest, which was cool to see What we learned I think I've reinforced the idea that the biggest failure modes are silent. Things that are confidently wrong. Both in AI's hallucinating, but also in molecular dynamics where things look ok, but are secretly messed up on the inside. What's next for Runcard I think the obvious answer is to a second person's runs onto the website. If we're really going to be the Github of MD, then we need collaboration and people. I want people to be able to share their results, and iterate on each other's work with much more ease.
