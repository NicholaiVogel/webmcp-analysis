# FROZEN SCORING RUBRIC (pre-registered before any Stage 1 review)

Scores are integers 1-10 on each of the FOUR official criteria. Every score carries:
rationale, evidence_surfaces, confidence (0-1). Reviewers use the full scale and these
band anchors verbatim. Category neutrality is absolute: a game, art piece, music tool,
CRM, weird toy, and developer tool are all eligible for 10/10 on every criterion if they
satisfy that criterion exceptionally well FOR WHAT THEY ARE TRYING TO BE.
No aesthetic penalties. Signs of hurried AI work matter only as actual product-quality
problems (incoherence, broken flow, placeholder content), never as style.

## WebMCP Leverage — what does WebMCP change for this product?
Judge the delta WebMCP makes to reliability, precision, structured state access,
shared human-agent state, repeatability, and capability. Tool COUNT is not leverage.
- 9-10 TRANSFORMATIVE: the intended experience is impractical without the structured
  agent surface; human and agent share live product state; the collaboration could not
  be replicated comparably well by a general agent driving the UI.
- 7-8 MAJOR DELTA: without WebMCP a general agent is markedly less reliable/precise
  here; the agent is central to the product, not a bolt-on.
- 5-6 MEANINGFUL DELTA: existing capability packaged into a genuinely better product
  experience via structured tools; same outcome otherwise possible with real friction.
- 3-4 MINOR DELTA: generic browser automation achieves comparable outcomes; WebMCP is
  a convenience layer.
- 1-2 COSMETIC: exposes an action a UI button already offers; no meaningful change.

## Execution — is the actual product coherent, intentional, complete for its scope?
Stage 1 scores EXECUTION_CLAIMED: coherence/intentionality/completeness as evidenced by
the packet (description + packaging + video frames/transcript). Thin evidence caps the
score (end-to-end credit requires end-to-end evidence) and lowers confidence.
Stage 2 may rescore as EXECUTION_OBSERVED; latest evidence-backed score replaces, never
sums.
- 9-10: all surfaces agree; central workflow demonstrably completes; states handled;
  feels finished for its stated scope.
- 7-8: strong coherence and intentionality; central workflow appears functional; minor
  unfinished edges.
- 5-6: plausibly real with visible gaps; scope unclear or core not demonstrated
  end-to-end.
- 3-4: thin prototype; contradictions between surfaces; placeholder content.
- 1-2: non-functional or incoherent for its claimed scope.

## Creativity & Ambition — novelty of concept and interaction model
Judge ONLY: novelty of the concept, novelty of the interaction model, originality
relative to existing concepts, ambition, memorable/surprising use of the medium, and
the depth with which the concept is pursued. WebMCP indispensability belongs under
WebMCP Leverage, NOT here: a project can be extremely creative even if another agent
API could theoretically have enabled something similar.
- 9-10: genuinely novel concept or interaction model; ambitious scope pursued deeply
  and coherently; memorable.
- 7-8: fresh synthesis or notably original interaction; ambitious for a hackathon.
- 5-6: competent take on a known category with a real new angle.
- 3-4: derivative; novelty mostly surface details.
- 1-2: clone/template with trivial changes.
"That the category exists" is never a penalty; judge the submitted concept.

## Potential Impact — credible value for the intended audience
Judge: is there a real audience; is there a real problem, desire, or use case for that
audience; does the demonstrated solution genuinely address it. NO commercial adoption,
monetization, market pull, or startup viability is required. Entertainment, play, art,
creative expression, learning, niche hobby use, experimentation, and community value
are legitimate forms of impact. A tiny audience fully justifies a top score.
- 9-10: specific real audience with a real problem/desire; solution demonstrably
  addresses it; credible value, appeal, usefulness, or meaning for that audience.
- 7-8: clear audience and problem, good fit, credible value.
- 5-6: identifiable audience, looser fit or partial address.
- 3-4: speculative audience or strained fit; impact mostly aspirational.
- 1-2: no coherent audience/problem pairing.
Audience size is NOT a multiplier.

## Aggregate & ranking
aggregate = leverage + execution + impact + creativity (4-40). Nothing else enters.
Tie-breaks: Leverage, then Execution, then Impact, then Creativity, then higher
combined evidence confidence, then slug alphabetical. See PROTOCOL.md Ranking section.

Switchboard Zero is a deterministic crisis-training sandbox for one difficult agent-design problem: how can a person delegate meaningful work without silently giving up authority? Inspiration The idea came from a question I kept returning to while shaping the project: if an agent can reset the exercise, change the policy, or approve its own exception, is the human actually in control? That pushed me away from a conventional assistant demo and toward a simulation where authority is enforced by the product, not merely promised in a prompt. The power grid is fictional, but the design problem is not. Any team testing agents in operations, security, or other high-stakes workflows needs to know not only whether an agent can complete a task, but whether it stops at the right moment. What it does Switchboard Zero places a human operator and a browser agent in the same fictional municipal-grid emergency. They have eight simulated minutes before a damaged feeder threatens the Hospital supply. The agent can inspect the incident, forecast the cost of delay, simulate recovery strategies, and execute actions that remain inside the operator's policy. When the next dependent action would disconnect a protected service or exceed the automatic risk ceiling, execution stops. The agent can explain the exact trade-off and place it in a visible decision queue, but only the human operator can authorize or reject it. The interface, Site Tools, policy controls, audit trail, and final debrief share one state. Agent actions are immediately visible to the person, and human decisions return to the same recovery plan. Why WebMCP This use case needs more than an agent clicking buttons or interpreting pixels. WebMCP gives the site a structured capability surface while preserving a fully usable human interface. Switchboard Zero exposes five Site Tools: inspect_grid reads the complete current state and authority boundary. simulate_delay_impact forecasts waiting without advancing the live exercise. simulate_recovery_plan creates a visible plan without applying switches. apply_authorized_actions executes in dependency order and stops at the first policy boundary. request_human_decision stages only the action that was actually withheld; it never approves or executes it. This makes the collaboration explicit: the agent is fast inside the boundary, while the person remains responsible for moving that boundary. Without a structured capability surface, this interaction would depend on fragile UI automation or split the agent, policy controls, and human decision into separate interfaces. How we built it The application is a browser-only React 19 and TypeScript project built with Vite. A deterministic domain engine models district service, generation, faults, recovery plans, policy locks, risk ceilings, approvals, dependency ordering, outcomes, and scoring. The five tools are registered with document.modelContext.registerTool . Read-only tools declare readOnlyHint ; state-changing tools commit to the same React state used by the visible control room. There is no backend, external runtime API, analytics, or connection to real infrastructure. Vitest covers 22 automated checks: 14 deterministic domain cases and 8 direct WebMCP registration and contract cases. GitHub Actions runs the complete test suite and production build, and GitHub Pages hosts the public app. Challenges The hardest problem was not generating a successful recovery plan. It was making the authority boundary real rather than decorative. Adversarial testing exposed a concrete failure mode: after a human-authorized transfer, changing recovery strategy could allow the strategy label to determine the outcome and leave Transit unrestored. I fixed it by deriving completion from the actual grid state, preserving the remaining recovery action, and adding a regression test for that sequence. Execution must never skip past a blocked dependency. A decision request must refer to the exact action previously withheld. Forecasting delay must not mutate the live clock. Approval, rejection, policy changes, time advancement, and exercise restart must remain human-only controls. Each rule is enforced in the domain model and reflected in the UI. A second challenge was keeping the agent and operator synchronized. Every simulation, action, approval, metric, and outcome had to update one shared state and one chronological decision trace. Trust boundary Switchboard Zero enforces an application-level delegation boundary: the agent receives a narrower capability surface than the human interface, and every state-changing tool is checked against the deterministic domain model. This demonstrates authority-aware tool design, but it is not presented as a production security boundary against hostile JavaScript, a compromised browser extension, or direct manipulation of client-side state. The current release has no backend, authentication, secrets, external content, analytics, or connection to real infrastructure. A production adaptation would require server-side authorization independent of the model, authenticated operators, durable tamper-evident audit records, replay-resistant action requests, and strict validation and isolation of external content. What we learned I learned that the hardest part of tool design is deciding what not to expose. A control labelled "human only" means very little if the same action is still available through an agent tool. That is why policy changes, approval, rejection, live time advancement, and exercise restart are absent from the WebMCP surface and enforced separately in the domain engine. I also learned that a human handoff is not just a confirmation dialog. It has to preserve the exact blocked action, explain its consequence, and return the decision to the plan the agent was already executing. The same interaction pattern could be used to train operators, compare whether different agents stop at the correct authority boundary, and replay policy regressions after model, prompt, or tool changes. The transferable result is not the fictional power grid; it is a visible and testable human-supervision pattern. This release proves that pattern in one deterministic scenario. It does not claim real-grid readiness or validation with real operators. What's next The current release proves the interaction model in one deterministic scenario. The next step is a scenario-authoring format and an evaluation suite for comparing agent behavior across different policies, time pressure, and human decisions. Our starting point was Crosspoint's existing exercise library and home-exercise product concepts. We saw an opportunity to connect two recurring tasks: preparing a clear program before the patient leaves, and making sense of their practice afterward. CoachPoint asks a practical question: What if an agent could handle more of the preparation and follow-up work, while the therapist kept every prescription decision? What it does CoachPoint connects a therapist, a patient, and a browser agent through one home-exercise workflow. The therapist creates the plan. Through WebMCP, the agent uses the curated movement library and its safety notes to assemble a visible, editable draft. We also want to measure preparation time and follow-up usefulness in practice before claiming productivity or clinical gains. Less preparation work. More informed conversations. Clinical decisions stay human. Like Comment Story Updates Why I made this I am a mathematician, and the example project in the app comes from an actual problem I have been working on: the $N$-commutator problem for derivations, with the expected threshold $$ N=n^2+2n-2. $$ The project has accumulated many possible approaches: tree cancellation, representation theory, computations for small $N$, path decompositions, and threshold arguments. Some approaches overlap, some depend on unproved intermediate claims, and some looked promising before running into an obstruction. Keeping all of this in my head became difficult. A paper only presents the final linear argument. Ordinary notes record pieces of the process, but they do not make the relationships between those pieces easy to see. Chat conversations have the same problem: an agent can produce a useful observation, but it usually remains somewhere inside a long transcript. That was the starting point for Research Blackboard. How the idea changed The first version of the idea was much larger. I imagined groups of agents with roles, communication channels, and assigned research directions. We tested that idea and found no clear evidence that a complicated multi-agent system reasoned better than one capable agent using its own tools and subagents. It was also adding a lot of interface without solving the central problem. So I removed it. The final project does not try to run agents. It gives an agent a structured place to work. Ideas, lemmas, computations, obstructions, resources, and tasks live on a persistent map. An agent can read the relevant part of that map through WebMCP and add useful results back to it. Building it with Codex I built the project together with Codex. I made the product and research-workflow decisions, while Codex helped implement the application, debug it, write regression tests, and deploy successive versions. The process was very iterative. I would test the live site and notice something concrete: notes behaved strangely while dragging, relations were hard to read, the canvas could not move far enough left, the inspector occupied too much space, or an agent could edit a project but could not create or navigate between projects. Codex would inspect the implementation, make the change, test it, and deploy a new version. This was also useful for rejecting bad features. At one stage the application had agent groups, roles, activity feeds, and communication ideas. They made the product look more “agentic,” but they did not make it more useful. Most of them were removed. Technically, the application uses TypeScript, React, Vinext, Vite, and React Flow. It runs as a Cloudflare Worker. Cloudflare D1 and Drizzle ORM store projects, revisions, membership, and activity history. Mathematical notes use Markdown and KaTeX. The app exposes twenty typed WebMCP tools through document.modelContext.registerTool(...) . They cover project navigation, bounded branch reads, nested idea scopes, versioned node updates, directed relations, resources, and tasks. What was difficult The main challenge was making this more than a mind map. Containment and relations had to mean different things. A lemma may live inside one idea while still supporting something outside it. Resources should exist once at the project level but remain usable from many branches. Local notes should stay hidden until the researcher opens their parent idea. Cycles also needed a deliberate rule. A conceptual cycle can be meaningful: two approaches may refine or support one another. A dependency cycle is different. The application therefore permits ordinary relational cycles but rejects circular depends on chains. Agent writes introduced another group of problems. Repeated calls should not create duplicate objects. Two edits should not silently overwrite one another. Two agents should not claim the same task without noticing. I added operation identifiers, object versions, project revisions, and atomic task claims to address these cases. The visual board also took more work than expected. Dragging, resizing, zooming, negative coordinates, edge labels, nested scopes, and collapsible panels all had to work without losing the underlying research state. What I learned The most useful WebMCP applications may not be the ones where an agent completely replaces the human workflow. Here, the website remains useful on its own. WebMCP gives the agent access to the same objects and operations that the researcher already uses. The human still has an essential role. During one smoke test, an agent saved the standard parameterization of Pythagorean triples. I noticed that it had omitted the scaling factor and the conditions for primitive triples. The agent then corrected the existing resource in place. The corrected version and its history remained on the board. That interaction is close to what I wanted: the agent performs explicit work, the human checks the meaning, and neither has to reconstruct the project from an old conversation. Research Blackboard does not solve research problems automatically. It tries to preserve the structure of the attempt.
