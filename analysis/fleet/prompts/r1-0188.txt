# FROZEN SCORING RUBRIC (pre-registered before any Stage 1 review)

Scores are integers 1-10 on each of the FOUR official criteria. Every score carries:
rationale, evidence_surfaces, confidence (0-1). Reviewers use the full scale and these
band anchors verbatim. Category neutrality is absolute: a game, art piece, music tool,
CRM, weird toy, and developer tool are all eligible for 10/10 on every criterion if they
satisfy that criterion exceptionally well FOR WHAT THEY ARE TRYING TO BE.
No aesthetic penalties. Signs of hurried AI work matter only as actual product-quality
problems (incoherence, broken flow, placeholder content), never as style.

## WebMCP Leverage — what does WebMCP change for this product?
Judge the delta WebMCP makes to reliability, precision, structured state access,
shared human-agent state, repeatability, and capability. Tool COUNT is not leverage.
- 9-10 TRANSFORMATIVE: the intended experience is impractical without the structured
  agent surface; human and agent share live product state; the collaboration could not
  be replicated comparably well by a general agent driving the UI.
- 7-8 MAJOR DELTA: without WebMCP a general agent is markedly less reliable/precise
  here; the agent is central to the product, not a bolt-on.
- 5-6 MEANINGFUL DELTA: existing capability packaged into a genuinely better product
  experience via structured tools; same outcome otherwise possible with real friction.
- 3-4 MINOR DELTA: generic browser automation achieves comparable outcomes; WebMCP is
  a convenience layer.
- 1-2 COSMETIC: exposes an action a UI button already offers; no meaningful change.

## Execution — is the actual product coherent, intentional, complete for its scope?
Stage 1 scores EXECUTION_CLAIMED: coherence/intentionality/completeness as evidenced by
the packet (description + packaging + video frames/transcript). Thin evidence caps the
score (end-to-end credit requires end-to-end evidence) and lowers confidence.
Stage 2 may rescore as EXECUTION_OBSERVED; latest evidence-backed score replaces, never
sums.
- 9-10: all surfaces agree; central workflow demonstrably completes; states handled;
  feels finished for its stated scope.
- 7-8: strong coherence and intentionality; central workflow appears functional; minor
  unfinished edges.
- 5-6: plausibly real with visible gaps; scope unclear or core not demonstrated
  end-to-end.
- 3-4: thin prototype; contradictions between surfaces; placeholder content.
- 1-2: non-functional or incoherent for its claimed scope.

## Creativity & Ambition — novelty of concept and interaction model
Judge ONLY: novelty of the concept, novelty of the interaction model, originality
relative to existing concepts, ambition, memorable/surprising use of the medium, and
the depth with which the concept is pursued. WebMCP indispensability belongs under
WebMCP Leverage, NOT here: a project can be extremely creative even if another agent
API could theoretically have enabled something similar.
- 9-10: genuinely novel concept or interaction model; ambitious scope pursued deeply
  and coherently; memorable.
- 7-8: fresh synthesis or notably original interaction; ambitious for a hackathon.
- 5-6: competent take on a known category with a real new angle.
- 3-4: derivative; novelty mostly surface details.
- 1-2: clone/template with trivial changes.
"That the category exists" is never a penalty; judge the submitted concept.

## Potential Impact — credible value for the intended audience
Judge: is there a real audience; is there a real problem, desire, or use case for that
audience; does the demonstrated solution genuinely address it. NO commercial adoption,
monetization, market pull, or startup viability is required. Entertainment, play, art,
creative expression, learning, niche hobby use, experimentation, and community value
are legitimate forms of impact. A tiny audience fully justifies a top score.
- 9-10: specific real audience with a real problem/desire; solution demonstrably
  addresses it; credible value, appeal, usefulness, or meaning for that audience.
- 7-8: clear audience and problem, good fit, credible value.
- 5-6: identifiable audience, looser fit or partial address.
- 3-4: speculative audience or strained fit; impact mostly aspirational.
- 1-2: no coherent audience/problem pairing.
Audience size is NOT a multiplier.

## Aggregate & ranking
aggregate = leverage + execution + impact + creativity (4-40). Nothing else enters.
Tie-breaks: Leverage, then Execution, then Impact, then Creativity, then higher
combined evidence confidence, then slug alphabetical. See PROTOCOL.md Ranking section.

Like 1 Comment Story Updates 01-intro 05-compare-tools-vs-baseline 03-blocked-at-gate 02-agent-filling 04-submitted-confirmation 06-webmcp-tools-panel CoAuth - an agent-native prior-authorization cockpit Inspiration Prior authorization is where American healthcare loses time it cannot afford - and it loses it to paperwork, not to medicine. In Medicare Advantage in 2024, 80.7% of appealed prior-authorization denials were fully or partially overturned, yet only 11.5% of denials were ever appealed ( KFF ). Read those two numbers together: most denials that get contested turn out to have been wrong, and almost no one contests them. The clinical decision is usually fine. The filing is where it breaks. That is an unusually clean fit for WebMCP, and not just because forms are tedious. Browser agents are weakest exactly where this work lives - long, write-heavy, multi-step forms full of coded fields (ICD-10, HCPCS J-codes, NPIs) that a pixel-clicking agent has no way to get right. And in this domain a human is not optional: a licensed clinician is legally required to attest. So the task splits along a natural seam. The agent is good at the mechanics; the human is mandatory for the judgment. That is what human-plus-agent collaboration is actually supposed to be - not an agent doing everything while a person watches a progress bar, but two participants each doing the part only they can. What it does CoAuth is a single live page that registers 13 typed WebMCP tools on document.modelContext . An agent loads a patient record, reads a payer's coverage rules as structured data, fills every non-judgment field, attaches clinical evidence, scores denial risk, detects contraindications and unmet step-therapy criteria, and drafts the medical-necessity narrative and an appeal letter. What it cannot do: fill a clinician-judgment field, resolve a critical contraindication, or submit. Those belong to the human, and both halves are enforced in code rather than described in prose. The demo case is Marcus Lee, a psoriatic-arthritis patient requesting Humira through Aetna. The agent fills the form cleanly and then catches what a rushed clinician might miss - a positive TB screen, a contraindication for this drug - flags it, tries to submit, and is stopped. It drafts the medical-necessity language from the chart but cannot adopt its own draft. From there the clinician signs in, makes the call on the TB screen and documents why, accepts the drafted wording as their own, attests, and signs. Only then does the submission go through, with a server-verified audit trail in the clinician's name. Two details make that boundary real rather than cosmetic. The signature is minted only for an authenticated clinician who re-enters their own credential at the moment of signing - each clinician has a distinct secret, so the name and NPI in the audit trail belong to the person who actually signed, not to anyone who knew a shared password. And the filed submission is rendered as an X12 278 - the HIPAA-mandated Health Care Services Review transaction payers actually receive - so every value the agent filled and the clinician signed maps to the real prior-authorization EDI, not just our own JSON. How I built it Vite, React, TypeScript, and Zustand on the front end; Vercel Edge Functions and Upstash Redis on the back end. A 15-rule clinical engine drives conflict detection, denial-risk scoring, and validation. The architectural decision that carried everything else: the WebMCP tool surface is the application's only write path. Every click in the interface calls the same invokeTool an agent calls, tagged with the actor - there is no parallel code path for humans. So the tools cannot rot behind the UI, attribution is a property of the call path rather than something each component must remember, and the built-in "with tools vs. without" comparison drives the real tools rather than a re-implementation. Tool schemas are generated from the seed data, so the patient, payer, drug and field enums cannot drift stale - an early bug had a payer hint still reading "uhc or aetna" after a third payer was added. Every property carries a description; write tools declare destructiveHint / idempotentHint so a runtime can require confirmation before submit ; and a get_submission_state tool lets an agent joining mid-task see what is filled, who filled it, what is unresolved, and whether the clinician has signed. Correctness is proven by 127 checks that run against the deployed system - not unit tests, and nothing mocked. They attempt to submit without a signature, with a forged approval, with a form edited after signing, with a clinical override swapped after signing, and with one approval replayed twelve times concurrently , requiring exactly one acceptance. They drive the registered WebMCP tools through a real model-context surface and try, as an agent would, to set the attending attestation. They drive every clinician control the way a hostile script would and require that none of them takes effect. And a meta-check reads the clinical rules table and fails if any rule is not exercised by a test. Challenges I faced The entire pitch is that an AI agent cannot file a prior auth without a real clinician signing off, and the hard part was making that actually true instead of just looking true. Every time I thought I'd locked it down, I'd poke at it and find another way around my own gate. My first gate was a browser check, which is useless against an agent that lives in the browser. I moved it to the server, then found the sign endpoint would hand a signature to anyone who asked, under any name they typed. I fixed that, then found a script could still sign using the logged-in doctor's own session, no click required. Turning on the database to stop replays silently rejected every submission in production. And the safety rules were reading tea leaves, so a note saying "no adverse events" got written into a letter to the insurer as a treatment failure. The real enemy was never a single bug. Every time I fixed the exact thing in front of me, the same kind of bug was sitting one layer over: I'd guard the button and leave the API open, guard one tool and leave its twin open, protect the signature and forget the reasoning behind it. What finally worked was to stop patching the specific hole and close the whole category at once, with one shared check instead of five copies I'd never keep in sync. What I learned Stop trusting the moment something starts working. Every real hole showed up after the feature looked done, and I only made progress once I started trying to break my own product on purpose. A passing test can also be a lie: I had one proving an approval couldn't be reused, and it passed while the live site happily accepted a burst of the same token, because the test wasn't checking the thing I was actually afraid of. And the big one, about the product itself: the interesting part of a human-plus-AI tool isn't making the AI capable, that part is easy. It's drawing the line the AI cannot cross, building the wall around what belongs to the human, and proving it holds when someone is actively trying to climb it. For something that touches patient care, that wall isn't a feature. It's the whole point. It would also be difficult to communicate which actions the signed-in user is actually permitted to perform. With WebMCP, the page exposes a Space specific catalog of typed operations. I also learned that human in the loop cannot be a sentence added to a prompt. It must be represented by durable state that prevents execution until the right person acts. Once that is true, the interface can clearly show what is waiting, what was approved, what executed, and what authority still remains with a human. About Mechanism Canvas Inspiration I kept coming back to a mismatch between how students solve organic chemistry problems and how AI tutors usually communicate. Reaction mechanisms live in diagrams. The important details are the atoms, bonds, lone pairs, curved arrows, and the order in which electrons move. A separate chat window sees almost none of that unless the student stops and translates the drawing into prose. Giving an agent broad control of the drawing surface creates a different problem. It can finish the exercise before the learner has worked through it. I wanted to see whether an agent could help inside the real mechanism canvas while the student still controlled the consequential part: what actually gets added to the answer. That became the central idea behind Mechanism Canvas. The agent gets a safe place to explore. The page checks the chemistry. The learner decides what comes back. What I built Mechanism Canvas is an organic chemistry workspace where a student and a WebMCP agent can work with the same structured mechanism. The page exposes meaningful chemistry objects instead of screen coordinates, so the agent can refer to a particular atom, bond, lone pair, or revision without guessing from pixels. The main demo is a counterfactual (S_\mathrm{N}2) lab. The agent tests two electron-flow paths away from the learner's draft. In the first branch, it forms the new bond but forgets the coupled leaving-group cleavage. The production validator rejects that attempt. The second branch contains both moves, so the validator approves it. The checked branch then reaches a visible proposal gate. It still does not change the learner's mechanism. The learner can inspect the evidence, add the proposal to the draft, request one more check, and make the final commit. An incorrect agent attempt becomes useful evidence instead of damage that has to be undone. The site also works as a complete manual tutor when WebMCP is unavailable. Direct agent collaboration is an extra layer on top of the same interface rather than a separate agent-only experience. How I built it I built the app with React, TypeScript, and Vite. The mechanism canvas is backed by a structured graph with stable IDs for atoms, bonds, electron sources, and curved-arrow moves. A deterministic chemistry engine evaluates proposed steps against six reviewed fixtures. The model can suggest and explore, but it does not decide whether a mechanism is chemically valid. The WebMCP layer registers 26 imperative tools through document.modelContext.registerTool . Each tool uses a closed JSON schema and calls the same TypeScript store commands as the visible interface. Mutating calls re-read live state and check the current revision, scope, and learner-granted permissions before they run. Tool discovery changes with the task. Coach mode begins with 16 available tools. Opening the two-path lab adds the branch controls. Starting a six-action comparison session narrows the surface to the capabilities needed for that job. Once the action budget is used, the work tools disappear and only four evidence controls remain. A cached tool cannot restore access that the learner has already closed. I also built a Collaboration Contract where the learner sets the agent's maximum role and hint level. An intent-bound session freezes the job, problem, revision, and action budget. The Live Run Observatory shows which capability batches the host accepted, and a Proof Ledger records privacy-minimized before-and-after receipts from the actual tool callback boundary. Around that core, I added keyboard-operable SVG structures, local persistence, reversible commits, state diffs, electron-flow replay, and a lazy-loaded Three.js molecular inspector. The finished project is a static GitHub Pages site with no backend, account, or credentials required. Challenges The hardest engineering problem was keeping tool discovery honest while the page changed. A fixed list of 26 tools would technically work, but it would hide the learner's current intent inside descriptions. My first dynamic version swung too far in the other direction: every small store notification could publish a transient capability batch. Those batches were real, but they did not represent a stable state that a judge or learner could understand. I changed the registration layer to coalesce related updates, wait for the complete batch, and record only surfaces the host had accepted. Proof was another difficult part. A model saying that it called a tool is only a claim. I needed the page to show whether the callback ran, whether the expected state changed, and whether the main draft stayed untouched. The Proof Ledger now wraps the real callback boundary, and the Observatory evaluates its seven demo claims from those receipts. I also had to make failure safe without making it invisible. The two lab branches have real revisioned state and use the same validator as the main canvas, but they remain isolated until the learner chooses a checked proposal. That separation took more work than simply giving the agent an undo button. It is also the part that makes the workflow useful for learning. What I learned I learned that an agent's tool list is part of the user interface. A capability name, its schema, when it appears, and when it disappears all communicate authority. Encoding the learner's intent in that surface made the boundary much clearer than relying on prompt instructions alone. I also learned how well deterministic software and agent reasoning can complement each other. The agent is good at generating alternatives and explaining a choice. The chemistry engine is good at enforcing exact constraints every time. Keeping those jobs separate made the result easier to trust. Most of all, this project changed how I think about mistakes in learning software. A wrong attempt does not have to be erased or quietly corrected. When it is isolated, checked, and kept visible, it can show the learner exactly why the complete mechanism needs both electron-flow moves. What's next The current chemistry engine is intentionally limited to reviewed fixtures. I would like to add an instructor authoring flow for new mechanisms and study how different permission contracts affect the explanations students give. The same pattern could also work in circuit design, geometry, and other visual STEM tools where exploration is useful but ownership should stay with the learner. What it does RuleRipple is a WebMCP-powered policy simulation sandbox for humans and AI agents. It helps users define allocation rules, preview their effects, and approve allocations with an audit trail. WebMCP is useful here because the agent works with the same visible policy workspace as the user. Agents can inspect policies and submit structured requests, while people can see the results, understand the effect on other requests, and retain control over approvals. Users can: Define available resources, protected reserves, eligibility requirements, and priorities. Receive budget requests from agents through HTTPS or WebMCP. Simulate how a policy distributes resources across competing requests. Change a rule and compare who becomes eligible, moves in priority, gains resources, or has to wait. Review the reasons behind each allocation and approve exact amounts. Track reservations, commitments, recorded consumption, and released resources in a ledger. The same approach supports resources such as agent credits, funding, API quotas, clinic hours, inventory, and inspection slots. A simple example Imagine two agents each request 70 credits from a shared budget of 100. There is enough to support either request fully, but not both. A priority-based policy might fund the urgent request first. A sharing policy might distribute credits between them, provided each receives enough to do useful work. RuleRipple lets a user test these choices before approving an allocation. Changing a priority rule or protecting a reserve reveals the effect across all requests. The “ripple” is the impact one policy change has on everyone competing for the same resources. How we built it We built RuleRipple with React and TypeScript, with a deterministic engine for policy evaluation, ranking, resource allocation, version comparison, and ledger accounting. The WebMCP tools use structured schemas and runtime validation. Firebase Authentication and Cloud Firestore support sign-in and persistent workspaces, and the application is deployed through ChatGPT Sites. An optional GitHub integration connects an authorized request to a specific external action, with source checks and a recorded execution receipt. Challenges we ran into One challenge was making the full effect of a rule change understandable. A change that helps one request can reduce the resources available to another. We built comparisons that expose those consequences across the complete set of requests. Another challenge was handling practical allocation constraints. A partial award may be useless if a task needs a minimum amount to run. RuleRipple accounts for those minimums alongside priorities, reserves, and resource limits. We also needed to keep simulation, authorization, and execution distinct. Previewing an allocation does not spend resources. The ledger tracks subsequent resource operations, and repeated requests must not accidentally reserve the same budget twice. Accomplishments that we're proud of We brought policy definition, simulation, explanations, approval, and resource accounting into one workflow. RuleRipple supports six allocation strategies, multiple resource pools, policy version comparisons, and explanations for individual outcomes. Its WebMCP tools make that workflow accessible to agents while keeping decisions visible to people. What we learned An allocation can follow its rules correctly and still produce an unexpected result. Showing the consequences of those rules is essential to helping people decide whether the policy matches their intent. We also learned the value of giving agents structured access to a shared application. Agents can help inspect and propose changes, while deterministic calculations and explicit authorization make the results reproducible and reviewable. What's next for RuleRipple We want to add organizational roles, richer approval workflows, more execution integrations, and live provider usage metering. Our goal is to help teams understand the consequences of resource decisions before committing to them—and retain a clear record afterward.
