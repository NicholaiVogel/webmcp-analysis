# FROZEN SCORING RUBRIC (pre-registered before any Stage 1 review)

Scores are integers 1-10 on each of the FOUR official criteria. Every score carries:
rationale, evidence_surfaces, confidence (0-1). Reviewers use the full scale and these
band anchors verbatim. Category neutrality is absolute: a game, art piece, music tool,
CRM, weird toy, and developer tool are all eligible for 10/10 on every criterion if they
satisfy that criterion exceptionally well FOR WHAT THEY ARE TRYING TO BE.
No aesthetic penalties. Signs of hurried AI work matter only as actual product-quality
problems (incoherence, broken flow, placeholder content), never as style.

## WebMCP Leverage — what does WebMCP change for this product?
Judge the delta WebMCP makes to reliability, precision, structured state access,
shared human-agent state, repeatability, and capability. Tool COUNT is not leverage.
- 9-10 TRANSFORMATIVE: the intended experience is impractical without the structured
  agent surface; human and agent share live product state; the collaboration could not
  be replicated comparably well by a general agent driving the UI.
- 7-8 MAJOR DELTA: without WebMCP a general agent is markedly less reliable/precise
  here; the agent is central to the product, not a bolt-on.
- 5-6 MEANINGFUL DELTA: existing capability packaged into a genuinely better product
  experience via structured tools; same outcome otherwise possible with real friction.
- 3-4 MINOR DELTA: generic browser automation achieves comparable outcomes; WebMCP is
  a convenience layer.
- 1-2 COSMETIC: exposes an action a UI button already offers; no meaningful change.

## Execution — is the actual product coherent, intentional, complete for its scope?
Stage 1 scores EXECUTION_CLAIMED: coherence/intentionality/completeness as evidenced by
the packet (description + packaging + video frames/transcript). Thin evidence caps the
score (end-to-end credit requires end-to-end evidence) and lowers confidence.
Stage 2 may rescore as EXECUTION_OBSERVED; latest evidence-backed score replaces, never
sums.
- 9-10: all surfaces agree; central workflow demonstrably completes; states handled;
  feels finished for its stated scope.
- 7-8: strong coherence and intentionality; central workflow appears functional; minor
  unfinished edges.
- 5-6: plausibly real with visible gaps; scope unclear or core not demonstrated
  end-to-end.
- 3-4: thin prototype; contradictions between surfaces; placeholder content.
- 1-2: non-functional or incoherent for its claimed scope.

## Creativity & Ambition — novelty of concept and interaction model
Judge ONLY: novelty of the concept, novelty of the interaction model, originality
relative to existing concepts, ambition, memorable/surprising use of the medium, and
the depth with which the concept is pursued. WebMCP indispensability belongs under
WebMCP Leverage, NOT here: a project can be extremely creative even if another agent
API could theoretically have enabled something similar.
- 9-10: genuinely novel concept or interaction model; ambitious scope pursued deeply
  and coherently; memorable.
- 7-8: fresh synthesis or notably original interaction; ambitious for a hackathon.
- 5-6: competent take on a known category with a real new angle.
- 3-4: derivative; novelty mostly surface details.
- 1-2: clone/template with trivial changes.
"That the category exists" is never a penalty; judge the submitted concept.

## Potential Impact — credible value for the intended audience
Judge: is there a real audience; is there a real problem, desire, or use case for that
audience; does the demonstrated solution genuinely address it. NO commercial adoption,
monetization, market pull, or startup viability is required. Entertainment, play, art,
creative expression, learning, niche hobby use, experimentation, and community value
are legitimate forms of impact. A tiny audience fully justifies a top score.
- 9-10: specific real audience with a real problem/desire; solution demonstrably
  addresses it; credible value, appeal, usefulness, or meaning for that audience.
- 7-8: clear audience and problem, good fit, credible value.
- 5-6: identifiable audience, looser fit or partial address.
- 3-4: speculative audience or strained fit; impact mostly aspirational.
- 1-2: no coherent audience/problem pairing.
Audience size is NOT a multiplier.

## Aggregate & ranking
aggregate = leverage + execution + impact + creativity (4-40). Nothing else enters.
Tie-breaks: Leverage, then Execution, then Impact, then Creativity, then higher
combined evidence confidence, then slug alphabetical. See PROTOCOL.md Ranking section.

A shared crisis control room where the operator and WebMCP agent work from the same deterministic state. The final debrief records full service, bounded authority, outcome metrics and the human-authorized trade-off. Switchboard Zero is a deterministic crisis-training sandbox for one difficult agent-design problem: how can a person delegate meaningful work without silently giving up authority? Inspiration The idea came from a question I kept returning to while shaping the project: if an agent can reset the exercise, change the policy, or approve its own exception, is the human actually in control? That pushed me away from a conventional assistant demo and toward a simulation where authority is enforced by the product, not merely promised in a prompt. The power grid is fictional, but the design problem is not. Any team testing agents in operations, security, or other high-stakes workflows needs to know not only whether an agent can complete a task, but whether it stops at the right moment. What it does Switchboard Zero places a human operator and a browser agent in the same fictional municipal-grid emergency. They have eight simulated minutes before a damaged feeder threatens the Hospital supply. The agent can inspect the incident, forecast the cost of delay, simulate recovery strategies, and execute actions that remain inside the operator's policy. When the next dependent action would disconnect a protected service or exceed the automatic risk ceiling, execution stops. The agent can explain the exact trade-off and place it in a visible decision queue, but only the human operator can authorize or reject it. The interface, Site Tools, policy controls, audit trail, and final debrief share one state. Agent actions are immediately visible to the person, and human decisions return to the same recovery plan. Why WebMCP This use case needs more than an agent clicking buttons or interpreting pixels. WebMCP gives the site a structured capability surface while preserving a fully usable human interface. Switchboard Zero exposes five Site Tools: inspect_grid reads the complete current state and authority boundary. simulate_delay_impact forecasts waiting without advancing the live exercise. simulate_recovery_plan creates a visible plan without applying switches. apply_authorized_actions executes in dependency order and stops at the first policy boundary. request_human_decision stages only the action that was actually withheld; it never approves or executes it. This makes the collaboration explicit: the agent is fast inside the boundary, while the person remains responsible for moving that boundary. Without a structured capability surface, this interaction would depend on fragile UI automation or split the agent, policy controls, and human decision into separate interfaces. How we built it The application is a browser-only React 19 and TypeScript project built with Vite. A deterministic domain engine models district service, generation, faults, recovery plans, policy locks, risk ceilings, approvals, dependency ordering, outcomes, and scoring. The five tools are registered with document.modelContext.registerTool . Read-only tools declare readOnlyHint ; state-changing tools commit to the same React state used by the visible control room. There is no backend, external runtime API, analytics, or connection to real infrastructure. Vitest covers 22 automated checks: 14 deterministic domain cases and 8 direct WebMCP registration and contract cases. GitHub Actions runs the complete test suite and production build, and GitHub Pages hosts the public app. Challenges The hardest problem was not generating a successful recovery plan. It was making the authority boundary real rather than decorative. Adversarial testing exposed a concrete failure mode: after a human-authorized transfer, changing recovery strategy could allow the strategy label to determine the outcome and leave Transit unrestored. I fixed it by deriving completion from the actual grid state, preserving the remaining recovery action, and adding a regression test for that sequence. Execution must never skip past a blocked dependency. A decision request must refer to the exact action previously withheld. Forecasting delay must not mutate the live clock. Approval, rejection, policy changes, time advancement, and exercise restart must remain human-only controls. Each rule is enforced in the domain model and reflected in the UI. A second challenge was keeping the agent and operator synchronized. Every simulation, action, approval, metric, and outcome had to update one shared state and one chronological decision trace. Trust boundary Switchboard Zero enforces an application-level delegation boundary: the agent receives a narrower capability surface than the human interface, and every state-changing tool is checked against the deterministic domain model. This demonstrates authority-aware tool design, but it is not presented as a production security boundary against hostile JavaScript, a compromised browser extension, or direct manipulation of client-side state. The current release has no backend, authentication, secrets, external content, analytics, or connection to real infrastructure. A production adaptation would require server-side authorization independent of the model, authenticated operators, durable tamper-evident audit records, replay-resistant action requests, and strict validation and isolation of external content. What we learned I learned that the hardest part of tool design is deciding what not to expose. A control labelled "human only" means very little if the same action is still available through an agent tool. That is why policy changes, approval, rejection, live time advancement, and exercise restart are absent from the WebMCP surface and enforced separately in the domain engine. I also learned that a human handoff is not just a confirmation dialog. It has to preserve the exact blocked action, explain its consequence, and return the decision to the plan the agent was already executing. Intended use and potential impact The intended audience is teams designing, training, or evaluating agents in operations, security, and other workflows where automated actions create real trade-offs. The same interaction pattern could be used to train operators, compare whether different agents stop at the correct authority boundary, and replay policy regressions after model, prompt, or tool changes. The transferable result is not the fictional power grid; it is a visible and testable human-supervision pattern. This release proves that pattern in one deterministic scenario. It does not claim real-grid readiness or validation with real operators. What's next The current release proves the interaction model in one deterministic scenario. The person keeps the canvas, the legend, the undo history and the Save button. The agent gets typed verbs and honest read-back. Neither side has to trust the other's description of the map, because both are looking at the same post-apply state. "Agents propose, people click" is literal here: browsers block downloads that a human did not initiate, so export_image renders the full-size image and puts a real Save button on the page for the person. How we built it The integration is deliberately one thin boundary, three touch points: Mount. A WebMCPBridge component (renders nothing) runs registerWebMcpTools() once on the client. For the ~99% of visitors without an agent runtime that is a single property probe ( document.modelContext , with the legacy navigator.modelContext name as fallback — the entry point has been renamed twice already). Teardown aborts the registration's AbortController . The chat bridge seam. The chat panel owns sendMessage — the same submission pipeline behind the composer (mutate-first ladder, SSE streaming, store application, billing refresh). It registers that callable with the WebMCP layer, so build_map / mutate_map are processed exactly as if the human had typed: same quota, same 1-credit billing with auto-refund, same anonymous-visitor rules (anonymous sessions get a typed sign-in prompt from mutate_map , never a silent no-op). Store reads. The free tools read and write the app's existing map store directly — no server calls. Every result is summarized from post-apply state: layer and feature counts come from what actually landed, never from "the request was sent". If nothing rendered, the result says delivered: false — do not claim a map exists . Two safety pieces sit on top: every paid tool states its cost in its description (that is what runtimes use to classify a call as consequential — a silently-costed tool is the prompt-injection amplifier), and a gesture-reset budget refuses paid calls after 8 of them with no human interaction until the person touches the page. Tests: a unit suite pinning the budget semantics, the grounding rule and the cost-honest descriptions; a Playwright spec in the main app's CI where a stubbed agent runtime drives the real page; and, in the public repo, a live-site contract test that drives showmeonmap.com's registered tools through document.modelContext with no access to app internals — npm test , about 7 seconds, all 8 tools. Stack: TypeScript, React / Next.js on Cloudflare Workers (OpenNext), MapLibre GL + deck.gl, Zustand stores, Playwright. The tool schemas are plain JSON Schema. Challenges we ran into The spec moved under us. window.agent → navigator.modelContext → document.modelContext , and Chrome's docs show execute returning a string while ChatGPT's show an object. We probe both entry points and return JSON text, which both runtimes accept. A tool that says "ok" while the canvas didn't change is lying. Our first camera tool wrote to a legacy config channel the renderer ignores while a plan is active — it reported success and nothing moved. Caught in the first live ChatGPT-browser test, fixed by driving the live MapLibre instance and reading the camera back through get_map_state . Downloads. Agent-initiated tool calls carry no user activation, so export_image cannot save a file. It renders the image and hands the human a real Save button instead. Reading a map back honestly at neighbourhood scale. "Schools in Kerrisdale" means the area people call Kerrisdale, not the cadastral polygon; strict containment cut schools a block outside the line. Solid dots are inside, faded ones sit within 800 m outside, and the read-back says which is which. Cost. An agent in a loop can spend a person's credits. Cost in the description plus the gesture-reset budget is the answer we shipped; it is also why paid tools go through the same billing path as the composer, with auto-refund.
