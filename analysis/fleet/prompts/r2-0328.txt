=== RUBRIC (frozen) ===
# FROZEN SCORING RUBRIC (pre-registered before any Stage 1 review)

Scores are integers 1-10 on each of the FOUR official criteria. Every score carries:
rationale, evidence_surfaces, confidence (0-1). Reviewers use the full scale and these
band anchors verbatim. Category neutrality is absolute: a game, art piece, music tool,
CRM, weird toy, and developer tool are all eligible for 10/10 on every criterion if they
satisfy that criterion exceptionally well FOR WHAT THEY ARE TRYING TO BE.
No aesthetic penalties. Signs of hurried AI work matter only as actual product-quality
problems (incoherence, broken flow, placeholder content), never as style.

## WebMCP Leverage — what does WebMCP change for this product?
Judge the delta WebMCP makes to reliability, precision, structured state access,
shared human-agent state, repeatability, and capability. Tool COUNT is not leverage.
- 9-10 TRANSFORMATIVE: the intended experience is impractical without the structured
  agent surface; human and agent share live product state; the collaboration could not
  be replicated comparably well by a general agent driving the UI.
- 7-8 MAJOR DELTA: without WebMCP a general agent is markedly less reliable/precise
  here; the agent is central to the product, not a bolt-on.
- 5-6 MEANINGFUL DELTA: existing capability packaged into a genuinely better product
  experience via structured tools; same outcome otherwise possible with real friction.
- 3-4 MINOR DELTA: generic browser automation achieves comparable outcomes; WebMCP is
  a convenience layer.
- 1-2 COSMETIC: exposes an action a UI button already offers; no meaningful change.

## Execution — is the actual product coherent, intentional, complete for its scope?
Stage 1 scores EXECUTION_CLAIMED: coherence/intentionality/completeness as evidenced by
the packet (description + packaging + video frames/transcript). Thin evidence caps the
score (end-to-end credit requires end-to-end evidence) and lowers confidence.
Stage 2 may rescore as EXECUTION_OBSERVED; latest evidence-backed score replaces, never
sums.
- 9-10: all surfaces agree; central workflow demonstrably completes; states handled;
  feels finished for its stated scope.
- 7-8: strong coherence and intentionality; central workflow appears functional; minor
  unfinished edges.
- 5-6: plausibly real with visible gaps; scope unclear or core not demonstrated
  end-to-end.
- 3-4: thin prototype; contradictions between surfaces; placeholder content.
- 1-2: non-functional or incoherent for its claimed scope.

## Creativity & Ambition — novelty of concept and interaction model
Judge ONLY: novelty of the concept, novelty of the interaction model, originality
relative to existing concepts, ambition, memorable/surprising use of the medium, and
the depth with which the concept is pursued. WebMCP indispensability belongs under
WebMCP Leverage, NOT here: a project can be extremely creative even if another agent
API could theoretically have enabled something similar.
- 9-10: genuinely novel concept or interaction model; ambitious scope pursued deeply
  and coherently; memorable.
- 7-8: fresh synthesis or notably original interaction; ambitious for a hackathon.
- 5-6: competent take on a known category with a real new angle.
- 3-4: derivative; novelty mostly surface details.
- 1-2: clone/template with trivial changes.
"That the category exists" is never a penalty; judge the submitted concept.

## Potential Impact — credible value for the intended audience
Judge: is there a real audience; is there a real problem, desire, or use case for that
audience; does the demonstrated solution genuinely address it. NO commercial adoption,
monetization, market pull, or startup viability is required. Entertainment, play, art,
creative expression, learning, niche hobby use, experimentation, and community value
are legitimate forms of impact. A tiny audience fully justifies a top score.
- 9-10: specific real audience with a real problem/desire; solution demonstrably
  addresses it; credible value, appeal, usefulness, or meaning for that audience.
- 7-8: clear audience and problem, good fit, credible value.
- 5-6: identifiable audience, looser fit or partial address.
- 3-4: speculative audience or strained fit; impact mostly aspirational.
- 1-2: no coherent audience/problem pairing.
Audience size is NOT a multiplier.

## Aggregate & ranking
aggregate = leverage + execution + impact + creativity (4-40). Nothing else enters.
Tie-breaks: Leverage, then Execution, then Impact, then Creativity, then higher
combined evidence confidence, then slug alphabetical. See PROTOCOL.md Ranking section.

Forkline lets an AI agent rehearse disposable operational futures through native WebMCP tools, while a human keeps exact-state approval over anything that can become real. Like Comment Story Updates Main UI Inspiration The dangerous part of an agent is not simply that it can be wrong. It is that it can be fluent while it is wrong, and then something irreversible happens. We kept seeing the same shape: a model explores a messy situation, picks a plan, and the UI treats “the agent said so” as permission. The human either rubber-stamps it or fights the interface. There is no clear place where the agent is allowed to be ambitious and forbidden to make its answer real. Forkline started from that split. Let the agent try every recovery. Do not let it touch the live world. The human sets the non-negotiables in the open. The only thing that becomes real is one exact state a person approved — not a vibe, and not a checkbox the model can invent. The Port Azure / St. Anne story is the sharpest version of that: hospital kits, a closed port, and a tempting air-freight cheat. If the agent can talk past “no air,” the whole thing is theater. What it does You open a disruption. The rules are on the page: protect hospital orders, keep critical fill above the floor, and use no air freight. An agent (via WebMCP) reads the network, forks disposable futures, reroutes, switches suppliers, reallocates stock, simulates, and compares. Bad futures stay useful. Emergency air looks fast and expensive, but it is illegal under the human rule . Regional stock-preserve starves the hospital. Those branches do not get a secret override. The agent can still ask to commit. Forkline says no until a human selects a branch and approves that exact state . Change the fill floor after approval, and the old yes is dead. Then you approve again, or you don’t. Six tools: get_network_state , fork_scenario , apply_intervention , simulate_scenario , compare_scenarios , commit_scenario . They use the same engine as the dashboard. If WebMCP is off, the page says so and still lets you walk the futures by hand. How we built it A small deterministic runtime in the browser handles the network, interventions, KPIs, violations, branch identity, and approval fingerprint. Then we put two faces on it: the ops UI and document.modelContext . Approval is not a field on the tool. The page mints a one-shot token bound to the scenario and the current human rules. Commit checks that fingerprint again. If the agent could mint “approved,” we would have built a liar. Challenges we ran into Making “the agent cannot authorize” actually true, not just a prompt. That meant fail-closed commit, stale tokens, and tests that try to cheat. WebMCP is still behind a flag, so a normal Chrome tab has to stay honest instead of fake-registering tools. The demo had to show a rejected commit, not just a pretty graph. That is the product. It is also the part people are most likely to skip when rushing a video. Accomplishments that we're proud of The agent can be wide. The human is narrow. You can watch both. You can watch an illegal recovery fail in public instead of getting smoothed into a recommendation. You can watch a feasible recovery still get refused until a person binds themselves to that exact snapshot — and watch that binding die if they move a slider. What we learned More tools do not create safety. A gate the model cannot write creates safety. If the human rules live in the prompt, they are decoration. If they live in the page and the commit path, they are real. Disposable futures only matter if commit is expensive. Cheap commit, and you have just built a faster way to ship a bad plan. What's next for Forkline Same protocol, nastier decisions: anything where “try it” should be free and “make it live” should not — config, money, clinical, deploys. Tighter WebMCP as the API hardens. Keep the map small until the gate is obviously the point, not the supply chain. Public tools cannot expose account identity or balances, connect a wallet, create an executable intent, sign, submit, transfer funds, or change liquidity. Plans return mode: "unsigned" , canExecute: false , and requiresWallet: false . The preview is SDK-call metadata, not a SCALE-encoded transaction. This entry provides autonomous planning, with no unattended trading. How we built it The implementation uses Vue 3, TypeScript, Vite, the existing SORA SDK and node connection, and FPNumber for precise token amounts. Tools register through document.modelContext.registerTool() when WebMCP is available. The playground registers its tools at the top level and forwards calls into its same-origin application iframe. Strict schemas require decimal-string amounts and reject additional properties. Public result projection excludes wallet and provider details. Cancellation, bounded errors, network-context checks, and five-minute expiry keep planning results predictable. No executable authorization is stored by planning. Everything ships as static assets suitable for IPFS, without hosted command middleware. An optional local Node.js stdio MCP bridge shares the same public catalogue. Humans use visible controls and agents use named tools, with both reaching the application's existing planning logic. The scheduler serializes requests, ignores superseded results, pauses while hidden, and bounds retries for transient failures. Like Comment Story Updates Well anchoring Plate mapping Results analysis Inspiration Reading a 96-well XTT plate from a photograph can be useful for exploratory drug discovery work, but it is easy to overinterpret color, overlook glare, lose track of controls, or confuse a plate map with a validated biological conclusion. We built AssayLens to make the workflow more transparent: keep the image, geometry, plate map, controls, quality-control signals, and interpretation limits visible together. To the best of our knowledge, AssayLens is a first of its kind app providing streamlined workflow of image analysis for XTT cell viability assay. What it does AssayLens is a browser-based application for exploratory image analysis of XTT 96-well plates and agar endpoint spot assays. For XTT workflows, a user can load a plate image, confirm well geometry, map samples and serial dilutions, assign growth controls and reagent blanks, review well-level QC, and generate image-derived relative metabolic-activity results. The app also includes a narrow WebMCP integration that lets a browser agent inspect readiness, configure a dilution series, assign controls, run the existing analysis, and focus the highest-priority QC item. AssayLens intentionally does not present its results as calibrated plate-reader absorbance, viable-cell counts, validated MIC values, efficacy evidence, or clinical conclusions. Human review remains essential. How we built it We built AssayLens as a browser-local React and TypeScript application powered by Vite, Vitest, and browser workers. The image-analysis workflow runs in the browser, helping keep plate images, raw pixels, local paths, and API keys out of WebMCP tool outputs. The WebMCP layer exposes five bounded, page-owned tools: Inspect XTT workflow readiness and scientific limits Configure horizontal serial-dilution series Assign explicit XTT controls and normalization groups Run the existing exploratory image-analysis worker Focus the most important QC review item Tool-driven plate-map changes use the same application state as the visible editor, so the map, Selection panel, and Serial dilution panel remain synchronized. Challenges we ran into The key challenge was balancing useful automation with scientific restraint. We wanted an agent to help configure and inspect an assay workflow without allowing it to invent results, silently change scientific assumptions, or bypass human image and QC review. We also addressed an image-QC edge case involving deep-red XTT wells. A simplistic clipping rule could treat naturally low blue or green pixel values as invalid, even when the well image was usable. We refined the QC logic to preserve meaningful chromatic pixels while retaining diagnostics for genuine clipping, glare, transparent regions, and near-black pixels. Another challenge was ensuring that WebMCP mutations were not merely hidden state changes. We connected them directly to the visible plate editor so users can immediately verify what changed. Accomplishments that we're proud of We are proud that AssayLens combines practical browser-based assay workflow support with explicit guardrails. The application makes controls, normalization groups, plate geometry, excluded wells, warnings, and review priorities visible instead of hiding them behind a single automated result. What we learned We learned that good scientific software should communicate uncertainty as clearly as it communicates results. A successful workflow is not just one that produces an output—it is one that makes the input quality, assumptions, controls, and limitations easy for a human reviewer to inspect. We also learned that agent tooling is most useful when it works alongside an existing, visible user interface. Bounded tools, strict input validation, atomic map updates, and clear state synchronization make automation more trustworthy and easier to audit. What's next for AssayLens-XTT Cell Viability Assay App Next, we plan to continue improving the image-QC and review experience, expand reproducible reporting workflows, and refine assay-specific guidance while preserving the app’s exploratory scientific scope. We will keep focusing on transparent, human-reviewed workflows rather than overstating what image-derived assay analysis can prove.
