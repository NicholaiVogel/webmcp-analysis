# FROZEN SCORING RUBRIC (pre-registered before any Stage 1 review)

Scores are integers 1-10 on each of the FOUR official criteria. Every score carries:
rationale, evidence_surfaces, confidence (0-1). Reviewers use the full scale and these
band anchors verbatim. Category neutrality is absolute: a game, art piece, music tool,
CRM, weird toy, and developer tool are all eligible for 10/10 on every criterion if they
satisfy that criterion exceptionally well FOR WHAT THEY ARE TRYING TO BE.
No aesthetic penalties. Signs of hurried AI work matter only as actual product-quality
problems (incoherence, broken flow, placeholder content), never as style.

## WebMCP Leverage — what does WebMCP change for this product?
Judge the delta WebMCP makes to reliability, precision, structured state access,
shared human-agent state, repeatability, and capability. Tool COUNT is not leverage.
- 9-10 TRANSFORMATIVE: the intended experience is impractical without the structured
  agent surface; human and agent share live product state; the collaboration could not
  be replicated comparably well by a general agent driving the UI.
- 7-8 MAJOR DELTA: without WebMCP a general agent is markedly less reliable/precise
  here; the agent is central to the product, not a bolt-on.
- 5-6 MEANINGFUL DELTA: existing capability packaged into a genuinely better product
  experience via structured tools; same outcome otherwise possible with real friction.
- 3-4 MINOR DELTA: generic browser automation achieves comparable outcomes; WebMCP is
  a convenience layer.
- 1-2 COSMETIC: exposes an action a UI button already offers; no meaningful change.

## Execution — is the actual product coherent, intentional, complete for its scope?
Stage 1 scores EXECUTION_CLAIMED: coherence/intentionality/completeness as evidenced by
the packet (description + packaging + video frames/transcript). Thin evidence caps the
score (end-to-end credit requires end-to-end evidence) and lowers confidence.
Stage 2 may rescore as EXECUTION_OBSERVED; latest evidence-backed score replaces, never
sums.
- 9-10: all surfaces agree; central workflow demonstrably completes; states handled;
  feels finished for its stated scope.
- 7-8: strong coherence and intentionality; central workflow appears functional; minor
  unfinished edges.
- 5-6: plausibly real with visible gaps; scope unclear or core not demonstrated
  end-to-end.
- 3-4: thin prototype; contradictions between surfaces; placeholder content.
- 1-2: non-functional or incoherent for its claimed scope.

## Creativity & Ambition — novelty of concept and interaction model
Judge ONLY: novelty of the concept, novelty of the interaction model, originality
relative to existing concepts, ambition, memorable/surprising use of the medium, and
the depth with which the concept is pursued. WebMCP indispensability belongs under
WebMCP Leverage, NOT here: a project can be extremely creative even if another agent
API could theoretically have enabled something similar.
- 9-10: genuinely novel concept or interaction model; ambitious scope pursued deeply
  and coherently; memorable.
- 7-8: fresh synthesis or notably original interaction; ambitious for a hackathon.
- 5-6: competent take on a known category with a real new angle.
- 3-4: derivative; novelty mostly surface details.
- 1-2: clone/template with trivial changes.
"That the category exists" is never a penalty; judge the submitted concept.

## Potential Impact — credible value for the intended audience
Judge: is there a real audience; is there a real problem, desire, or use case for that
audience; does the demonstrated solution genuinely address it. NO commercial adoption,
monetization, market pull, or startup viability is required. Entertainment, play, art,
creative expression, learning, niche hobby use, experimentation, and community value
are legitimate forms of impact. A tiny audience fully justifies a top score.
- 9-10: specific real audience with a real problem/desire; solution demonstrably
  addresses it; credible value, appeal, usefulness, or meaning for that audience.
- 7-8: clear audience and problem, good fit, credible value.
- 5-6: identifiable audience, looser fit or partial address.
- 3-4: speculative audience or strained fit; impact mostly aspirational.
- 1-2: no coherent audience/problem pairing.
Audience size is NOT a multiplier.

## Aggregate & ranking
aggregate = leverage + execution + impact + creativity (4-40). Nothing else enters.
Tie-breaks: Leverage, then Execution, then Impact, then Creativity, then higher
combined evidence confidence, then slug alphabetical. See PROTOCOL.md Ranking section.

A shared architecture canvas where humans and AI agents build together. WebMCP lets agents inspect live app state and create, connect, arrange, and review the same workspace humans edit. Like Comment Story Updates Inspiration AI assistants are getting much better, but I kept noticing the same gap. Most of the time I am still talking to an AI about the application I am using instead of letting the AI actually work with me inside it. I wanted to explore a simple question: what if I and an AI agent could work on the same live application state? I chose architecture diagrams because they make this easy to see. I can move a component myself, ask the agent to continue working, and the agent should be able to understand what is on the canvas now instead of starting from a separate representation. That idea became NodeCraft. What it does NodeCraft is an architecture canvas where a human and an AI agent can work together. I can create, move, remove, and organize components directly on the canvas. At the same time, NodeCraft exposes capabilities through WebMCP that a compatible AI agent can discover and use. The agent can inspect the canvas, create components, connect or disconnect them, arrange the architecture, remove components, and flag something for design review. The key part is that there is no separate AI version of the diagram. My actions and the agent's actions update the same application state. For example, I can ask an agent to add an API service, Postgres database, Redis cache, and payment service. The agent can discover NodeCraft's WebMCP tools and create those components directly on the live canvas. I can then manually change the canvas and the agent can work from the updated state. I also added undo and redo, local persistence, editable human and agent names, an activity feed showing who did what, JSON import and export, and a simple way to start a new canvas. How I built it I built NodeCraft with React and HTML5 Canvas. The application registers seven WebMCP tools using document.modelContext.registerTool() : get_canvas_state create_canvas_node connect_nodes auto_layout_nodes remove_node disconnect_nodes flag_for_review I tried to make the tools describe useful actions rather than low level UI operations. An agent should be able to ask NodeCraft to connect two components. It should not need to know how I draw the connection on the canvas. The tools that modify the canvas update the same React state used by the normal human interface. get_canvas_state gives the agent a structured view of the nodes and connections that currently exist, and I marked it as read only. This is really the core of NodeCraft. I can make a change manually, the agent can inspect the current canvas, make its own changes through WebMCP, and I can continue working from there. I also added cleanup for registered tools, duplicate connection protection, human and agent action tracking, undo and redo, local browser persistence, and versioned JSON import and export. I experimented with WebMCP's cross origin model too. The flag_for_review tool is set up so that it can be selectively exposed to an authorized reviewer origin. WebMCP is important to the project because I did not want to build another chat box connected directly to an LLM. NodeCraft exposes what the application knows how to do, and a compatible agent can discover those capabilities and use them. The agent and the application do not have to be designed as one tightly coupled system. Challenges I ran into One of the first challenges was deciding what the agent should actually be able to do. I could have exposed low level actions that imitate how a human uses the interface, but that did not feel useful. If the user wants the API connected to Postgres, the agent should express that intent and NodeCraft should handle the visual details. Shared state was another challenge. There were also a lot of smaller problems that showed up while building: preventing duplicate directed connections, removing connections when a node is deleted, making sure WebMCP handlers have the latest React state, tracking whether an action came from the human or agent, safely importing project files, and cleaning up registered tools correctly. Testing was interesting too. At first I could manually execute individual WebMCP tools, which proved the tools worked, but that was not the experience I was trying to build. The agent discovered the tools exposed by the page and created an API Service, Postgres Database, Redis Cache, and Payment Service directly on the canvas. That was the moment the idea became much more real to me. Accomplishments that I'm proud of I am most proud that NodeCraft got beyond simply registering a WebMCP tool and showing that it could be called. Seeing an agent discover the tools from the page and create components on the same canvas I was editing manually was the main thing I wanted to prove with this project. I am also happy with the state reading side of it. get_canvas_state means the agent has a way to understand what currently exists instead of only having actions it can execute. I care about dragging boxes and seeing connections on a canvas. The agent needs capabilities such as create_canvas_node , connect_nodes , and get_canvas_state . I also underestimated how important reading state would be. Giving an agent actions is useful, but giving it a way to understand what the human has already done makes the collaboration much more interesting. It means the agent can continue from the current application instead of treating every request like a new task. I started the project thinking mostly about WebMCP tool calls. By the end, I was thinking about WebMCP more as an agent facing interface to the application itself. That is probably the biggest thing I will take away from this project. They need fewer obstacles between a potential customer deciding, "I need help with this," and actually taking the next step. That was the attraction of WebMCP. The interesting part for me is that the user owns the AI agent . A business website does not have to create its own assistant, replace its existing workflow or force visitors to learn a new interface. Clip Magic is a real small-business website, so I deliberately chose a practical task for this challenge: preparing a website-review enquiry. I chose a pragmatic approach rather than a dramatic one. For some users, especially those who find dense, field-by-field interfaces difficult, a conversational route can be useful. It is not a replacement for accessible form design, and it does not remove human choice. The ordinary form remains available; the agent simply helps a user reach the same workflow and leaves the final decision with them. Most people I know would rather explain what they need than fill in boxes on screen or on paper. The opportunity extends well beyond one website. Small and medium-sized enterprises represent around 90% of businesses worldwide , yet reliable data about the proportion of websites they operate is not generally available. What is clear is that there is already a large installed base of small-business websites: in 2025, 76.67% of EU small enterprises with 10–49 employees reported having a website ( Eurostat e-business data ), while 17% reported using AI, compared with 55% of large enterprises ( Eurostat AI statistics ). The question behind the project was simple: Can a user's AI agent understand that Clip Magic may be able to help, gather the minimum missing information, and prepare the existing enquiry form without bypassing the human or creating a separate AI workflow? The aim was not to build the most elaborate WebMCP demo. It was to see whether WebMCP could remove friction from something businesses and customers already do every day. What it does The live Clip Magic website exposes one bounded WebMCP capability: request_website_review A user can describe what they want in natural language. Their agent can inspect the public website, determine whether the request is relevant, ask for missing information, open the existing Contact workflow and populate the visible form. The important part is what happens next: nothing is submitted automatically . The user can read the completed form, change anything they want, and decide whether to click Submit. The human and agent therefore use the same underlying workflow: Contact dialog → existing FormBuilder form → existing validation and spam controls → human review → human submission WebMCP does not bypass that process. It progressively enhances it. If WebMCP is unavailable, the standard human form still works exactly as before. How I built it Clip Magic is an existing ProcessWire website. Its Contact form is managed by the commercial ProcessWire FormBuilder module, which already handles validation, spam filtering, storage and email delivery. Rather than duplicate any of that logic, I kept FormBuilder authoritative and added a thin WebMCP layer around the existing form. The implementation has two parts. First, the rendered form carries declarative WebMCP metadata describing the capability and its parameters. This keeps the agent-facing schema close to the real form instead of maintaining a separate copy of the business rules. Second, I added a small imperative compatibility bridge. During testing, Chrome's WebMCP client could discover the declarative tool, but ChatGPT's in-app browser did not. The bridge derives its name, description, parameters and required fields from the rendered declarative form, so it provides compatibility without becoming a second independent schema. WebMCP tools are discovered at the document level, so the capability is available as soon as the page loads. The Contact form remains the existing destination for the agent's prepared data, and the existing FormBuilder workflow remains unchanged. The capability is not tied to the Contact dialog's open or closed state. The dialog can be opened and closed normally without changing WebMCP availability; when the agent uses the capability, it opens the existing Contact workflow and populates the visible form. The live implementation was tested in both ChatGPT's in-app browser and Chrome with WebMCP enabled. Challenges The biggest challenge was not creating the tool. It was making WebMCP coexist safely with a real website that already had years of working behaviour and production constraints. Working with an existing form The challenge was to add an agent-facing capability without rebuilding the Contact system or duplicating its business rules. The solution was to keep the WebMCP layer close to the existing visible form while leaving the shared dialog system and FormBuilder workflow intact. Different WebMCP client behaviour Declarative WebMCP worked in Chrome testing but was not discovered by ChatGPT's in-app browser during development. Even Google's public declarative demo showed the same limitation in that environment. Rather than abandon the declarative implementation, I kept it as the primary definition and added the small compatibility bridge. This let both clients exercise the same capability without duplicating the schema manually. The useful boundary for this project is that the agent can do the repetitive preparation work, but the person sees the actual values in the real form and remains responsible for submission. The workflow was tested for cancellation, required-field validation, stale tool reuse, rapid double-submission, unexpected parameters, honeypot handling, email-header injection, unsafe URL schemes, prompt-injection text, session/token behaviour and controlled network failure. The supplied website URL is treated as form data only. The site does not fetch it, and no CMS, customer, authenticated or administrative capability is exposed through WebMCP. What I learned The most useful lesson was that WebMCP does not have to mean rebuilding a website for agents. For an existing business site, the better approach may be to identify a useful customer outcome, find the authoritative workflow that already handles it, and expose the smallest capability needed to help the user's agent reach that workflow reliably. I also learned that progressive enhancement matters just as much for agents as it does for browsers . The human path should not become dependent on WebMCP. The WebMCP path should not gain privileges the human path does not have. Both should meet at the same validation, security and business rules. Finally, testing natural-language requests reinforced an important distinction: the website does not need to encode every sales or qualification decision as another tool.

The AI-packed bag fits the 8 kg limit and the 6 × 8 spatial grid. Inspiration Most “AI personalization” demos are recommendation lists or theme pickers. They ask the user to repeat preferences the assistant may already understand, then return another block of text. Packing a carry-on makes that boundary immediately visible. The AI can decide what matters for this person right now; the browser knows every item’s weight and physical footprint and can make those choices fit. What it does 8KG is an interactive carry-on shared by a person and their AI. The user copies one short request into the AI connected to the page. Through WebMCP, the AI reads an anonymous item catalog and sends back only accepted item IDs. The browser then packs those items into a 6 × 8 spatial grid while enforcing an 8 kg cabin limit. The result appears immediately as a visual suitcase rather than a text recommendation. The collaboration continues after the first pack. A user can remove an item or mark it Keep this . A later AI repack sees the public bag state and must work around the user’s locked decision. The page protects those locks even if the model omits them. No prompt, identity, destination, conversation history, private facts, or AI reasoning is accepted by the website. There is no application backend or embedded LLM. Bag state lives only in the current tab. How we built it React 19 and TypeScript for the client-only application. WebMCP tools registered through feature-detected document.modelContext or navigator.modelContext . A constrained catalog of 18 items with weight and grid dimensions. A deterministic first-fit packing engine that validates weight, space, unique IDs, and user locks. Six narrow tools: get_available_items , get_bag_state , pack_bag , add_items , remove_items , and reset_bag . ChatGPT Sites for hosting, with no database, API key, analytics, cookies, or server-side profile. The visual controls and WebMCP tools call the same functions in lib/packing.ts . Challenges The hardest part was finding a use of AI memory that felt meaningfully different from ordinary controls. Like Comment Story Updates gotothearchive home inside the design folder lightbox preview of one design example the workbench the workbench expanded view stats taste view About gotothearchive Inspiration Context is probably the biggest problem with agents right now, and almost every product treats it as something the agent should quietly accumulate on its own. I think context is only the surface of the real problem anyway. Underneath it is taste, meaning the judgment behind what you save, what you throw out, and what you keep sending back for another pass, and an agent that is actually useful to you has to be working from that rather than from a summary of your last ten prompts. That is where gotothearchive started. You own the Archive, and the same interface where you organize it is the one that decides, task by task, what any agent is allowed to touch. What it does The Archive is a plain visual workspace for your images, links, documents, notes, and prior work, and it is genuinely useful on its own with no agent connected. When you do want help, you open Agent Access and pick, per folder/region, whether an agent can view, contribute to, or edit that part of the Archive for the current task. WebMCP takes those grants and compiles them straight into the agent's tool surface. Which tools appear on document.modelContext, and which regions are enumerable inside each tool's input schema, is exactly what you have granted at that moment. Narrow a grant and the tools re-register with tighter schemas. Revoke one and the affected tools are removed, not left as shells that fail on call, and the server rejects any request built against the schema the agent was still holding. The agent works from the context you allowed and returns its result to the Workbench as an immutable, versioned artifact. You see what it retrieved, which references actually shaped the output, who produced it, and what was denied along the way. You annotate a version, mark a region on it, approve it, request changes, or reject it. If the artifact is a component it runs live in the Workbench in an isolated iframe, so you can click through what the agent built rather than looking at a screenshot of it. From your feedback the agent can then propose a taste signal, tied to the exact annotations behind it, and nothing enters your profile until you confirm it. You edit the wording, rescope it from personal to a project, reject it, or accept it, and a confirmed signal starts steering retrieval on the next task. How I built it The build restarted more than once early on. Every time I laid out the full scope an agent would decide it was too ambitious and talk me into dropping something that turned out to be load-bearing, and I would end up rebuilding it a session later. It runs on Cloudflare Workers with a single Durable Object holding the SQLite database for every Archive, region, task, grant, artifact, annotation, taste signal, provenance record, and audit event. One object is a real ceiling, but it also means that several people and their agents hitting the same Archive concurrently stay consistent by construction, with no coordination code, which is what makes the shared demo safe. R2 stores original media, and Workers AI produces the structured visual descriptions where they earn their keep. Since some of this incurs billed usage I calculated a quota for each user as well while we're in our "Beta" state. Retrieval is deliberately not a managed search service. It resolves the authorized set first, then fuses full-text search, recency, and the context graph and multiplies through authority, curation, and confirmed-taste priors. Something you cannot see is absent from the candidate list, never present with a low score. Provenance is kept as three record types that never get collapsed into one. Influence means a reference shaped the result, access means it was retrieved for the task, and denial means it was unavailable, and only the first two show up in ordinary provenance. Taste corrections are bi-temporal. A materially changed signal is not overwritten. The old row is superseded and the new one points back at it, so the Taste page can show how a judgement moved over time. The part I got most wrong was taste itself. I built it server-side first, with a classifier that bucketed your annotations by dimension and a template that wrote the preference sentence from the most common words in the grouped notes. The output was things like "Leans away from the current typography on posters", which is vague and is exactly the kind of background inference this project exists to remove. So I deleted the whole path. A taste signal now exists only because an agent read your feedback and named the pattern with cited evidence through propose_taste_signal , or because you wrote it yourself, and the server's only remaining job there is to keep the evidence and confidence honest when you later edit a note. A bit less frequent, but it's high quality now, just takes longer to start curating. Partway through I ran the deployed site through an external WebMCP audit and fixed what it flagged, most of it around when tools register on first paint and where instruction-shaped text was leaking into tool descriptions instead of staying declarative. It tightened the surface noticeably. Challenges I ran into The hardest was making permission real in every layer at once. The interface, the compiled tool surface, retrieval, the context graph, and server authorization all had to agree, including in the second immediately after a revoke, and any one of them lagging would have made the whole claim a demo trick. Multimodal context was the next one. Images are how people actually communicate taste, but WebMCP is shaped around JSON-compatible inputs and outputs. I had to resolve media through short-lived signed URLs, keep the canonical original separate from every derived caption and palette, give the agent a structured representation it can reason over, and record the provenance of every derived field so a wrong value is visible rather than mysterious. Component artifacts added their own problem, since running agent-authored HTML meant an opaque-origin sandbox with a locked-down content security policy that still had to permit real fonts and the CDN scripts a component genuinely needs. And revocation had to go deeper than hiding items. A taste signal is a compressed version of the material behind it, so if a folder taught a preference, revoking that folder has to withdraw the preference from what the agent retrieves, not just the folder's contents. What I learned WebMCP can be much more than a cleaner way for an agent to operate a website.
