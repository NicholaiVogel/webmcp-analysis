# FROZEN SCORING RUBRIC (pre-registered before any Stage 1 review)

Scores are integers 1-10 on each of the FOUR official criteria. Every score carries:
rationale, evidence_surfaces, confidence (0-1). Reviewers use the full scale and these
band anchors verbatim. Category neutrality is absolute: a game, art piece, music tool,
CRM, weird toy, and developer tool are all eligible for 10/10 on every criterion if they
satisfy that criterion exceptionally well FOR WHAT THEY ARE TRYING TO BE.
No aesthetic penalties. Signs of hurried AI work matter only as actual product-quality
problems (incoherence, broken flow, placeholder content), never as style.

## WebMCP Leverage — what does WebMCP change for this product?
Judge the delta WebMCP makes to reliability, precision, structured state access,
shared human-agent state, repeatability, and capability. Tool COUNT is not leverage.
- 9-10 TRANSFORMATIVE: the intended experience is impractical without the structured
  agent surface; human and agent share live product state; the collaboration could not
  be replicated comparably well by a general agent driving the UI.
- 7-8 MAJOR DELTA: without WebMCP a general agent is markedly less reliable/precise
  here; the agent is central to the product, not a bolt-on.
- 5-6 MEANINGFUL DELTA: existing capability packaged into a genuinely better product
  experience via structured tools; same outcome otherwise possible with real friction.
- 3-4 MINOR DELTA: generic browser automation achieves comparable outcomes; WebMCP is
  a convenience layer.
- 1-2 COSMETIC: exposes an action a UI button already offers; no meaningful change.

## Execution — is the actual product coherent, intentional, complete for its scope?
Stage 1 scores EXECUTION_CLAIMED: coherence/intentionality/completeness as evidenced by
the packet (description + packaging + video frames/transcript). Thin evidence caps the
score (end-to-end credit requires end-to-end evidence) and lowers confidence.
Stage 2 may rescore as EXECUTION_OBSERVED; latest evidence-backed score replaces, never
sums.
- 9-10: all surfaces agree; central workflow demonstrably completes; states handled;
  feels finished for its stated scope.
- 7-8: strong coherence and intentionality; central workflow appears functional; minor
  unfinished edges.
- 5-6: plausibly real with visible gaps; scope unclear or core not demonstrated
  end-to-end.
- 3-4: thin prototype; contradictions between surfaces; placeholder content.
- 1-2: non-functional or incoherent for its claimed scope.

## Creativity & Ambition — novelty of concept and interaction model
Judge ONLY: novelty of the concept, novelty of the interaction model, originality
relative to existing concepts, ambition, memorable/surprising use of the medium, and
the depth with which the concept is pursued. WebMCP indispensability belongs under
WebMCP Leverage, NOT here: a project can be extremely creative even if another agent
API could theoretically have enabled something similar.
- 9-10: genuinely novel concept or interaction model; ambitious scope pursued deeply
  and coherently; memorable.
- 7-8: fresh synthesis or notably original interaction; ambitious for a hackathon.
- 5-6: competent take on a known category with a real new angle.
- 3-4: derivative; novelty mostly surface details.
- 1-2: clone/template with trivial changes.
"That the category exists" is never a penalty; judge the submitted concept.

## Potential Impact — credible value for the intended audience
Judge: is there a real audience; is there a real problem, desire, or use case for that
audience; does the demonstrated solution genuinely address it. NO commercial adoption,
monetization, market pull, or startup viability is required. Entertainment, play, art,
creative expression, learning, niche hobby use, experimentation, and community value
are legitimate forms of impact. A tiny audience fully justifies a top score.
- 9-10: specific real audience with a real problem/desire; solution demonstrably
  addresses it; credible value, appeal, usefulness, or meaning for that audience.
- 7-8: clear audience and problem, good fit, credible value.
- 5-6: identifiable audience, looser fit or partial address.
- 3-4: speculative audience or strained fit; impact mostly aspirational.
- 1-2: no coherent audience/problem pairing.
Audience size is NOT a multiplier.

## Aggregate & ranking
aggregate = leverage + execution + impact + creativity (4-40). Nothing else enters.
Tie-breaks: Leverage, then Execution, then Impact, then Creativity, then higher
combined evidence confidence, then slug alphabetical. See PROTOCOL.md Ranking section.

Like Comment Story Updates Inspiration I’m a security practitioner, and I keep seeing investigations reduced to an LLM summary. The summary helps, but it can leave the analyst a step away from the evidence. Meanwhile, people are building personal agent setups with their own models, memory, skills, and runbooks. I wanted a workbench where you could bring that agent into the case without giving it the final say. What it does WATCH//FLOOR is a visual WebMCP workbench for escalated cases. Evidence sufficiency, restricted telemetry, response approval, and case closure stay with the analyst. That works for short tasks, but long-running projects need something more durable. The original idea started as a personal workspace where an AI agent could retrieve technical notes, project context, and decisions. Over time, the bigger problem became obvious: the real issue was not giving one agent more memory. It was giving humans and multiple agents a shared place where project state could persist independently of any single conversation. SharedState was built around that idea. Instead of treating chat history as the source of truth, the project stores tasks, decisions, knowledge, activity, proposals, and project context as structured workspace state. Agents can come and go, while the project remains. Agents come and go. Your workspace remembers. What it does SharedState is a self-hosted workspace platform where humans and AI agents collaborate on the same persistent project state. Humans use the visual interface to review work, manage projects, and authorize important decisions. External AI agents connect through WebMCP and can directly discover workspaces, inspect structured context, read open work and activity, create and update tasks, add knowledge, and propose decisions without having to operate the interface through clicks. A persistent Resident Agent acts as a read-only continuity layer. It interprets the same workspace differently depending on what the user is looking at: Tasks → priorities, blockers, dependencies, next work Decisions → confirmed direction, proposals, conflicts, missing decisions Knowledge → key facts, gaps, contradictions, open questions Activity → what changed and why it matters These analyses are persisted, so simply navigating the interface does not repeatedly call the model. SharedState also includes a specialist Import Agent for bootstrapping existing public GitHub projects. Rather than ingesting an entire repository blindly, it progressively explores only the evidence it needs—README files, metadata, issues, documentation, configuration, and selected source files. The result is an evidence-backed preview containing suggested tasks, knowledge, decision proposals, open questions, risks, and provenance. A human reviews that preview before anything becomes authoritative workspace state. The core principle is simple: External agents act. Humans authorize. Resident agents interpret. The public deployment can sit behind Cloudflare Tunnel while the application itself only needs to expose HTTP internally. That quickly becomes difficult to reason about: context grows indefinitely, agents can disagree, and different agents do not naturally share conversation history. The solution was to make the structured workspace the source of truth instead. Another challenge was authority. AI agents should be able to contribute useful work without silently turning recommendations into project decisions. SharedState therefore separates decision proposals from confirmed decisions. An external agent can propose a direction, but human approval is what makes it authoritative. We also had to separate different kinds of agents clearly. Each has a narrowly defined role and authority boundary. Repository importing created another challenge: how to understand a real project without sending the entire repository to a model. The importer uses progressive exploration instead. It maintains a compact understanding of the project, identifies the most valuable unresolved question, and reads another source only when that evidence is expected to answer something specific. Finally, WebMCP compatibility itself required some debugging. The site tools were correctly registered and visible, but tool invocation depended on the active model/runtime. The distinction between facts, hypotheses, questions, proposals, and confirmed decisions is something we consider central to making agent collaboration trustworthy. For this challenge, we pushed that further: what if an AI agent could actually use Terrain alongside you? What it does Terrain is a map-first prospecting and sales platform. Users can discover local businesses, inspect their digital presence, enrich leads, analyze competitors, generate briefs, choose services, and move opportunities through a visual pipeline. The goal is to move from: discovery → research → analysis → service → outreach → delivery How we built it Terrain is built with React, TypeScript, Vite, shadcn/ui, Supabase, and AI-powered workflows. Terrain was already an MCP server before this challenge, so AI clients could use its backend capabilities completely headlessly. With WebMCP, we added something different: frontend tools for the browser agent itself. The agent can read the map state, move and zoom the map, frame results, focus on businesses or leads, switch map views, and navigate different parts of the Terrain interface. Those frontend tools work alongside the same backend functionality Terrain already exposes. Challenges The biggest challenge was keeping the frontend synchronized with what the agent was doing in the backend. If an agent finds a business but the map never updates, the experience feels broken. We had to make sure agent actions, map state, discovered businesses, and visible UI all stayed connected without building a separate version of Terrain just for AI. What we learned Our biggest takeaway was that WebMCP becomes especially powerful when you expose frontend functionality, not just backend functions. MCP lets an agent use Terrain headlessly. WebMCP lets the browser agent actually operate the application with the user. Instead of relying only on screenshots and mouse clicks, the agent gets structured tools for navigating and manipulating the real interface. What's next We want to keep expanding Terrain's automation, agent workflows, bulk prospecting, outreach, and AI-powered services so a single developer or small agency can operate with much more leverage. The hard part was not learning one single thing. It was keeping up with dozens of small things every day: checking glucose, remembering meals and insulin, and trying to work out why the same routine sometimes produced a completely different result. \n\n A lot of the tools I found felt old, expensive, or disconnected from how I actually wanted to manage everything. At the same time, ChatGPT and other AI tools were becoming genuinely useful. I kept wondering what would happen if an agent could work inside a diabetes app with me, using the information already on the screen instead of making me repeat it all in a separate chat.\nThat idea became BetaTrace. \n\n What it does \n\n BetaTrace is a Type 1 diabetes dashboard that both a person and a browser-based agent can use. \n\n The dashboard lets someone explore glucose readings, meals, insulin entries, trends, and time in range. The WebMCP tools let an agent work with that same demo data: \n\n get_demo_state reads the current synthetic data, including seven days of glucose readings and logged meals. \n\n log_demo_entry records a glucose reading, meal, or insulin dose that the user says has already happened. \n\n reset_demo_data clears added entries and restores the original sample data, but only after the user confirms it. \n\n For example, I can ask an agent to graph my glucose and meals from the past week, log a new meal, and then generate the graph again. The new meal appears in both the dashboard and the agent’s updated graph. \n\n Without WebMCP, the agent would have to inspect the page, guess what each control does, and click through the interface. BetaTrace gives it clear tools with defined inputs and responses. \n\n How I built it \n\n BetaTrace is a React app hosted on Vercel. \n\n The page registers its tools directly with document.modelContext.registerTool(). There is no separate WebMCP server and no polyfill. The dashboard and the agent tools use the same validation and local data, so an entry logged by the agent appears on screen immediately. \n\n For the seven-day graph, get_demo_state returns 336 chronological glucose readings along with the meals recorded during that period. This gives the agent enough real structure to produce a useful graph instead of a rough summary. \n\n The public demo only uses synthetic data stored in the browser. Its WebMCP tools cannot reach Supabase, private accounts, or real patient information. BetaTrace can record an insulin dose that the user says they already took, but it will not calculate or recommend one. \n\n Challenges I ran into \n\n The first version could return a summary of the demo, but that was not enough to make a proper seven-day graph. We had to expose the underlying timeline while keeping the response clear and predictable for the agent. \n\n Keeping the agent and the visible dashboard in sync was another challenge. A successful tool call was not enough. If the dashboard did not update at the same moment, the whole idea of working together inside one page fell apart. \n\n The safety boundaries also took some thought. Health data is not a good place for vague permissions. We limited the demo to synthetic local data, validated every write, blocked dose recommendations, and made reset require confirmation. \n\n Accomplishments that I'm proud of \n\n The part I am most proud of is the full loop. \n\n An agent can read seven days of glucose and meal data, turn it into an interactive graph, log a meal supplied by the user, and then produce a new graph containing that meal. The person sees the same change inside BetaTrace. \n\n It sounds simple when written out, but that shared state is what makes the project feel like collaboration instead of ordinary browser automation. \n\n I am also proud that the safety limitations are part of the design rather than a disclaimer added at the end. The demo is useful enough to prove the idea without pretending that an experimental agent should be making treatment decisions. \n\n What we learned \n\n A button exposed as a tool is not automatically useful. \n\n The tool has to match something a person would naturally ask for. It needs understandable inputs, useful output, proper validation, and clear limits. Building the WebMCP layer forced us to think more carefully about what BetaTrace actually knows and what an agent should be allowed to do with it. \n\n We also learned that the best WebMCP moments are visible. When the agent logs a meal and the dashboard changes immediately, there is no need for a long explanation. You can see that the person and the agent are working with the same application. \n\n What's next for Betatrace \n\n The current version stays inside a synthetic guest demo on purpose. \n\n The next step would be optional authenticated accounts with narrow permissions, a visible history of agent actions, and confirmation for anything sensitive. Users should always be able to see what the agent accessed and what it changed. \n\n Future tools could prepare appointment summaries, compare glucose responses after recurring meals, and help someone collect questions for their care team. They would still stop short of diagnosis or automatic dosing advice. \n\n The goal is not to hand diabetes management over to an agent. It is to reduce some of the repetitive work while keeping the person in control. \n\n \n\n \n Built With \n\n claude codex css html javascript react tailwind vercel vite vitest webmcp \n \n\n \n Try it out \n\n \n \n \n betatrace.vercel.app \n \n \n \n GitHub Repo \n \n \n \n\n \n\n \n\n \n \n Submitted to\n \n\n \n \n \n \n \n\n \n \n The WebMCP Challenge \n \n\n\n \n\n\n \n\n \n \n\n \n \n Created by\n \n\n \n \n \n \n \n \n \n \n \n \n\n \n David Cilliers \n \n \n\n \n\n \n \n \n \n\n \n \n \n \n \n \n \n \n \n\n \n \n \n \n \n\n \n\n \n\n\n \n \n \n \n \n\n \n \n \n \n \n \n \n \n \n Like\n 2 \n \n \n\n\n \n 2 people like this:\n \n\n \n \n \n \n \n \n \n \n\n \n\n\n \n \n \n \n Share this project:\n \n\n\n \n \n \n \n \n \n \n \n \n\n \n\n \n \n \n \n \n \n \n \n \n \n\n \n\n \n \n \n \n\n \n \n \n\n \n \n \n \n \n\n\n\n \n \n \n Updates \n\n \n \n \n \n \n\n \n \n \n \n \n \n David Cilliers \n started this project\n \n —\n \n 2 days ago \n \n \n\n
