shared human-agent state, repeatability, and capability. Tool COUNT is not leverage.

API could theoretically have enabled something similar.

  and coherently; memorable.
- 7-8: fresh synthesis or notably original interaction; ambitious for a hackathon.
- 5-6: competent take on a known category with a real new angle.
- 3-4: derivative; novelty mostly surface details.

Meanwhile, every AI tutor we knew worked beside an educational tool, never inside it. The learner plays with a simulation, then manually types what happened into a chat; the model reasons from a stale summary and, worst of all, happily does the thinking the student was supposed to do. That combination suggested a question: what if the tutor lived inside the lab page itself — reading the same live bench, proposing evidence, but structurally unable to take over the scientific method? A tutor that is bidirectional: the agent sees what the learner actually did, and the learner sees and approves everything the agent actually proposes. LabMate is our answer. What it does LabMate is a 15-minute high-school physics inquiry around one LED circuit, following the scientific method: Predict → Design → Evidence → Reflect . The learner states a prediction ("if resistance decreases, the LED gets brighter because current increases") and locks it. The hypothesis and the final conclusion are learner-owned : visible to the agent, writable by no tool. Through WebMCP, the agent sees the exact live bench — voltages, saved trials, the current stage, model limits, and the page's revision number. It proposes one fair experiment at a time. Once a controlled pair exists, the page compares trials and reports the valid pairs. The learner — not the agent — answers four reflection prompts (what changed, what stayed constant, what the measurements show, whether the prediction was supported) and locks the conclusion. Understanding provenance often requires knowing where to look, opening specialized tools, and interpreting technical metadata. \n\n So we asked: \n\n \n What if understanding the history of content was as easy as asking a question? \n \n\n That inspired VerifyHere — an exploration of making Content Credentials conversational with WebMCP . \n\n What it does \n\n VerifyHere lets you talk to the provenance of your content. \n\n Instead of manually digging through metadata, you can simply ask: \n\n \n "Where did this photo come from?" \n\n "Has this image been edited?" \n\n "What changed?" \n\n "Take me back to the original." \n \n\n VerifyHere uses WebMCP to connect these natural-language questions with the underlying provenance information. \n\n The experience follows content across its journey: \n\n Capture → Verify → Edit → Trace → Recover \n\n For example, a photographer can capture an image with Content Credentials, inspect its provenance, edit it in Photoshop, and then explore how the edited asset relates to the original. \n\n The goal isn't to tell people what to trust. It's to give them more context about how content came to be . \n\n How we built it \n\n We built VerifyHere as a web experience combining WebMCP with C2PA Content Credentials . \n\n Our application exposes provenance capabilities as tools that an AI agent can discover and invoke through WebMCP. \n\n This means the user doesn't need to understand C2PA manifests, cryptographic signatures, or metadata structures. \n\n They can express their intent naturally: \n\n \n "Tell me where this image came from." \n \n\n WebMCP can then connect that request to the appropriate capability in VerifyHere, inspect the available provenance information, and return an understandable answer. \n\n When an edited asset contains provenance linking it to an earlier asset, VerifyHere can also help users explore that chain and move back toward the original. \n\n This turns Content Credentials from something users inspect into something they can interact with conversationally . \n\n Challenges we ran into \n\n One of our biggest challenges was provenance at capture . \n\n We experimented with different mobile capture workflows and quickly discovered that real-world C2PA interoperability isn't always as straightforward as the specification makes it appear. \n\n Credentials can behave differently across capture applications, exports, file transfers, editing workflows, and verification experiences. \n\n That challenge actually reinforced the motivation behind VerifyHere: \n\n \n Users shouldn't have to understand the implementation details of provenance technology to benefit from it. \n \n\n We also thought carefully about the distinction between edited content and untrustworthy content . \n\n An image being edited doesn't automatically make it misleading. Photography and creative work naturally involve editing. \n\n Content provenance isn't about labeling content as "good" or "bad." It's about providing transparency and context , so people can understand what happened and make their own informed decisions. \n\n What we learned \n\n Our biggest learning was that Content Credentials become much more approachable when we stop thinking about provenance purely as metadata people need to inspect . \n\n Instead, provenance can become context that agents can reason over . \n\n WebMCP provides an exciting bridge between these two worlds. \n\n The underlying provenance can remain structured, machine-readable, and cryptographically verifiable, while the user experience can become as simple as asking: \n\n \n "Tell me the story behind this image." \n \n\n We also learned how important durability and interoperability are. \n\n Content travels everywhere — through editing tools, websites, social platforms, downloads, screenshots, and even into the physical world. Provenance becomes most useful when people can continue discovering that history wherever the content travels. \n\n What's next for VerifyHere \n\n We imagine VerifyHere going far beyond inspecting individual images. \n\n The same conversational provenance experience could extend to: \n\n \n Creative libraries \n Published media \n Video \n AI-generated and AI-edited content \n Social content \n Physical and printed media \n \n\n Imagine asking: \n\n \n "Which photos in my library are original camera captures?" \n \n\n or: \n\n \n "Show me everything derived from this original." \n \n\n or simply: \n\n \n "Can I learn more about where this came from?" \n \n\n Agents could help creators trace their work, understand how it has changed, discover missing provenance, find originals, and receive attribution as their content travels across the internet. \n\n Our vision is simple: provenance shouldn't be something you have to hunt for. It should be as easy as asking. \n\n \n\n \n Built With \n\n c2pa node.js webmcp \n \n\n \n Try it out \n\n \n \n \n navyaagarwal-tech.github.io \n \n \n \n\n \n\n \n\n \n \n Submitted to\n \n\n \n \n \n \n \n\n \n \n The WebMCP Challenge \n \n\n\n \n\n\n \n\n \n \n\n \n \n Created by\n \n\n \n \n \n \n \n \n \n \n \n \n\n \n Navya Agarwal \n \n \n\n \n\n \n \n \n \n\n \n \n \n \n \n \n \n \n \n\n \n \n \n \n \n\n \n\n \n\n\n \n \n \n \n \n\n \n \n \n \n \n \n \n \n \n Like\n \n \n\n\n \n \n \n\n \n \n\n \n\n\n \n \n \n \n Share this project:\n \n\n\n \n \n \n \n \n \n \n \n \n\n \n\n \n \n \n \n \n \n \n \n \n \n\n \n\n \n \n \n \n\n \n \n \n\n \n \n \n \n \n\n\n\n \n \n \n Updates \n\n \n \n \n \n \n\n \n \n \n \n \n \n Navya Agarwal \n started this project\n \n —\n \n 2 days ago \n \n \n\n
