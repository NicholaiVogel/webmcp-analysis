# FROZEN SCORING RUBRIC (pre-registered before any Stage 1 review)

Scores are integers 1-10 on each of the FOUR official criteria. Every score carries:
rationale, evidence_surfaces, confidence (0-1). Reviewers use the full scale and these
band anchors verbatim. Category neutrality is absolute: a game, art piece, music tool,
CRM, weird toy, and developer tool are all eligible for 10/10 on every criterion if they
satisfy that criterion exceptionally well FOR WHAT THEY ARE TRYING TO BE.
No aesthetic penalties. Signs of hurried AI work matter only as actual product-quality
problems (incoherence, broken flow, placeholder content), never as style.

## WebMCP Leverage — what does WebMCP change for this product?
Judge the delta WebMCP makes to reliability, precision, structured state access,
shared human-agent state, repeatability, and capability. Tool COUNT is not leverage.
- 9-10 TRANSFORMATIVE: the intended experience is impractical without the structured
  agent surface; human and agent share live product state; the collaboration could not
  be replicated comparably well by a general agent driving the UI.
- 7-8 MAJOR DELTA: without WebMCP a general agent is markedly less reliable/precise
  here; the agent is central to the product, not a bolt-on.
- 5-6 MEANINGFUL DELTA: existing capability packaged into a genuinely better product
  experience via structured tools; same outcome otherwise possible with real friction.
- 3-4 MINOR DELTA: generic browser automation achieves comparable outcomes; WebMCP is
  a convenience layer.
- 1-2 COSMETIC: exposes an action a UI button already offers; no meaningful change.

## Execution — is the actual product coherent, intentional, complete for its scope?
Stage 1 scores EXECUTION_CLAIMED: coherence/intentionality/completeness as evidenced by
the packet (description + packaging + video frames/transcript). Thin evidence caps the
score (end-to-end credit requires end-to-end evidence) and lowers confidence.
Stage 2 may rescore as EXECUTION_OBSERVED; latest evidence-backed score replaces, never
sums.
- 9-10: all surfaces agree; central workflow demonstrably completes; states handled;
  feels finished for its stated scope.
- 7-8: strong coherence and intentionality; central workflow appears functional; minor
  unfinished edges.
- 5-6: plausibly real with visible gaps; scope unclear or core not demonstrated
  end-to-end.
- 3-4: thin prototype; contradictions between surfaces; placeholder content.
- 1-2: non-functional or incoherent for its claimed scope.

## Creativity & Ambition — novelty of concept and interaction model
Judge ONLY: novelty of the concept, novelty of the interaction model, originality
relative to existing concepts, ambition, memorable/surprising use of the medium, and
the depth with which the concept is pursued. WebMCP indispensability belongs under
WebMCP Leverage, NOT here: a project can be extremely creative even if another agent
API could theoretically have enabled something similar.
- 9-10: genuinely novel concept or interaction model; ambitious scope pursued deeply
  and coherently; memorable.
- 7-8: fresh synthesis or notably original interaction; ambitious for a hackathon.
- 5-6: competent take on a known category with a real new angle.
- 3-4: derivative; novelty mostly surface details.
- 1-2: clone/template with trivial changes.
"That the category exists" is never a penalty; judge the submitted concept.

## Potential Impact — credible value for the intended audience
Judge: is there a real audience; is there a real problem, desire, or use case for that
audience; does the demonstrated solution genuinely address it. NO commercial adoption,
monetization, market pull, or startup viability is required. Entertainment, play, art,
creative expression, learning, niche hobby use, experimentation, and community value
are legitimate forms of impact. A tiny audience fully justifies a top score.
- 9-10: specific real audience with a real problem/desire; solution demonstrably
  addresses it; credible value, appeal, usefulness, or meaning for that audience.
- 7-8: clear audience and problem, good fit, credible value.
- 5-6: identifiable audience, looser fit or partial address.
- 3-4: speculative audience or strained fit; impact mostly aspirational.
- 1-2: no coherent audience/problem pairing.
Audience size is NOT a multiplier.

## Aggregate & ranking
aggregate = leverage + execution + impact + creativity (4-40). Nothing else enters.
Tie-breaks: Leverage, then Execution, then Impact, then Creativity, then higher
combined evidence confidence, then slug alphabetical. See PROTOCOL.md Ranking section.

Inspiration Most AI experiences revolve around a chat box: the human asks, the agent answers, and one side usually has all the important context. We wanted to explore something more physical, cooperative, and playful. CROSSTALK began with a simple question: What if WebMCP was not merely an integration layer, but a core game mechanic? We drew inspiration from cooperative bomb-defusal games, escape rooms, radio communication, and the tension of working with someone who knows things you cannot see. One browser AI. Six specialist perspectives. You decide. Like Comment Story Updates One COO and 6 Ai Assistant Agents Inspiration Founders often need to make consequential decisions with incomplete evidence, changing constraints and too many disconnected conversations. AI Boardroom puts a founder and a browser assistant in one shared decision room where specialist perspectives, evidence and trade-offs remain visible. What it does AI Boardroom gives one browser AI six specialist perspectives: Product, Evidence, Commercial, Operations, Technology and Chair. WebMCP lets the assistant read the live brief, request named roles, post evidence-linked contributions, respond when constraints change, and retrieve the exact decision record. The central interaction is deliberately human-controlled. A fictional launch budget changes from R20,000 to R8,000; the previous recommendation becomes stale, the assistant reads the new revision, and a new recommendation can be prepared without rewriting the earlier record. The founder reviews alternatives and reservations, then approves, rejects or requests revision in the visible page form. This is stateful collaboration rather than a detached chatbot transcript. The app does not run six independent AI workers or call a model API. One browser assistant adopts the roles and the human retains the final decision. How we built it The app is built with the OpenAI Sites React and TypeScript scaffold, Vinext and D1. Native document.modelContext.registerTool exposes seven WebMCP tools: start_session, get_boardroom_context, update_agenda, request_perspective, add_contribution, prepare_recommendation and get_decision_record. UI actions and WebMCP tools share the same API and persistence logic. Mutation inputs use expectedRevision for optimistic concurrency and operationId for safe retries. Evidence is stored with the relevant contribution and rendered as untrusted text. The visible review channel is a workflow boundary, not proof of human identity. Challenges The design challenge was making an AI council useful without pretending that six hidden agents or automatic consensus exist. We made the roles explicit, preserved disagreement, invalidated recommendations when the brief changed, and kept approval out of the WebMCP tool surface. A second challenge was handling uncertain browser or network outcomes without duplicating or overwriting decisions. What we learned WebMCP is most valuable when the agent can operate on the same visible, stateful interface as the human. Structured tools make the collaboration legible, while revision checks, evidence snapshots and a human review stop make the limits legible too. What's next Production use would require stronger identity and access controls, consent design, privacy and retention rules, abuse and rate-limit controls, independent security review, and integrations governed by explicit human approval. Disclosure All briefs, budgets, evidence and decisions in this project are fictional. The app does not make purchases, send messages, or change external systems. Browser-generated council text is created at runtime and is not preseeded consensus. An agent native motion design workspace bridging visual timeline editing with WebMCP driven key-framing, SVG orchestration, and real-time animation. Like Comment Story Updates Inspiration Motion design tools are powerful, but even small animation changes often require repetitive timeline editing, precise layer targeting, and multiple export steps. Browser agents usually struggle with these tools because they must guess what the user selected and interact through fragile screen coordinates. I built KineticFlow to explore a better model: the designer points to what they mean, while the AI agent handles the structured motion work. WebMCP makes this possible by giving agents direct, typed access to the application’s canvas and timeline state. Live app: kinetic-flow.netlify.app Source code: github.com/SamyakJ05/KineticFlow Demo video: Watch on YouTube What it does KineticFlow is a browser-native vector motion design studio where humans and AI agents work on the same canvas. A designer can select a layer and ask an agent to: Inspect the complete scene and current human selection Add rectangles, circles, hearts, stars, and sparkles Refine a layer’s position, size, color, rotation, scale, or opacity Generate multi-track animation keyframes Apply easing curves and spring physics Load polished motion presets Play, pause, or seek the live preview Export the animation as CSS, animated SVG, or Lottie JSON Every operation appears in a live Tool Stream with its inputs, structured result, status, and execution time. Designers can continue editing manually, drag objects, adjust properties and keyframes, or undo an agent’s changes. When WebMCP is unavailable, an in-page co-pilot and manual tool tester use the same underlying handlers, making the complete experience accessible in standard browsers. How I built it KineticFlow is built with: React TypeScript Vite Zustand Tailwind CSS SVG Netlify The WebMCP integration registers seven imperative tools through document.modelContext.registerTool() . Each tool includes: A focused name and human-readable title A natural-language description A strict JSON Schema inputSchema Input validation Structured return values Cancellation-signal support Read-only annotations where appropriate The main architectural component is a shared Zustand editor store. The canvas, timeline, property controls, in-page co-pilot, and browser agent all read and update the same state. The getCanvasScene tool returns more than a scene snapshot. It also provides: The layer selected by the human The current playhead position Playback status Loop state This enables natural handoffs such as: Make this coral, add a spring pulse, and play it. The user does not need to find or provide an internal layer ID. A requestAnimationFrame loop drives the live preview, while a custom interpolation engine evaluates position, scale, rotation, opacity, width, and height tracks. The exporter compiles the shared scene into reusable CSS and HTML, standalone animated SVG, or Lottie JSON. The application is deployed on Netlify with SPA routing, security headers, asset caching, and an explicit tools=(self) permissions policy. Challenges I ran into WebMCP is an emerging specification, so one major challenge was keeping the implementation aligned with the latest API. Earlier experiments used navigator.modelContext and a parameters field, while the current specification uses document.modelContext and inputSchema . I migrated the primary implementation while retaining a compatibility path for earlier experimental builds. Another challenge was designing tools that were useful to an agent without removing control from the designer. Small atomic tools were precise, but agents also needed enough context to understand the user’s intent. Returning the current human selection alongside the scene created a much more natural collaboration model. I also had to ensure that tool responses reflected the updated state rather than a stale snapshot. Playback and animation handlers now read the store again after mutations before returning structured results. Finally, animation exports required translating the same internal motion model into three different formats while preserving timing, transforms, opacity, and layer geometry. Accomplishments that I’m proud of Built a complete motion-design product rather than only a technical proof of concept Implemented seven structured WebMCP tools Created a shared-context workflow between human selection and agent action Added multi-track keyframes, easing curves, and spring-motion interpolation Made agent operations visible through a real-time execution stream Produced CSS, animated SVG, and Lottie exports Added a fallback co-pilot and manual tester powered by the same handlers Created automated smoke tests that register and execute all seven tools Deployed the application publicly with WebMCP-compatible permissions What I learned I learned that agent-native applications work best when agents receive semantic actions and meaningful application state—not just access to buttons and screen coordinates. WebMCP is especially valuable for creative software because human intent is often communicated through selection. A designer clicking a layer is already providing useful context. Exposing that selection to the agent allows short, natural instructions to become precise structured operations. I also learned that tool descriptions, schemas, validation, and return values are part of the product experience. Well-designed tools make agents more reliable while making the system easier for humans to understand and trust. What’s next for KineticFlow: AI Motion Design Studio Planned improvements include: Agent-generated motion plans that designers can preview before applying Multi-layer selection and batch animation operations Natural-language editing of easing curves and spring parameters SVG import and advanced path manipulation Collaborative history with agent-versus-human change attribution Reusable brand and motion-style systems Additional production exports and framework integrations Declarative WebMCP support as the specification matures The long-term goal is to make KineticFlow a creative workspace where humans provide taste, direction, and final judgment while agents handle repetitive, structured animation work.
