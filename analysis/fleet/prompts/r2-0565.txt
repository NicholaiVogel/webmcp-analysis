Scores are integers 1-10 on each of the FOUR official criteria. Every score carries:
rationale, evidence_surfaces, confidence (0-1). Reviewers use the full scale and these

No aesthetic penalties. Signs of hurried AI work matter only as actual product-quality
problems (incoherence, broken flow, placeholder content), never as style.

## WebMCP Leverage — what does WebMCP change for this product?
Judge the delta WebMCP makes to reliability, precision, structured state access,
shared human-agent state, repeatability, and capability. Tool COUNT is not leverage.
- 9-10 TRANSFORMATIVE: the intended experience is impractical without the structured
  agent surface; human and agent share live product state; the collaboration could not
  be replicated comparably well by a general agent driving the UI.
- 7-8 MAJOR DELTA: without WebMCP a general agent is markedly less reliable/precise
  here; the agent is central to the product, not a bolt-on.
- 5-6 MEANINGFUL DELTA: existing capability packaged into a genuinely better product
  experience via structured tools; same outcome otherwise possible with real friction.
- 3-4 MINOR DELTA: generic browser automation achieves comparable outcomes; WebMCP is
  a convenience layer.
- 1-2 COSMETIC: exposes an action a UI button already offers; no meaningful change.

## Execution — is the actual product coherent, intentional, complete for its scope?
Stage 1 scores EXECUTION_CLAIMED: coherence/intentionality/completeness as evidenced by
the packet (description + packaging + video frames/transcript). Thin evidence caps the
score (end-to-end credit requires end-to-end evidence) and lowers confidence.
Stage 2 may rescore as EXECUTION_OBSERVED; latest evidence-backed score replaces, never
sums.
- 9-10: all surfaces agree; central workflow demonstrably completes; states handled;
  feels finished for its stated scope.
- 7-8: strong coherence and intentionality; central workflow appears functional; minor
  unfinished edges.
- 5-6: plausibly real with visible gaps; scope unclear or core not demonstrated
  end-to-end.
- 3-4: thin prototype; contradictions between surfaces; placeholder content.
- 1-2: non-functional or incoherent for its claimed scope.

## Creativity & Ambition — novelty of concept and interaction model
Judge ONLY: novelty of the concept, novelty of the interaction model, originality
relative to existing concepts, ambition, memorable/surprising use of the medium, and
the depth with which the concept is pursued. WebMCP indispensability belongs under
WebMCP Leverage, NOT here: a project can be extremely creative even if another agent
API could theoretically have enabled something similar.
- 9-10: genuinely novel concept or interaction model; ambitious scope pursued deeply
  and coherently; memorable.
- 7-8: fresh synthesis or notably original interaction; ambitious for a hackathon.
- 5-6: competent take on a known category with a real new angle.
- 3-4: derivative; novelty mostly surface details.
- 1-2: clone/template with trivial changes.
"That the category exists" is never a penalty; judge the submitted concept.

## Potential Impact — credible value for the intended audience
Judge: is there a real audience; is there a real problem, desire, or use case for that
audience; does the demonstrated solution genuinely address it. NO commercial adoption,
monetization, market pull, or startup viability is required. Entertainment, play, art,
creative expression, learning, niche hobby use, experimentation, and community value
are legitimate forms of impact. A tiny audience fully justifies a top score.
- 9-10: specific real audience with a real problem/desire; solution demonstrably
  addresses it; credible value, appeal, usefulness, or meaning for that audience.
- 7-8: clear audience and problem, good fit, credible value.
- 5-6: identifiable audience, looser fit or partial address.
- 3-4: speculative audience or strained fit; impact mostly aspirational.
- 1-2: no coherent audience/problem pairing.
Audience size is NOT a multiplier.

## Aggregate & ranking
aggregate = leverage + execution + impact + creativity (4-40). Nothing else enters.
Tie-breaks: Leverage, then Execution, then Impact, then Creativity, then higher
combined evidence confidence, then slug alphabetical. See PROTOCOL.md Ranking section.

Like Comment Story Updates Inspiration WebMCP makes web apps easier for agents to use by exposing structured tools directly. Doorman explores a boundary that becomes important as soon as those tools can change state: capability_available != capability_exercised A tool being discoverable should not automatically mean an agent has blanket authority to use it. What it does Doorman is a small shared-board demo where the human UI and the WebMCP tool surface operate over the same local state. Four tools are normally registered: list_items , add_item , update_item , and request_approval . Their authority is intentionally narrower than their existence suggests. For example, update_item can only modify items created by the current agent session. The more interesting case is delete_item . It is not merely registered and then denied by policy. It is genuinely absent from the exposed WebMCP surface until a human approves one specific deletion target. Once approved, the capability is registered dynamically, can be used once for that approved target, and is then unregistered again. Each decision also produces a small local receipt that keeps policy decision and execution outcome separate. Why WebMCP is a strong fit Without WebMCP, an agent would need to infer what the page can do from presentation-level UI. Doorman instead exposes an explicit tool surface while preserving a separate authority boundary. That creates a cleaner human-agent collaboration model: the agent can discover structured capabilities, application policy can constrain what those capabilities are allowed to affect, the human can grant a narrow one-shot capability when needed, and the capability can disappear again after use. The result is not "the agent can use everything on the page." It is a negotiated command surface whose available tools can change with human approval. How it works The implementation is deliberately small and inspectable. src/doorman.js wraps document.modelContext.registerTool(tool, { signal }) . The WebMCP callback and the local doorman.invoke() path share the same policy and execution boundary, so the demo does not maintain one policy for the UI and another for agents. Dynamic registration uses the AbortSignal lifecycle: aborting the signal unregisters delete_item . Registration is transactional, and a browser-registration failure rolls back the local entry. The one-shot delete grant is consumed at policy decision time rather than after the handler finishes. This prevents two concurrent invocations of the same approved target from both receiving authorization. The application is static and has no backend, account, or database. Board state and receipts live only in browser localStorage . Testing and evidence The repository includes Node tests, a browser probe that exercises real getTools() / executeTool() in a compatible browser, and a recorded interactive WebMCP cycle using the public URL: add, list, request approval, human approval, one-shot delete, then dynamic unregistration. Mentor explanations remain local drafts until a separate Save decision. Saving, discarding, Undo/Redo, and PDF export are not WebMCP capabilities. A source link establishes traceability, not scientific correctness. Neither a successful callback nor a digest proves that the model interpreted the paper correctly. How we built it The public application is an anonymous static reader on GitHub Pages: no account, API key, database, or local server is needed. PDF.js renders the document; PaperPilot owns the spatial annotations and source anchors. Canonical records and a reversible reducer govern state. Graphology supplies topology, while Sigma and a complete keyboard-operable DOM outline provide graph views. Moving a node changes presentation, not evidence. Optional browser recovery is tied to the exact PDF bytes by SHA-256. It retains workspace records and saved notes, not the PDF itself or unsaved mentor drafts. The original document is unchanged, and annotated-PDF export is excluded. Codex helped with planning, implementation, tests, bounded parallel reviews, and browser verification. Real failures became regression tests: one navigation bug reported the right page while the viewer settled elsewhere after Undo/Redo. Replaying the repaired interaction checked the visible effect, not just the response. The demo uses edited live capture and disclosed synthetic narration. Build WebMCP services from business workflows—agent-ready and Cloudflare-ready. Like Comment Story Updates Core ERP service graph generated from one Mantle Manifest. Typed data models include constraints, indexes, and agent-ready operations. A handoff package guides a coding agent from the backend contract to a deployable site. API, MCP, and WebMCP interfaces are generated and documented together. Mantle Builder uses WebMCP to rapidly create your own WebMCP-enabled business service and take it to Cloudflare. Describe the workflow in agent chat; the agent builds and validates the Mantle contract, previews its API, MCP, WebMCP, and Admin surfaces, then hands it to a coding agent to finish the frontend, authentication, and production deployment. Inspiration Companies adopting agent workflows quickly run into business-logic gaps. Agents can reason and browse, but the organization's real rules—who can do what, which records exist, how an order or request moves, and which operations are public or staff-only—are still trapped in documents and bespoke code. Conventional app builders also make the human perform every configuration step through a visual editor. We wanted a shared workspace where a person supplies intent and approval while an agent supplies structured implementation. What it does Mantle Builder lets a person describe a workflow and lets their agent build the service behind it through WebMCP. What is Mantle? Mantle is the open-source, config-as-code runtime beneath the Builder. An agent writes four atoms—Schema, View, Procedure, and Trigger—in one YAML Manifest; Mantle validates the contract and carries the runtime complexity. The same contract can power real consumer-facing services such as commerce, reservations, and application flows while immediately producing API, MCP, WebMCP, and Admin surfaces. Agents get the tools missing from their workflows, while operations teams get one place to manage customers, orders, requests, and queues. The agent works through four typed WebMCP tools: builder_get_started — inspect the active project, Mantle grammar, presets, and exact next calls. builder_apply_preset — start from a tested business workflow. builder_apply_manifest_patch — compile and safely activate confirmed changes. builder_execute_preview — run the service as a mock actor, inspect mutations, and follow the result in Admin. The prompt requires the agent to interview the user about actors, data, operations, permissions, and entry points, summarize the proposal, and wait for confirmation before changing the project. Every accepted change immediately appears inside an embedded Mantle Admin developer console. The person can inspect schemas, procedures, triggers, interfaces, and operational Admin views. The agent can execute the service as an anonymous visitor, member, or owner, compare canonical entries before and after mutations, and watch Admin follow the affected View, collection, or record. When the contract is ready, the user downloads a coding-agent handoff containing the complete Manifest, pinned Mantle references, a frontend WebMCP registration example, and deployment guidance. A coding agent can then add the consumer-owned frontend, authentication, provider configuration, and production wiring. Why WebMCP WebMCP turns Mantle Builder from a one-way generator into a shared control surface for people and agents. An agent can author the service, seed data, and exercise public, member, and staff workflows through typed preview tools. Each action updates the same persistent sandbox the human is viewing, while the embedded Admin console follows the affected page, collection, or record. The agent acts; the human can watch, inspect, and approve the result as it happens. This closes the loop from intent to implementation to verification. Teams can create MCP and WebMCP services that fill gaps in existing agent workflows, while operations still gets a trusted interface for managing customers, orders, requests, and queues. How we built it The React host registers Mantle-derived capabilities with document.modelContext . WebMCP calls enter one serialized mutation path where inputs and project revisions are validated. Valid Mantle resources compile into a runtime plan; a browser-hosted adapter and mock environment power the service preview. Mantle Admin runs in a same-origin iframe and talks to the host runtime over a constrained message bridge. Project Manifests and canonical preview entries autosave to IndexedDB. The production host is deployed as a Cloudflare Worker. What we learned A useful agent interface needs more than tool registration. The tools must expose enough grammar and current state for an agent to act reliably, mutations need optimistic revision checks, invalid candidates must never replace the last working project, and the human needs a visual way to verify what the agent created. The deeper thesis: an agent that teaches an instrument should read the state that actually matters — the notes you played, their timing, your score — never pixels, and it should never apply its own proposal without you. What it does Drums, a 25-key keyboard, and a six-string guitar (electric or acoustic) live in a single web page. Learn music : 27 short lessons across all three instruments, with a local, rule-based teacher that scores your timing note-by-note. Learn with an agent : the page registers seven tools on document.modelContext (WebMCP). An agent in the browser can read your completed take, open a lesson, demonstrate it on the real instrument, point at the exact step you fumbled, explain why in its own attributed panel, and propose an easier exercise. A human presses Apply exercise or Keep mine — the agent can never apply its own proposal — and Undo agent exercise restores your prior state. The panel shows recent tool calls so you can watch exactly what the agent touched. This whole loop is self-contained in the page — no backend, no microphone, works for anyone who opens the live URL. Why WebMCP is a good fit An agent can already talk about music. It cannot hear you play, and it cannot reach into a page to open the right lesson. Everything that matters — the note the audio engine actually produced, when it landed, whether it was on the beat, which lesson is open, what your last take scored — lives in the page. That state is worthless in a chat box and priceless to a teacher. There's no server to run, no API key, no MCP endpoint to host. The page registers seven tools with document.modelContext and any WebMCP-aware agent in that browser can use them. Camera frames and microphone audio are never in a tool result — there's no microphone in this build at all. How we built it Plain ES modules, no build step, no framework. Seven tools via document.modelContext.registerTool (with a navigator.modelContext fallback): reading state, listing and opening lessons, playing a demo or a take, proposing and explaining exercises, and focusing attention on one step. Local hand tracking runs in a worker; Web Audio does the drum, key and string synthesis, including a real plucked-string model for guitar. Every agent proposal carries a revision check — change the exercise yourself and a stale proposal is rejected rather than silently applied. Challenges we ran into The guitar's pinch-and-strum input had a real coordinate-mapping bug: the code only registered a strum inside a small active zone of the stage, so a hand could be visibly crossing the strings and still trigger nothing. Traced and fixed. Getting the same teach → try → feedback → easier-exercise loop to work identically across three very different instruments (drums, keys, strings) took more care than expected — each has its own timing and input model underneath one shared agent contract. What we learned Building a teaching loop where the agent can suggest but never act is a different design problem than a normal tool-calling agent — the human has to stay the one holding the instrument, in the interaction design itself, not just in a permissions check. AI Store Manager where humans edit agent plans, WebMCP re-evaluates them, and only reviewed changes are applied. Like Comment Story Updates One live operating state across people, sales, stock and costs. The agent materializes a reviewable plan — not just a chat answer. The owner can directly change the agent's proposal. WebMCP re-reads the exact human-edited candidate before commitment. One operating model, adapted across markets and languages. Inspiration OwnerOps started from a problem that feels very practical to me. In Korea, I see many people worrying about stable employment or considering small businesses as another path. But running a small store is difficult in ways that are easy to underestimate. Labor costs keep increasing. Employee schedules are difficult to manage. Ingredient prices change. Rent is expensive. And even understanding how much money is actually left after all of those costs can be surprisingly difficult. Large companies have separate people for finance, purchasing, HR, and operations. A small business owner often has to do all of that alone. My background is not traditional web product development, so I did not start this project because I wanted to build an impressive AI demo. I started with a much simpler question: Could AI actually make running a small business a little easier? To me, this feels like one of the positive ways AI can be used — not replacing the owner, but helping them understand the business, organize information, compare options, and make better decisions. That idea became OwnerOps , an AI Store Manager built with WebMCP. What it does I tried to build OwnerOps around the kinds of questions I think a real store owner might ask: Am I spending too much on labor? Can I change this schedule without hurting service? Which inventory items are becoming risky? Are ingredient costs getting too high? How much money is the store actually keeping? What happens if operating costs increase? OwnerOps brings staffing, schedules, sales, inventory, waste, purchase costs, operating costs, and profitability into one working store state. The owner uses a normal visual application. The AI agent uses nine structured WebMCP Site Tools. Both work with the same StoreState. For example, the owner can ask: Improve this week's operating profitability without creating a service coverage gap. The agent can read the relevant store information and create a StorePlan Preview. But I did not want the AI to simply make the change by itself. The owner can modify the proposed worker or shift time directly in the UI. When that happens, the previous review becomes invalid. The agent must then read the exact human-edited candidate again using evaluate_current_plan . Only after that version becomes REVIEWED can the owner apply it. The core flow is: Agent proposes → Human edits → Agent re-evaluates the exact edit → Human applies That became the most important interaction in OwnerOps. Why WebMCP I did not want the AI to understand the store by looking at screenshots and moving a mouse around a website. The application already knows its schedules, sales, inventory, costs, and current plan. WebMCP lets the agent work with those capabilities directly. The OwnerOps UI is for the human. The WebMCP tools are for the agent. But both operate on the same state. I deliberately kept the WebMCP surface to nine intent-level tools instead of exposing every internal function. The tools represent things the agent actually needs to do — read the store, create and preview plans, record events, evaluate the current candidate, and apply reviewed changes. Direct interaction with the rendered UI is left to the human for things such as editing the proposal and approving Apply. How I built it OwnerOps is built with Next.js, React, TypeScript, PostgreSQL/Supabase, and WebMCP through document.modelContext.registerTool(...) . Because web product development is not my main professional background, I used AI tools heavily throughout the project. I used them to learn unfamiliar technologies, implement and review code, debug problems, generate tests, and repeatedly verify whether the product actually behaved the way I intended. I still tried to make decisions from the problem first rather than adding AI features simply because they were possible. The current prototype covers connected operating areas including staffing, sales, inventory, waste, purchasing, operating costs, and profitability. I also experimented with different markets including Seoul, New York, Tokyo, Madrid, and Shanghai to see how the same operating model could adapt to different currencies and local contexts. Challenges The hardest part was not getting the AI to generate a recommendation. It was making sure the human and the agent did not silently end up working with different versions of the same plan. If the agent proposes a schedule change and the owner manually changes it, the AI cannot continue reasoning about its original proposal. The human-edited version has to become the current truth. That is why OwnerOps invalidates the previous review after a human edit and requires the agent to evaluate the current candidate again before Apply. Testing the real WebMCP flow was also harder than I expected. There were cases where ChatGPT could see the OwnerOps Site Tools, but that did not always mean it would actually stay on the structured tool path. I had to test the real ChatGPT desktop workflow repeatedly and make the Site-Tools-only boundary clearer. Several proof attempts failed before the complete flow worked reliably. That taught me an important difference: A tool being registered is not the same as a real agent successfully using it end to end. I also tried to be careful about data. It would have been easy to make every number in the demo look like live external data, but I did not want to do that. OwnerOps distinguishes store data, deterministic demo data, cached or seeded references, and external observations. During final video production, the schedule-history data source returned no usable history rows. Instead of fabricating the missing states just to make the demo look better, I left that sequence out. What I learned The biggest thing I learned is that useful AI for real operations is not only about generating a good answer. The state behind the answer matters. The person using the system needs to understand what the AI is proposing, be able to change it, and remain in control of consequential actions. I also learned how much AI development tools can lower the barrier between understanding a problem and being able to build software around it. For me, going from a small-business problem to a working WebMCP application was one of the most valuable parts of the challenge. What's next There are several areas where I think OwnerOps could become much more useful. One is public data. Ingredient prices, wages, consumer prices, rent, weather, and local economic information could be connected to public APIs and open datasets. That would allow a store owner to compare their real operating costs with broader market conditions instead of relying only on stored reference data. I am also interested in the openness of POS and store data. Today, a lot of small-business operating data is tied to specific POS vendors, credit-card companies, delivery platforms, or other closed systems. I would like to see a future where store owners can control and use their own sales, inventory, cost, and operating data more freely. With more open standards and open-source software, tools like OwnerOps would not need to belong to one POS vendor or one platform. Different developers and AI agents could build useful tools around the same open store data. That is much bigger than this prototype, but it is the direction I would like to explore. I hope AI can make capabilities that were once practical mainly for larger companies more accessible to small business owners — while keeping their decisions and their data under their own control. B
