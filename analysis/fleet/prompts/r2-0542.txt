# FROZEN SCORING RUBRIC (pre-registered before any Stage 1 review)

Scores are integers 1-10 on each of the FOUR official criteria. Every score carries:
rationale, evidence_surfaces, confidence (0-1). Reviewers use the full scale and these
band anchors verbatim. Category neutrality is absolute: a game, art piece, music tool,
CRM, weird toy, and developer tool are all eligible for 10/10 on every criterion if they
satisfy that criterion exceptionally well FOR WHAT THEY ARE TRYING TO BE.
No aesthetic penalties. Signs of hurried AI work matter only as actual product-quality
problems (incoherence, broken flow, placeholder content), never as style.

## WebMCP Leverage — what does WebMCP change for this product?
Judge the delta WebMCP makes to reliability, precision, structured state access,
shared human-agent state, repeatability, and capability. Tool COUNT is not leverage.
- 9-10 TRANSFORMATIVE: the intended experience is impractical without the structured
  agent surface; human and agent share live product state; the collaboration could not
  be replicated comparably well by a general agent driving the UI.
- 7-8 MAJOR DELTA: without WebMCP a general agent is markedly less reliable/precise
  here; the agent is central to the product, not a bolt-on.
- 5-6 MEANINGFUL DELTA: existing capability packaged into a genuinely better product
  experience via structured tools; same outcome otherwise possible with real friction.
- 3-4 MINOR DELTA: generic browser automation achieves comparable outcomes; WebMCP is
  a convenience layer.
- 1-2 COSMETIC: exposes an action a UI button already offers; no meaningful change.

## Execution — is the actual product coherent, intentional, complete for its scope?
Stage 1 scores EXECUTION_CLAIMED: coherence/intentionality/completeness as evidenced by
the packet (description + packaging + video frames/transcript). Thin evidence caps the
score (end-to-end credit requires end-to-end evidence) and lowers confidence.
Stage 2 may rescore as EXECUTION_OBSERVED; latest evidence-backed score replaces, never
sums.
- 9-10: all surfaces agree; central workflow demonstrably completes; states handled;
  feels finished for its stated scope.
- 7-8: strong coherence and intentionality; central workflow appears functional; minor
  unfinished edges.
- 5-6: plausibly real with visible gaps; scope unclear or core not demonstrated
  end-to-end.
- 3-4: thin prototype; contradictions between surfaces; placeholder content.
- 1-2: non-functional or incoherent for its claimed scope.

## Creativity & Ambition — novelty of concept and interaction model
Judge ONLY: novelty of the concept, novelty of the interaction model, originality
relative to existing concepts, ambition, memorable/surprising use of the medium, and
the depth with which the concept is pursued. WebMCP indispensability belongs under
WebMCP Leverage, NOT here: a project can be extremely creative even if another agent
API could theoretically have enabled something similar.
- 9-10: genuinely novel concept or interaction model; ambitious scope pursued deeply
  and coherently; memorable.
- 7-8: fresh synthesis or notably original interaction; ambitious for a hackathon.
- 5-6: competent take on a known category with a real new angle.
- 3-4: derivative; novelty mostly surface details.
- 1-2: clone/template with trivial changes.
"That the category exists" is never a penalty; judge the submitted concept.

## Potential Impact — credible value for the intended audience
Judge: is there a real audience; is there a real problem, desire, or use case for that
audience; does the demonstrated solution genuinely address it. NO commercial adoption,
monetization, market pull, or startup viability is required. Entertainment, play, art,
creative expression, learning, niche hobby use, experimentation, and community value
are legitimate forms of impact. A tiny audience fully justifies a top score.
- 9-10: specific real audience with a real problem/desire; solution demonstrably
  addresses it; credible value, appeal, usefulness, or meaning for that audience.
- 7-8: clear audience and problem, good fit, credible value.
- 5-6: identifiable audience, looser fit or partial address.
- 3-4: speculative audience or strained fit; impact mostly aspirational.
- 1-2: no coherent audience/problem pairing.
Audience size is NOT a multiplier.

## Aggregate & ranking
aggregate = leverage + execution + impact + creativity (4-40). Nothing else enters.
Tie-breaks: Leverage, then Execution, then Impact, then Creativity, then higher
combined evidence confidence, then slug alphabetical. See PROTOCOL.md Ranking section.

Like Comment Story Updates Inspiration Space science is full of ideas that are fascinating but difficult to experience. A light-year is an enormous distance. A signal can travel at the speed of light and still take 23 years to arrive. A spacecraft may have enough fuel to escape or enough time to transmit its discoveries, but not both. A video popped up on my Youtube feed while I was on a walk and gave me the inspiration for the base idea. I wanted to make them something a person could investigate with an AI agent. WORLDLINE began with a simple question: What if WebMCP could turn a webpage into a shared science lesson, where the agent investigates the evidence, the learner contributes their own thinking and the final decision remains human? What WORLDLINE does A probe near a black hole has recorded measurements showing how gravity bends space and changes light. Its computer has processed those measurements into two files. The probe is 23 light-years away and has only 71 seconds of radio contact remaining. An early, powerful burn can help it escape, but turns its antenna away from Earth. A later, gentler burn can keep the antenna connected long enough to send both files, but cannot save the probe. The browser agent receives the mission objective through WebMCP. It reads separate information about the files, radio connection, engine and maneuver limits. It forms hypotheses, predicts what each burn will do and uses a limited simulator to test possible worldlines. A worldline is the path something takes through space and time. Each tested maneuver creates a different possible future. The experience unfolds through three exchanges: The agent investigates the two clearest possibilities: saving the probe and sending the files. The learner calculates the transmission time and predicts why one burn may not achieve both goals. The agent reads that answer, tests a compromise and a counterexample, then explains what the evidence shows. The learner decides what to save. That decision creates one exact, temporary WebMCP action that the agent can execute once. The central calculation is intentionally approachable: $$ 18\text{ MB} + 12\text{ MB} = 30\text{ MB} $$ $$ 30\text{ MB} \div 1.2\text{ MB/s} = 25\text{ seconds} $$ The lesson then connects that calculation to the probe's remaining communication window, antenna alignment and escape requirements. Why WebMCP matters WebMCP is not an extra control attached to a fixed animation. It is the mechanism that makes the lesson responsive. The page initially registers six tools using document.modelContext.registerTool() . These expose separate mission facts, scientific files, maneuver constraints, a bounded simulator and presentation checkpoints. The agent must decide: Which evidence it needs Which possible burns are worth testing What each test is expected to prove Whether the learner's prediction matches the evidence Which outcome it recommends and why The learner's calculation and prediction become part of the shared page state. The agent reads that work and chooses tests that examine it. The resulting explanation develops from the evidence and the learner's participation rather than following a prerecorded sequence. When the learner makes the final choice, the page registers a seventh tool. This tool takes no arguments and is locked to the exact tested maneuver the learner selected. It can run only once. After execution, all planning and action tools disappear, leaving two read-only tools that verify the final state and transmission receipt. The visible lifecycle is: $$ 6 \rightarrow 7 \rightarrow 2\text{ tools} $$ The agent investigates. The learner decides. The page controls the resulting action. How we built it WORLDLINE is built with WebMCP, TypeScript, React and Vite, and is deployed on Vercel. It was coded using Codex. A revisioned TypeScript state machine is shared by the visible experience and the WebMCP tools. It records the mission evidence, simulation budget, tested worldlines, learner calculation, learner prediction, recommendation, human choice and final receipt. Tool schemas are closed and reject unexpected properties. Revision checks prevent stale operations. The simulator permits no more than five tests. The final execution tool closes over the selected maneuver, so the agent cannot change its timing, speed or payload after approval. Every visible path, result, calculation, animation and final receipt is derived from this shared state. Challenges we faced The hardest challenge was making the agent's intelligence visible. Agents can call several tools quickly, but a learner needs time to understand why each test matters. We redesigned the experience so results remain on screen until the learner chooses to continue. We also had to make the difference between agent mode and ordinary page interaction unmistakable. WORLDLINE does not secretly replace a missing agent with a scripted imitation. If WebMCP is unavailable, the page clearly explains what is required. Another challenge was keeping the science understandable without pretending this is a precision spacecraft simulator. The mission is fictional, but its lesson uses clear relationships between file size, transmission speed, communication time, burn timing and speed change. Finally, we needed to preserve human authority without reducing the learner to an approval button. The learner performs the calculation, proposes an explanation and makes the value decision that physics cannot answer. What we learned We learned that WebMCP can support more than task automation. It can give an AI agent access to the structure of an educational experience while allowing the webpage to remain the shared visual workspace. We also learned that a useful educational agent should not simply reveal an answer. It should test competing possibilities, respond to the learner's thinking, explain why an idea succeeds or fails and stop when the remaining question is a human judgment. WORLDLINE is an interactive science lesson about black holes, light-years, spacecraft communication and possible futures. It lets people investigate those ideas with an AI agent instead of merely watching them. Like Comment Story Updates Inspiration I'm bad at finding things in apps. Not proud of it, but it's true. Some real ones: Auth0. I needed to change one setting for a login flow and spent way too long clicking around the dashboard before I found it. The docs said "go to Settings". There are a lot of places called Settings. Jira. New team, new board, and a few days in I'm running standup from it in front of everyone, trying to find where you change an issue type or add a column while people wait. Datadog. When I was learning it I couldn't find where to filter logs properly, and later I struggled to find where the RUM integrations live. And that's me, with a technical background. I can only imagine how the websites regular people use every day make people from other backgrounds struggle. These all have the same problem. The answer exists, someone wrote it down, and it still doesn't help, because it's not on your screen. The AI answer to this is "let the agent do it for you". Agents are great, I use them all day. But that answer has two problems. You didn't learn anything, so next month you'll ask again. And the site now has to trust a robot with your login settings or your money. And sometimes you just can't hand it off. You're presenting. You're running standup. You're the one who's supposed to know where it is. Nobody wants to say "hold on, let me ask the agent" in front of the room. So I didn't want an agent that does it for me. I wanted one that points at my screen and says "there", waits for me to click, and next time I don't need it. That's ShowMe. What it does You open a site in a browser that has WebMCP, and you ask your agent something like "how do I turn on international transfers?" Then this happens: The site gives the agent a map of its own interface. Every screen, every menu, every button, with a sentence about each one. The agent works out the path and hands the whole thing to the page in one call. The page lights up the first thing you need to click, with a short message. You click it. The next one lights up. And so on. You don't wait for the model between steps. It's not involved between steps. Click the wrong thing and the page tells the agent, which re-plans. Say "just do it for me" and the site decides. Opening a menu, fine. Pressing Confirm on a transfer, no. The agent tells you why. When you're done, the agent tells you the path in one line so you remember it. I made two sites to show it. Neither has any guide code in it, just the attributes. PeruBank is a fake bank. The switch for international transfers is four levels deep, and the final Confirm is a button I never want an agent pressing. Meridian is a fake team tracker. Board settings behind one three-dots menu, project settings behind another, a type icon nobody realises is a button. Basically the board I struggled with. Who it's for: anyone in an app with hundreds of settings, like developer dashboards and admin consoles. People who should never hand an agent their account, like my parents and their bank. And anyone learning a tool in front of other people. Why WebMCP To show someone where something is, you need three things, and only the page has them: A map of the interface that's actually true right now. A way to wait for a real click and know it happened. A say in what the agent is allowed to do. A screen-reading agent guesses at all three. You could maybe get the map from an accessibility tree. You can't get the other two. WebMCP gives the site one standard way to hand all three to whatever agent the person is using. That's why this works now and didn't before. How I built it One JavaScript file, one stylesheet, no build step. Both sites use the same two files. The site marks its elements: data-guide is the id. data-guide-desc is a sentence about it, written like you'd explain it to a new colleague. data-guide-delegable means an agent may do this step for you. Leave it off and it's yours. data-guide-danger means it can't be undone. Never delegated. data-screen , data-panel , data-dialog , data-menu group things so the agent knows what has to be open first. The map is rebuilt from the live page every time it's asked for, so anything rendered later is in it too. Whether something is open is read from what's actually visible on screen. Five tools, all registered through document.modelContext.registerTool : get_ui_map and get_current_view . Read-only. Flagged untrustedContentHint because they echo text people typed. run_walkthrough . The long one. Takes the whole path, paces you through it on the page, listens for the AbortSignal , and comes back with what you did and where you are now. If it's taking a while it returns in_progress after 40 seconds and the agent calls again. do_step_for_person . Reads the policy from the markup. Refuses anything not marked delegable. end_walkthrough . Only exists while a guide is running. Registered with an AbortController , so when the guide ends the tool disappears and toolchange fires. A Playwright test drives both sites through getTools and executeTool , with Google's polyfill injected so it runs on plain Chrome. Each site also has an Agent activity panel. It shows which bits of the API have actually been used this session, plus a transcript of every call and every click. I added it because the agent's own call log is just two lines, and everything interesting happens inside one of them. Challenges I ran into The first version did one spotlight per tool call. In ChatGPT's browser every round trip was 10 to 20 seconds, so you'd click and then sit there. Handing the whole path to the page in one call fixed that. Then I hit tool timeouts, which is where in_progress came from. Knowing what the person actually did. Clicks are easy. Text fields need Enter or a pause. Checkboxes fire change, not click, if you hit the label. "Clicked somewhere else" needed a listener on the whole document that ignores the overlay, the label around a checkbox, and the panel button. Things that aren't visible yet. If the agent asks for something inside a closed menu, the page has to say what to open first, in a sentence the agent can act on. Annotations. Not every browser accepts every one, so registration retries without the rejected one and the panel says which. The map is big. About 20K characters on the bank, 40K on the tracker. It works, but it's most of the minute the first answer takes. Accomplishments that I'm proud of The first time it worked end to end in ChatGPT's browser.
