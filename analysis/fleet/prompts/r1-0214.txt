# FROZEN SCORING RUBRIC (pre-registered before any Stage 1 review)

Scores are integers 1-10 on each of the FOUR official criteria. Every score carries:
rationale, evidence_surfaces, confidence (0-1). Reviewers use the full scale and these
band anchors verbatim. Category neutrality is absolute: a game, art piece, music tool,
CRM, weird toy, and developer tool are all eligible for 10/10 on every criterion if they
satisfy that criterion exceptionally well FOR WHAT THEY ARE TRYING TO BE.
No aesthetic penalties. Signs of hurried AI work matter only as actual product-quality
problems (incoherence, broken flow, placeholder content), never as style.

## WebMCP Leverage — what does WebMCP change for this product?
Judge the delta WebMCP makes to reliability, precision, structured state access,
shared human-agent state, repeatability, and capability. Tool COUNT is not leverage.
- 9-10 TRANSFORMATIVE: the intended experience is impractical without the structured
  agent surface; human and agent share live product state; the collaboration could not
  be replicated comparably well by a general agent driving the UI.
- 7-8 MAJOR DELTA: without WebMCP a general agent is markedly less reliable/precise
  here; the agent is central to the product, not a bolt-on.
- 5-6 MEANINGFUL DELTA: existing capability packaged into a genuinely better product
  experience via structured tools; same outcome otherwise possible with real friction.
- 3-4 MINOR DELTA: generic browser automation achieves comparable outcomes; WebMCP is
  a convenience layer.
- 1-2 COSMETIC: exposes an action a UI button already offers; no meaningful change.

## Execution — is the actual product coherent, intentional, complete for its scope?
Stage 1 scores EXECUTION_CLAIMED: coherence/intentionality/completeness as evidenced by
the packet (description + packaging + video frames/transcript). Thin evidence caps the
score (end-to-end credit requires end-to-end evidence) and lowers confidence.
Stage 2 may rescore as EXECUTION_OBSERVED; latest evidence-backed score replaces, never
sums.
- 9-10: all surfaces agree; central workflow demonstrably completes; states handled;
  feels finished for its stated scope.
- 7-8: strong coherence and intentionality; central workflow appears functional; minor
  unfinished edges.
- 5-6: plausibly real with visible gaps; scope unclear or core not demonstrated
  end-to-end.
- 3-4: thin prototype; contradictions between surfaces; placeholder content.
- 1-2: non-functional or incoherent for its claimed scope.

## Creativity & Ambition — novelty of concept and interaction model
Judge ONLY: novelty of the concept, novelty of the interaction model, originality
relative to existing concepts, ambition, memorable/surprising use of the medium, and
the depth with which the concept is pursued. WebMCP indispensability belongs under
WebMCP Leverage, NOT here: a project can be extremely creative even if another agent
API could theoretically have enabled something similar.
- 9-10: genuinely novel concept or interaction model; ambitious scope pursued deeply
  and coherently; memorable.
- 7-8: fresh synthesis or notably original interaction; ambitious for a hackathon.
- 5-6: competent take on a known category with a real new angle.
- 3-4: derivative; novelty mostly surface details.
- 1-2: clone/template with trivial changes.
"That the category exists" is never a penalty; judge the submitted concept.

## Potential Impact — credible value for the intended audience
Judge: is there a real audience; is there a real problem, desire, or use case for that
audience; does the demonstrated solution genuinely address it. NO commercial adoption,
monetization, market pull, or startup viability is required. Entertainment, play, art,
creative expression, learning, niche hobby use, experimentation, and community value
are legitimate forms of impact. A tiny audience fully justifies a top score.
- 9-10: specific real audience with a real problem/desire; solution demonstrably
  addresses it; credible value, appeal, usefulness, or meaning for that audience.
- 7-8: clear audience and problem, good fit, credible value.
- 5-6: identifiable audience, looser fit or partial address.
- 3-4: speculative audience or strained fit; impact mostly aspirational.
- 1-2: no coherent audience/problem pairing.
Audience size is NOT a multiplier.

## Aggregate & ranking
aggregate = leverage + execution + impact + creativity (4-40). Nothing else enters.
Tie-breaks: Leverage, then Execution, then Impact, then Creativity, then higher
combined evidence confidence, then slug alphabetical. See PROTOCOL.md Ranking section.

Traditional optimization software can solve these problems, but describing a model often requires specialized mathematical knowledge. AI assistants make natural-language interaction easier, but an LLM should not be trusted to invent a schedule and claim it is valid. ConstraintLab combines the strengths of both: Humans express goals and judge trade-offs. AI agents translate intent into structured actions. A deterministic solver computes and validates the schedule. We built ConstraintLab to explore what an agent-native optimization workspace could look like—one where the human interface, AI agent, solver, and audit history all operate on the same canonical application state. What It Does ConstraintLab is a browser-based job-shop scheduling workspace. Users can: View jobs, machines, dependencies, deadlines, and downtime windows. Inspect schedules through an interactive Gantt chart. Add or edit scheduling constraints manually. Ask a WebMCP-capable AI agent to inspect and modify the model. Run a real mixed-integer linear programming solver in the browser. Detect when a solved schedule becomes stale after the model changes. Receive deterministic explanations when a model is infeasible. Review every human, agent, and solver action in an audit timeline. A typical interaction looks like this: “Machine 2 will be unavailable between 1 PM and 3 PM, and Job 7 must finish before noon.” The agent discovers ConstraintLab’s site tools, adds typed constraints, and requests a solve. The HiGHS solver—not the language model—computes the resulting schedule. If another request creates a contradiction, ConstraintLab reports the genuine infeasible result and identifies the relevant constraints using deterministic conflict analysis. How We Built It ConstraintLab is built with React, TypeScript, Vite, Zustand, Zod, Web Workers, WebAssembly, HiGHS, WebMCP, Vitest, and Netlify. The most important architectural decision was to maintain one canonical application state. Human interface ──┐ ▼ Domain actions ▲ AI agent ─────────┘ │ Canonical Zustand store ├── Solver ├── Gantt chart └── Audit timeline The interface and WebMCP handlers call the exact same domain actions. There is no separate “agent state” or hidden model that can drift away from what the user sees. ConstraintLab exposes nine typed site tools: get_problem_state add_job update_job add_constraint remove_constraint set_objective solve_problem get_solution analyze_infeasibility Every tool input is validated with Zod, and write operations are validated again inside the domain layer. Agent arguments are treated as requests—not as trusted data. For optimization, we compile the scheduling problem into a CPLEX LP-format model and send it to HiGHS running inside a Web Worker. This prevents the WebAssembly solver from blocking the interface. The objective is to minimize makespan: $$ \min C_{\max} $$ For every job (j), completion must satisfy: $$ C_{\max} \geq S_j + p_j $$ where (S_j) is its start time and (p_j) is its duration. Precedence, deadlines, machine assignments, availability windows, and non-overlapping jobs are represented as linear constraints. Binary ordering variables handle jobs that may compete for the same machine. The resulting assignments are translated back into application objects and applied through a single solver-result domain action. We also track a modelVersion . Every structural edit increments this version, and every solution records which version it solved. If the two differ, the interface immediately displays: Model changed — re-solve required. Challenges We Ran Into One major challenge was correctly separating language understanding from mathematical correctness. The agent is useful for translating a human request into typed tool calls, but it must never become the scheduling engine. Keeping that boundary strict influenced the entire architecture. Variable names had to remain stable enough to map solver columns back to jobs and resources. Scheduling downtime is more complicated than adding a simple bound. A job must occur either before or after an unavailable window, which requires additional binary decisions and big-(M) constraints. Infeasibility explanations were also difficult. A solver can report that a model is infeasible without producing an explanation suitable for a user. Instead of asking an LLM to guess, we built typed, rule-based checkers that examine deadlines, precedence chains, locked starts, resource assignments, and machine availability. The generated summaries are based on computed scheduling facts. Finally, WebMCP is still an emerging browser capability. We needed feature detection, top-level tool registration, a fallback path, and a clear unavailable state so that the human scheduling interface remains functional even when WebMCP is not enabled. The AI agent modifies the same state that the human sees. The schedule is computed by a real deterministic MILP solver. Constraints have stable IDs and structured types. Invalid agent inputs are rejected safely. Solver work runs outside the main browser thread. Model edits automatically invalidate stale solutions. Infeasibility explanations come from deterministic analysis. Every human, agent, and solver action is auditable. The entire application works without an AI agent. The complete optimization stack runs inside the browser and can be deployed as a static application. Our headline demo intentionally creates a real contradiction involving Job 4, Machine 2, machine downtime, and a deadline. The solver returns an actual infeasible status, and ConstraintLab identifies the relevant conflict instead of showing a scripted response. What We Learned We learned that the most valuable role for an AI agent in optimization is not solving the mathematics itself. Its value is translating intent, operating tools, and helping users navigate the model. Typed tools are essential. Allowing an agent to submit arbitrary mathematical expressions would make validation, auditing, and explanations significantly less reliable. A deliberately small vocabulary of structured constraints creates a much safer system. We also learned that shared state is more important than chat history. The collaboration feels trustworthy because every agent action appears immediately in the visual workspace, and every human edit is visible to the agent during its next inspection. Finally, infeasibility is not merely an error state. In real optimization work, discovering why requirements conflict can be as valuable as finding an optimal schedule. A living intelligence dashboard where humans and AI agents organize the web together. Like Comment Story Updates Inspiration The web is still mostly designed around humans opening tabs, searching pages, and manually organizing what they find. AI agents can answer questions about the web, but they usually operate in a separate chat experience rather than inside the same persistent workspace as the user. OpenBento explores a different model: what if humans and agents could work on the same web-native intelligence dashboard? What it does OpenBento is a persistent dashboard for following topics as they evolve. Each Canvas contains one live Dashboard where users can combine persistent WatchBots, web/news Cards, X posts with rich media and engagement data, playable YouTube Cards, notes, structured Columns, financial Cards, and AI assistance. A WatchBot owns a dedicated Column. New discoveries appear at the top of that stream instead of endlessly expanding the workspace. When something matters, the user can drag that Card out of the live feed and place it directly onto the Dashboard, preserving it while the WatchBot continues monitoring. The result is somewhere between a live research desk, TweetDeck, an intelligence dashboard, and an AI workspace. Why WebMCP is a strong fit OpenBento is fundamentally about shared state between humans and agents . Without WebMCP, an AI assistant can describe what the user should do, but it cannot naturally operate the same web application the user is already working in. With WebMCP, OpenBento exposes meaningful application actions directly to compatible agents. An agent can work with the same persistent Canvases, Cards, WatchBots, and Dashboard state that the human user sees. The agent is not controlling the application through screenshots or brittle coordinate-based browser automation. It interacts through explicit tools published by the website itself. That makes agent actions more reliable, understandable, and aligned with the application's own permission and domain model. What humans and agents can do together A person can ask an agent to create or modify parts of the workspace while continuing to interact with the same Dashboard manually. For example: A user asks an agent to create a WatchBot for Apple news. OpenBento creates a persistent WatchBot and its dedicated live Column. The WatchBot begins collecting relevant web, X, and YouTube developments. The user pulls an important Card out of that stream and pins it elsewhere on the Dashboard. A WebMCP-capable agent can later create Cards, move content, update the Canvas, or manage the WatchBot. Both the human and the agent continue working against the same persistent state. This turns the agent from an external chatbot into a participant in the user's workspace. How WebMCP is implemented OpenBento registers WebMCP tools through document.modelContext.registerTool . The WebMCP layer maps a safe set of snake_case tools onto the same authoritative domain action system used by the application's own UI. Current tools cover retrieving Canvas state, creating and switching Canvases, creating/updating/moving/resizing Cards, managing viewport state, fullscreen Dashboard presentation, creating and managing WatchBots, and reading WatchBot status. OpenBento currently exposes 15 WebMCP tools . The WebMCP implementation does not maintain a separate privileged mutation layer. Authentication, ownership checks, validation, and persistence pass through the same shared domain executor used by the human UI. Architecture OpenBento uses Next.js 16, React, TypeScript, Supabase PostgreSQL/Auth, Railway, XYFlow, OpenAI, X API, YouTube Data API, Sentry, and Resend. The application runs as a web service plus dedicated background workers for different monitoring providers. Challenges One of the biggest challenges was making background AI activity coexist with a persistent spatial interface. An early version placed every newly discovered Card directly onto an infinite Canvas. That quickly became unusable. We redesigned OpenBento around a finite Dashboard with dedicated streaming Columns. WatchBots continuously update their Columns while users can pull important information out into the persistent Dashboard. We also had to make WebMCP a genuine part of the application's architecture instead of a demo-only wrapper. WebMCP actions use the same domain action catalog, authentication, validation, and persistence layer as the human UI. What we learned The most interesting lesson was that agent-friendly websites benefit from exposing intent-level operations , not reproducing mouse and keyboard interaction. A tool like create_watchbot or move_card communicates much more useful application semantics than asking an agent to infer coordinates and manipulate the UI visually. WebMCP makes that model native to the website. What's next OpenBento can expand into a broader personal intelligence environment with richer market Cards, more social/news providers, AI-generated layouts, configurable Dashboard templates, team collaboration, and additional WebMCP actions. AI organizes the story. Sources remain the story.
