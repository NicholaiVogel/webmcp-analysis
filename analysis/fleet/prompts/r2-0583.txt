# FROZEN SCORING RUBRIC (pre-registered before any Stage 1 review)

Scores are integers 1-10 on each of the FOUR official criteria. Every score carries:
rationale, evidence_surfaces, confidence (0-1). Reviewers use the full scale and these
band anchors verbatim. Category neutrality is absolute: a game, art piece, music tool,
CRM, weird toy, and developer tool are all eligible for 10/10 on every criterion if they
satisfy that criterion exceptionally well FOR WHAT THEY ARE TRYING TO BE.
No aesthetic penalties. Signs of hurried AI work matter only as actual product-quality
problems (incoherence, broken flow, placeholder content), never as style.

## WebMCP Leverage — what does WebMCP change for this product?
Judge the delta WebMCP makes to reliability, precision, structured state access,
shared human-agent state, repeatability, and capability. Tool COUNT is not leverage.
- 9-10 TRANSFORMATIVE: the intended experience is impractical without the structured
  agent surface; human and agent share live product state; the collaboration could not
  be replicated comparably well by a general agent driving the UI.
- 7-8 MAJOR DELTA: without WebMCP a general agent is markedly less reliable/precise
  here; the agent is central to the product, not a bolt-on.
- 5-6 MEANINGFUL DELTA: existing capability packaged into a genuinely better product
  experience via structured tools; same outcome otherwise possible with real friction.
- 3-4 MINOR DELTA: generic browser automation achieves comparable outcomes; WebMCP is
  a convenience layer.
- 1-2 COSMETIC: exposes an action a UI button already offers; no meaningful change.

## Execution — is the actual product coherent, intentional, complete for its scope?
Stage 1 scores EXECUTION_CLAIMED: coherence/intentionality/completeness as evidenced by
the packet (description + packaging + video frames/transcript). Thin evidence caps the
score (end-to-end credit requires end-to-end evidence) and lowers confidence.
Stage 2 may rescore as EXECUTION_OBSERVED; latest evidence-backed score replaces, never
sums.
- 9-10: all surfaces agree; central workflow demonstrably completes; states handled;
  feels finished for its stated scope.
- 7-8: strong coherence and intentionality; central workflow appears functional; minor
  unfinished edges.
- 5-6: plausibly real with visible gaps; scope unclear or core not demonstrated
  end-to-end.
- 3-4: thin prototype; contradictions between surfaces; placeholder content.
- 1-2: non-functional or incoherent for its claimed scope.

## Creativity & Ambition — novelty of concept and interaction model
Judge ONLY: novelty of the concept, novelty of the interaction model, originality
relative to existing concepts, ambition, memorable/surprising use of the medium, and
the depth with which the concept is pursued. WebMCP indispensability belongs under
WebMCP Leverage, NOT here: a project can be extremely creative even if another agent
API could theoretically have enabled something similar.
- 9-10: genuinely novel concept or interaction model; ambitious scope pursued deeply
  and coherently; memorable.
- 7-8: fresh synthesis or notably original interaction; ambitious for a hackathon.
- 5-6: competent take on a known category with a real new angle.
- 3-4: derivative; novelty mostly surface details.
- 1-2: clone/template with trivial changes.
"That the category exists" is never a penalty; judge the submitted concept.

## Potential Impact — credible value for the intended audience
Judge: is there a real audience; is there a real problem, desire, or use case for that
audience; does the demonstrated solution genuinely address it. NO commercial adoption,
monetization, market pull, or startup viability is required. Entertainment, play, art,
creative expression, learning, niche hobby use, experimentation, and community value
are legitimate forms of impact. A tiny audience fully justifies a top score.
- 9-10: specific real audience with a real problem/desire; solution demonstrably
  addresses it; credible value, appeal, usefulness, or meaning for that audience.
- 7-8: clear audience and problem, good fit, credible value.
- 5-6: identifiable audience, looser fit or partial address.
- 3-4: speculative audience or strained fit; impact mostly aspirational.
- 1-2: no coherent audience/problem pairing.
Audience size is NOT a multiplier.

## Aggregate & ranking
aggregate = leverage + execution + impact + creativity (4-40). Nothing else enters.
Tie-breaks: Leverage, then Execution, then Impact, then Creativity, then higher
combined evidence confidence, then slug alphabetical. See PROTOCOL.md Ranking section.

Like Comment Story Updates OpenCast Inspiration Two ideas collided. The first: tools like Descript proved that the transcript is the best interface for editing talk-heavy video — you delete a sentence, the footage follows. The second: WebMCP proposes that a web page should hand agents typed, callable tools instead of making them guess at buttons. Put together, they describe the same product. If editing is already "operate on words," then an agent that speaks in words is a native editor, not a bolted-on chatbot. I wanted to build the version of that idea where the human and the agent aren't in separate worlds — they look at the same live page , pull the same levers , and every change lands in the same undo stack. Editing a podcast felt like the sharpest possible test: it's real work, it's language-shaped, and you can see whether the collaboration is real. What it does OpenCast is a transcript-first podcast/video editor. Upload a recording; the transcript becomes editable in about a minute; then you — or your agent, through 47 WebMCP tools — cut filler words and silences, fix mis-transcriptions, assemble named compositions (hooks, clips) from timeline ranges, turn on live captions, layer timed images over or under the footage, remove the background with in-browser ML, and export a finished MP4 with everything burned in — rendered entirely in the browser. How I built it The architecture is three layers, and it's the part I'd defend in any design review: Page state — a Zustand store holding words (with per-word timestamps), sources, compositions, overlays. One shared action hub — every edit is a narrow verb: cutWords , renameSpeaker , addToComposition , removeOverlay … Two front ends to the same hub — buttons for humans, and WebMCP tools ( document.modelContext.registerTool — a name, a JSON schema, a callback) for agents. Same verbs; nobody gets a special door. Tools return what changed , so the agent can verify itself, and destructive ones require explicit confirmation. Around that core: a Next.js app on Vercel; a Fly.io Docker worker that takes resumable 16 MiB upload chunks straight to a volume, extracts audio with ffmpeg, and runs a words-first pipeline — Whisper word timings through a bounded concurrent pool unlock the editor fast, while speaker diarization runs as a background enrichment that relabels the live transcript when it lands (and multi-track projects skip ML diarization entirely, because the track is the speaker). Exports use a canvas compositor: play the kept ranges once, draw background layer → footage (MediaPipe selfie segmentation for background removal) → B-roll layer → captions, and record it with the source audio via MediaRecorder to MP4. Challenges I ran into The silent five-minute ceiling. Long diarization calls kept dying with a cryptic Headers Timeout Error : Node's fetch (undici) aborts any request whose response headers take over 300s — and retried the doomed request forever. Diagnosing that, giving OpenAI calls a real deadline through an explicit dispatcher, and then restructuring to words-first (never let the slow model block the editable transcript) was the single biggest turn in the project. Embedded browsers are a different planet. ChatGPT Desktop's browser is narrow, blocks programmatic downloads without a user gesture, and can't be trusted with a GPU delegate. That forced real fixes: on-screen layers scoped to the footage's aspect ratio instead of the pane, a CPU fallback for segmentation, and layouts audited at 480px. Canvas taint. Recording a canvas fails if any drawn image isn't CORS-readable — one bad overlay URL could poison a whole render. Locally uploaded images are compressed into data URLs (always safe); non-CORS remote images are skipped rather than killing the export. Building on a moving spec. WebMCP is pre-standard; API names have already shifted. Keeping the tool layer thin over the action hub meant the churn only ever touches one file. What I learned Design tools as narrow verbs with clear side effects that report what changed — agents chain small verbs better than they wield big ones. Words-first beats complete-but-slow: shipping the editable thing early and enriching in the background changed how the product feels more than any feature. And the deepest one: the trust problem with agents isn't solved by logs or promises — it's solved by a shared screen , where every mutation happens in front of you and your undo key still works. What's next Program-cut-aware multicam rendering on the worker, cross-chunk speaker identity stitching, and a consent surface for tool calls that's worthy of the spec this is all betting on. Live-code together. Like Comment Story Updates The agent isn't controlling another Strudel session. It's playing in yours. Strudel WebMCP lets a browser agent join the exact live Strudel REPL a human is already performing with. You type, select code, and drag inline sliders as normal; the agent reads and edits that same unsaved CodeMirror document, evaluates its changes in the same running scheduler, and can record what the result sounds like — under a permission dial you own. No embedded LLM, no MCP server, no WebSocket bridge, no session ID. Live demo: https://strudel-webmcp.vercel.app Repository: https://github.com/alliecatowo/strudel-webmcp Why WebMCP Live coding is a bad fit for every earlier agent-integration shape, because the thing an agent needs to touch — the unsaved editor buffer, the exact cursor and selection, the inline slider values, the scheduler that is playing right now — exists in one browser tab and changes every second. A file-based or bridged integration has to keep a second copy of that in sync, and the copy is stale the moment a slider moves. WebMCP has no second copy. The tool call is a method on the same page, over the same document, in the same tab the human is looking at. Nothing to install, nothing to configure, no bridge that can drift. That makes something ordinary that was previously very hard: a human dragging a filter-cutoff slider with the mouse while an agent, in the same instant, rewrites a different line of the same unsaved buffer — and neither can clobber the other's most recent change. What the agent can do Thirteen tools, registered through document.modelContext.registerTool (src/webmcp/tools.ts), all backed by one adapter over the live (src/strudel/adapter.ts): Read live context: playback state, cursor, the human's current selection, what is sounding now, the agent mode, and any pending proposal, solo or recording. Read the exact current source, unsaved edits included, optionally by line range. Apply precise range edits, or replace the whole source — in review mode both become proposals. Evaluate through Strudel's own Update path (host.editor.evaluate()), so an agent edit becomes audible exactly the way a human's does, without stopping the clock; or solo just a range; or audition a pending proposal. Play, stop, and select-and-scroll to a range so the human sees what the agent means. Record the live output — master, or one .analyze("id") voice — and get dBFS and band analysis back, plus a playable take left in the page. List and extend the sound registry the running scheduler actually resolves s("...") against. Snapshot the source as a named version; switch the editor theme. Every mutating tool requires the codeHash from a previous read, computed fresh from the live document, and is refused with STALE_CODE if anything changed since — including a slider drag, because Strudel's slider widgets rewrite the source's numeric literals directly. The human's most recent edit always wins. The permission dial has three positions. Read: the agent can look, record and point, nothing else. Review: every edit arrives as a proposal with a diff the human auditions, accepts or discards — the source is unchanged until you say so. Live: edits apply directly. What people and agents can do together You keep performing. The agent reads the exact state you're in — down to the characters you have selected — and makes structural edits to the lines you didn't ask it to leave alone, then evaluates them into the same running pattern. Both of you act through the one Strudel instance; the audio reflects both immediately because neither owns a separate copy. In review mode the agent pitches instead of acting: "swap the kick for a different one" appears as a diff you can hear before it lands. And the agent has ears: ask "is the bass too loud?" and it records four seconds of the live output, answers from measured dBFS band energy, and suggests a concrete gain change. Who this is for Live coders, algorave performers, and people learning music through code — an audience for whom the editor buffer is the whole instrument, and for whom anything that isn't that exact buffer is useless mid-performance. Today, getting an agent into that instrument means standing up an MCP server, a bridge, and usually a modified frontend before a single note changes. That is real friction for a hobbyist or student and dead weight for a performer who wants a second pair of hands for eight bars. The claim is direct: browser-native creative tools shouldn't need to embed a model or ship a dedicated connector to be legible to an agent. The page can expose the instrument the human already has open. Strudel is the proof; the pattern — unsaved live document, precise agent edits, human-owned permission dial — applies to any browser-native creative or editing tool with real-time state worth exposing. Live coding is a specialized audience, but the engine underneath it is not niche: @strudel/core , the pattern engine every Strudel-based tool depends on, drew about 30,000 npm downloads in the 30 days ending August 29, 2026, and the wider @strudel/* family this project embeds — mini-notation, transpiler, WebAudio backend, CodeMirror integration, the REPL itself — adds roughly 76,000 more over the same window. The seam this project adds is generic to any of those downstream tools, not specific to this demo's drum pattern. Compared with the existing Strudel MCP bridges Existing Strudel MCP projects drive Strudel through an MCP server, a WebSocket or SSE bridge, and often a special frontend or a session ID copied between browser and agent. They work. The difference is what it takes to start: a connector, a bridge, session synchronization, and MCP configuration on one side; opening the page on the other. Here the page publishes its own controls, and the browser agent already accompanying the user joins the REPL with no external bridge, no session ID, no agent-specific server, and no second source of truth. Why this is different from "AI + music" Most "AI + music" demos are generation: describe a vibe, get a clip, no instrument in the loop. Most WebMCP demos so far are forms and dashboards. This is neither. It is a person and a browser agent performing through the same live instrument — one typing, selecting, dragging and listening; the other reading exact state, making precise edits, evaluating, and pointing back into the source. The review-mode propose/audition/accept loop goes further: the agent gets a way to pitch a change and let the human hear it before committing, which has no analogue in a form-filling demo. How it's built A static site — no server, no bridge — wrapping the official Strudel REPL and CodeMirror editor. The adapter is the only code that touches @strudel/repl internals; the tools, schemas, registration and error helpers sit above it. Unit tests (Vitest) cover hashing, position and offset conversion, edit validation, stale-hash rejection, source-size bounds and error normalization. Playwright end-to-end tests run against the real <strudel-editor> , including one that physically moves an inline slider and confirms a stale agent write against the pre-slider hash is rejected, and a modes suite covering read denials, the proposal lifecycle, solo, both recording modes, samples, snapshots and themes. An AI-powered city planner that uses WebMCP to discover, combine, and book a personalized night out across independent local services. Like Comment Story Updates Inspiration I started Serendipity because planning a free evening in Tokyo kept turning into admin. I would find an interesting place, look for dinner nearby, check when both closed, open a map, and then discover that the route did not fit. A simple night out could leave me with ten tabs and no actual plan. The first version was a technical reservation-network demo. It worked, but it did not answer the question a normal user would ask: why would I use this? I kept the reliable parts and rebuilt the product around a simpler job. Give it the evening you have, and get back one plan whose times and prices you can check for yourself. What it does You choose Shibuya, Shinjuku, or Ikebukuro, then set the group size, time, per-person budget, walking limit, and the kind of night you want. Dinner can be included or left out. Serendipity builds one route with two or three stops. It shows when to visit, how long the walks are, what each stop should cost, and the estimated total for the group. If three stops do not fit, it says why it returned two. If nothing fits, it does not quietly loosen the request. It shows the closest option and waits for the user to approve the change. Each stop has a sources section with the publisher, checked date, published hours, official menu, and official link. This is planning from published information, not a live booking service. Serendipity does not claim a seat is available and does not send anything to a venue. Why WebMCP is a strong fit This kind of planning often starts as a conversation: “Plan a calm Ikebukuro evening for three, include dinner, and keep it under ¥4,000 each.” Then comes the follow-up: show me where the meal price came from, or change one stop. An assistant can try to operate the form like a person, but it has to interpret the layout and guess whether the page really changed. WebMCP gives it five actions instead: find a plan, show the evidence for a place, change one stop, save the plan, or delete it. Those actions use stable plan and place IDs, and the result appears on the same page the user is watching. I stopped at five tools. Finding a plan, checking evidence, and changing a stop are read-only. Saving and deleting are the only write actions, and they only affect this browser. Opening a restaurant or venue website is still a normal link the user chooses. How I built it The app is built with Next.js, React, and TypeScript and deployed on Vercel. The planner is deterministic. It checks combinations of activities and meals against published opening hours, visit length, closing time, budget, and walking limits. Restaurant budgets use the highest price in the official menu range. Walking time is marked as a coordinate-based estimate. I reviewed 21 places across the three hubs: four activities and three meals in each area. Every place in a route needs support for its name, address, coordinates, hours, public access, and price. I wrote the short descriptions myself and used official pages instead of copying marketplace reviews or photos. Google enrichment exists behind a server flag, but it is off in the submitted version. The live app works from its reviewed data and official menu pages. The human controls and the five Site Tools call the same controller. I did not want an agent demo sitting beside a separate, less reliable product. If a request is stale, malformed, cancelled, or unsafe, both paths reject it in the same way. Challenges The hardest part was deciding what the app was allowed to say. It is easy to make a schedule look convincing. It is much harder to prove that a place is actually open at that time and that the displayed price came from a menu. I spent more time removing weak data than adding new places. Parks without published hours, prices inferred as zero, stale closure calendars, and unclear reuse rights all forced changes to the data model. The UI went through several bad versions. One was two dashboards side by side. Another had oversized type and decorative cards that made the route harder to follow. Feedback on those versions was blunt, but correct. I removed most of the explanation from the main path, gave the route the full page, and kept source details behind disclosures. The agent and the human also kept finding different edge cases. A late response could overwrite a newer search. Browser Back could make the URL disagree with the plan. A damaged saved record could break reopening. Fixing those bugs led to one shared state controller instead of two implementations. Accomplishments The live app covers three Tokyo hubs, with four reviewed activities and three official-menu meals in each one. Codex discovered all five Site Tools in the ChatGPT desktop browser and used them to build a real plan and open its meal evidence on the visible page. A strict request can return an honest no-result. The app only offers a relaxed search after the server has verified it and the user chooses it. What I learned A difficult technical demo is not automatically a useful product. The first version proved that several sites could coordinate, but people still asked, “What is this for?” Rebuilding it around one understandable job made the engineering choices much clearer. Good Site Tools are closer to product actions than UI controls. The agent does not need a tool for every button. It needs a few useful verbs, clear limits, and enough evidence for the person to check the result. WebMCP worked best when the agent and the user could look at the same updated plan, not when the agent disappeared into a background workflow. What’s next I would add more neighborhoods only after reviewing them to the same standard as the current three. The next practical improvements are transit-aware travel, shareable saved plans, and an optional live Google check controlled by the user. I would not add booking or payment until a venue provides real booking authority and there is a safe way to handle the transaction.
