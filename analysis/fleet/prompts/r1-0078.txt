# FROZEN SCORING RUBRIC (pre-registered before any Stage 1 review)

Scores are integers 1-10 on each of the FOUR official criteria. Every score carries:
rationale, evidence_surfaces, confidence (0-1). Reviewers use the full scale and these
band anchors verbatim. Category neutrality is absolute: a game, art piece, music tool,
CRM, weird toy, and developer tool are all eligible for 10/10 on every criterion if they
satisfy that criterion exceptionally well FOR WHAT THEY ARE TRYING TO BE.
No aesthetic penalties. Signs of hurried AI work matter only as actual product-quality
problems (incoherence, broken flow, placeholder content), never as style.

## WebMCP Leverage — what does WebMCP change for this product?
Judge the delta WebMCP makes to reliability, precision, structured state access,
shared human-agent state, repeatability, and capability. Tool COUNT is not leverage.
- 9-10 TRANSFORMATIVE: the intended experience is impractical without the structured
  agent surface; human and agent share live product state; the collaboration could not
  be replicated comparably well by a general agent driving the UI.
- 7-8 MAJOR DELTA: without WebMCP a general agent is markedly less reliable/precise
  here; the agent is central to the product, not a bolt-on.
- 5-6 MEANINGFUL DELTA: existing capability packaged into a genuinely better product
  experience via structured tools; same outcome otherwise possible with real friction.
- 3-4 MINOR DELTA: generic browser automation achieves comparable outcomes; WebMCP is
  a convenience layer.
- 1-2 COSMETIC: exposes an action a UI button already offers; no meaningful change.

## Execution — is the actual product coherent, intentional, complete for its scope?
Stage 1 scores EXECUTION_CLAIMED: coherence/intentionality/completeness as evidenced by
the packet (description + packaging + video frames/transcript). Thin evidence caps the
score (end-to-end credit requires end-to-end evidence) and lowers confidence.
Stage 2 may rescore as EXECUTION_OBSERVED; latest evidence-backed score replaces, never
sums.
- 9-10: all surfaces agree; central workflow demonstrably completes; states handled;
  feels finished for its stated scope.
- 7-8: strong coherence and intentionality; central workflow appears functional; minor
  unfinished edges.
- 5-6: plausibly real with visible gaps; scope unclear or core not demonstrated
  end-to-end.
- 3-4: thin prototype; contradictions between surfaces; placeholder content.
- 1-2: non-functional or incoherent for its claimed scope.

## Creativity & Ambition — novelty of concept and interaction model
Judge ONLY: novelty of the concept, novelty of the interaction model, originality
relative to existing concepts, ambition, memorable/surprising use of the medium, and
the depth with which the concept is pursued. WebMCP indispensability belongs under
WebMCP Leverage, NOT here: a project can be extremely creative even if another agent
API could theoretically have enabled something similar.
- 9-10: genuinely novel concept or interaction model; ambitious scope pursued deeply
  and coherently; memorable.
- 7-8: fresh synthesis or notably original interaction; ambitious for a hackathon.
- 5-6: competent take on a known category with a real new angle.
- 3-4: derivative; novelty mostly surface details.
- 1-2: clone/template with trivial changes.
"That the category exists" is never a penalty; judge the submitted concept.

## Potential Impact — credible value for the intended audience
Judge: is there a real audience; is there a real problem, desire, or use case for that
audience; does the demonstrated solution genuinely address it. NO commercial adoption,
monetization, market pull, or startup viability is required. Entertainment, play, art,
creative expression, learning, niche hobby use, experimentation, and community value
are legitimate forms of impact. A tiny audience fully justifies a top score.
- 9-10: specific real audience with a real problem/desire; solution demonstrably
  addresses it; credible value, appeal, usefulness, or meaning for that audience.
- 7-8: clear audience and problem, good fit, credible value.
- 5-6: identifiable audience, looser fit or partial address.
- 3-4: speculative audience or strained fit; impact mostly aspirational.
- 1-2: no coherent audience/problem pairing.
Audience size is NOT a multiplier.

## Aggregate & ranking
aggregate = leverage + execution + impact + creativity (4-40). Nothing else enters.
Tie-breaks: Leverage, then Execution, then Impact, then Creativity, then higher
combined evidence confidence, then slug alphabetical. See PROTOCOL.md Ranking section.

Like Comment Story Updates Booking Interface Codex with WebMCP Inspiration Tour bookings are often controlled by large marketplaces that sit between travelers and local operators. These intermediaries can add commissions, weaken the direct customer relationship, and make small operators dependent on centralized platforms. Our inspiration was to use WebMCP to create a more direct model. A traveler can ask an LLM what they want in natural language, and the agent can interact directly with tools published by the tour operator's own website—without scraping the page or routing the customer through a marketplace. The agent automates repetitive steps such as discovering tours, comparing departures, checking current capacity, and preparing the visible cart. The operator retains ownership of its inventory, brand, and customer relationship, while the traveler remains in control of checkout, payment, and final confirmation. What it does Tours Booking WebMCP lets an AI agent work with the same inventory and cart that the traveler sees. It exposes four WebMCP tools: search_departures searches the page's current inventory snapshot. get_departure refreshes and re-checks the capacity, price, and transfer options for a specific departure. prepare_booking validates a complete selection against refreshed capacity and atomically replaces the visible cart. get_booking_summary reports the cart contents, total price, estimated authorization amount, editability, and recommended next action. An agent can help travelers discover suitable tours, verify that enough spaces remain, and prepare a cart for review. It cannot collect contact or billing details, handle cards, authorize payment, reserve inventory, or confirm a booking. Those actions remain human-controlled in the visible booking experience. The normal interface continues to work when WebMCP is unavailable, making the integration a progressive enhancement. How we built it We built a framework-independent TypeScript adapter around WebMCP's imperative document.modelContext.registerTool() API. The host booking page supplies normalized inventory and cart-state callbacks. The adapter owns the tool descriptions, strict JSON schemas, matching runtime validation, freshness rules, response formatting, cancellation, lifecycle cleanup, and safe cart-preparation protocol. For an exact departure check or cart preparation, the adapter asks the host to refresh inventory instead of presenting cached data as current. prepare_booking validates the complete replacement cart, checks the page state before and after the asynchronous refresh, and performs exactly one atomic commit. If validation or a state check fails, the existing cart remains unchanged. Inventory-derived results are marked as untrusted content. Read-only tools are annotated accordingly, while the cart-mutating tool is explicitly identified as non-read-only. The repository also includes a backend-free Vite demo with deterministic in-memory inventory, a fake document.modelContext test harness, documentation, and agent-evaluation scenarios. Challenges we ran into One challenge was designing around an experimental browser API whose availability and behavior may change. The application still needed to remain usable in browsers without WebMCP. Freshness was another major challenge. Availability can change between an agent's search and its attempt to prepare a cart, so saved inventory cannot be presented as current. We added explicit freshness metadata, forced refreshes for exact checks, and capacity validation immediately before committing cart state. Asynchronous refreshes also introduced race conditions. Checkout could begin, another browser tab could become active, or an invocation could be cancelled while capacity was being refreshed. We addressed these cases with before-and-after state checks, serialized preparation, abort-signal propagation, and an atomic commit boundary. Finally, we wanted useful agent capabilities without quietly turning a cart tool into a payment or reservation tool. Identity, billing, cards, payments, reservations, and confirmations therefore remain completely outside the tool contracts. Accomplishments that we're proud of Built a coherent four-tool WebMCP experience rather than a single demonstration call. Connected agent actions to the same visible cart controlled by the traveler. Implemented fresh-capacity validation and all-or-nothing cart preparation. Preserved a strict human boundary around personal data, payment, reservation, and confirmation. Added 21 deterministic tests covering schemas, freshness, time zones, capacity, atomicity, concurrency, cancellation, lifecycle cleanup, and safety boundaries. Defined nine agent-evaluation scenarios covering tool selection, argument accuracy, ambiguity, unavailable data, and unsafe payment requests. Kept search responses within a compact 1,536-byte target. Created an independently runnable demo requiring no credentials, customer data, private API, or payment provider. Passed the complete formatting, testing, TypeScript build, demo build, and package-verification pipeline. What we learned We learned that designing WebMCP tools is as much a product and security exercise as it is an API exercise. Tool names, descriptions, schemas, annotations, and results collectively shape how reliably an agent behaves. We also learned that freshness must be represented explicitly. "Available" is not meaningful unless the agent and traveler know whether the information came from a current refresh or a saved snapshot. Most importantly, we learned that useful agent assistance does not require handing over the entire user journey. Agents can reduce discovery and coordination work while preserving meaningful human control over sensitive and consequential actions. Connect the adapter to normalized production inventory through the existing host callbacks without exposing private booking endpoints. Run the evaluation suite across additional WebMCP-capable agents and browsers. Add more inventory scenarios, accessibility testing, and localized user-facing explanations. Continue refining consent and review UX while keeping payment and confirmation human-controlled. That's not "AI generates a slide deck." That's "a human and an agent share one editable document, through the same interface." We wanted to see if that idea actually held up once a real agent, not a scripted demo, was driving it. What it does SlideForge is a presentation editor with one shared state — a Zustand store — and exactly one way to change it. A slide is a named layout ( title-slide , two-column , media-split , …) made of typed, addressable regions: title , body , left_body , text, bullets, a real bar/pie chart, a flow diagram. A human clicks a region and types. An agent calls one of 19 tools — set_bullets , set_chart_data , apply_theme , reorder_slides — and the exact same store updates, the exact same way. Both are logged to a live Activity Panel, so nothing an agent does is invisible. There's also an Agent Console built into the app itself — pick any of the 19 tools, fill in its arguments, run it. It calls the identical code a real WebMCP host would call, which turned out to be essential, because most browsers don't support WebMCP yet (more on that below). The agent calls set_chart_data with real {label, value} numbers — that's an actual rendered chart, not a screenshot of one pasted into a slide. How we built it React 19 + TypeScript + Vite, Zustand for state (wrapped in persist , so a page refresh doesn't wipe the deck out from under a live agent conversation), Tailwind for styling. No backend, no API keys, no server — the whole thing is a static SPA. The part we're proudest of architecturally: every slide is rendered at a fixed 960×540 resolution and scaled into whatever container it's in via a ResizeObserver + CSS transform . The filmstrip thumbnails and the full canvas are the same component tree , just scaled differently — not two rendering paths that can drift out of sync. The WebMCP layer is intentionally thin: a ToolDef array, one runTool() function that wraps every call in try/catch and logs the result, and a single useRegisterTools() hook that registers all 19 at the app root. Every tool's execute() calls straight into a plain Zustand store action — the same action a click handler in the UI calls. That's the whole trick: there was never a second write path to keep in sync, because we never built one. Stale ids that quietly broke everything. Every time the deck reset — a page refresh, a "Reset to sample deck" click — every slide and region got a brand new random id. An agent mid-conversation had no way to know that happened, so it kept confidently calling tools with ids that no longer existed. SweetSpot helps people find the right area to live by combining their preferences, such as commute time, groceries, parks, schools, and healthcare and see it visually on the map Like Comment Story Updates application-page Inspiration We work for Apple Maps, so maps and geographic information systems are a big part of our daily work. For this hackathon, we wanted to explore the GIS domain from a different angle: helping people make complex location decisions with an AI agent. Choosing where to live often means balancing several needs at once. You may want a short commute, groceries within walking distance, a nearby park, and easy access to healthcare or nearby school for your child. Most map products can answer these questions separately, but finding the areas where all the requirements overlap takes a lot of manual work. That inspired us to build Sweet Spot. What it does Sweet Spot helps people find areas that match several travel and lifestyle needs. A user can ask: “Find an area within an 8-minute bike ride of San Francisco City Hall, a 5-minute walk of a supermarket, and a 4-minute walk of a park.” Using WebMCP, ChatGPT can work directly with the Sweet Spot map. It can search for real places, add travel limits, combine conditions, rank different areas, explain trade-offs, update the plan, undo changes, and create a shareable result. Some preferences are difficult to describe with words or forms. In those cases, the agent can pause and ask the user to draw an area directly on the map. The agent then continues its work using that boundary. Sweet Spot also records whether each action came from the user or the agent, keeping the process visible and reversible. How we built it We built Sweet Spot as a client-side web application using React, TypeScript, MapLibre, Web Workers, H3 spatial indexing, and WebMCP. The application uses real OpenStreetMap street and place data, along with official neighborhood data where available. Instead of drawing simple circles around a destination, Sweet Spot calculates reachable areas using the street network and different travel modes. The product exposes 18 WebMCP tools. These tools use the same application logic as the visible interface, so every agent action immediately appears on the shared map. The geographic calculations run on the user’s device. We use a Web Worker, spatial indexing, and reusable distance caches to keep the interface responsive. Challenges we ran into Our biggest challenge was running large geographic calculations inside the browser. Early versions were slow because they repeatedly searched the road network and recalculated the same distances. We improved this with H3-based lookups, bounded caches, and per-layer progress updates. Location search was also harder than expected. Users often enter partial names, different spellings, or small typos. We added normalized, word-aware, typo-tolerant search with an online fallback. We also learned that WebMCP tools need to support complete user goals, not merely copy individual button actions. We created an atomic planning tool for full requests while keeping smaller tools for later edits and conversation. Accomplishments that we're proud of We are proud that Sweet Spot is a complete product experience rather than a WebMCP demonstration added to a map. It was good to see that ChatGPT could build a real geographic plan, update the visible interface, explain the results, and hand control to the user when human judgment is needed. The agent can pause in the middle of its task, ask the user to draw a personal boundary, wait for the response, and then continue calculating. What we learned The agent is good at turning a natural-language request into several structured operations and comparing many results. The user is better at expressing personal preferences, judging the map visually, and making the final decision. WebMCP connects those strengths. The user no longer has to copy an AI recommendation into another product and rebuild the search manually. The agent can use the product’s real tools while the user watches, guides, and corrects the process. What's next for Sweet Spot Next, we want to support more cities and add public transit, live traffic, elevation, housing costs, accessibility needs, and other real-world factors. We also want to improve collaborative planning so families, friends, or teams can contribute their own destinations and preferences to the same workspace. Our larger goal is to make Sweet Spot useful for more than choosing where to live. The same approach could help people select an office, plan public services, compare business locations, or explore where community resources are most needed. Also, we feel that these sort of tools should be available in Google and Apple Maps. It will be very helpful for users in real life.
