# FROZEN SCORING RUBRIC (pre-registered before any Stage 1 review)

Scores are integers 1-10 on each of the FOUR official criteria. Every score carries:
rationale, evidence_surfaces, confidence (0-1). Reviewers use the full scale and these
band anchors verbatim. Category neutrality is absolute: a game, art piece, music tool,
CRM, weird toy, and developer tool are all eligible for 10/10 on every criterion if they
satisfy that criterion exceptionally well FOR WHAT THEY ARE TRYING TO BE.
No aesthetic penalties. Signs of hurried AI work matter only as actual product-quality
problems (incoherence, broken flow, placeholder content), never as style.

## WebMCP Leverage — what does WebMCP change for this product?
Judge the delta WebMCP makes to reliability, precision, structured state access,
shared human-agent state, repeatability, and capability. Tool COUNT is not leverage.
- 9-10 TRANSFORMATIVE: the intended experience is impractical without the structured
  agent surface; human and agent share live product state; the collaboration could not
  be replicated comparably well by a general agent driving the UI.
- 7-8 MAJOR DELTA: without WebMCP a general agent is markedly less reliable/precise
  here; the agent is central to the product, not a bolt-on.
- 5-6 MEANINGFUL DELTA: existing capability packaged into a genuinely better product
  experience via structured tools; same outcome otherwise possible with real friction.
- 3-4 MINOR DELTA: generic browser automation achieves comparable outcomes; WebMCP is
  a convenience layer.
- 1-2 COSMETIC: exposes an action a UI button already offers; no meaningful change.

## Execution — is the actual product coherent, intentional, complete for its scope?
Stage 1 scores EXECUTION_CLAIMED: coherence/intentionality/completeness as evidenced by
the packet (description + packaging + video frames/transcript). Thin evidence caps the
score (end-to-end credit requires end-to-end evidence) and lowers confidence.
Stage 2 may rescore as EXECUTION_OBSERVED; latest evidence-backed score replaces, never
sums.
- 9-10: all surfaces agree; central workflow demonstrably completes; states handled;
  feels finished for its stated scope.
- 7-8: strong coherence and intentionality; central workflow appears functional; minor
  unfinished edges.
- 5-6: plausibly real with visible gaps; scope unclear or core not demonstrated
  end-to-end.
- 3-4: thin prototype; contradictions between surfaces; placeholder content.
- 1-2: non-functional or incoherent for its claimed scope.

## Creativity & Ambition — novelty of concept and interaction model
Judge ONLY: novelty of the concept, novelty of the interaction model, originality
relative to existing concepts, ambition, memorable/surprising use of the medium, and
the depth with which the concept is pursued. WebMCP indispensability belongs under
WebMCP Leverage, NOT here: a project can be extremely creative even if another agent
API could theoretically have enabled something similar.
- 9-10: genuinely novel concept or interaction model; ambitious scope pursued deeply
  and coherently; memorable.
- 7-8: fresh synthesis or notably original interaction; ambitious for a hackathon.
- 5-6: competent take on a known category with a real new angle.
- 3-4: derivative; novelty mostly surface details.
- 1-2: clone/template with trivial changes.
"That the category exists" is never a penalty; judge the submitted concept.

## Potential Impact — credible value for the intended audience
Judge: is there a real audience; is there a real problem, desire, or use case for that
audience; does the demonstrated solution genuinely address it. NO commercial adoption,
monetization, market pull, or startup viability is required. Entertainment, play, art,
creative expression, learning, niche hobby use, experimentation, and community value
are legitimate forms of impact. A tiny audience fully justifies a top score.
- 9-10: specific real audience with a real problem/desire; solution demonstrably
  addresses it; credible value, appeal, usefulness, or meaning for that audience.
- 7-8: clear audience and problem, good fit, credible value.
- 5-6: identifiable audience, looser fit or partial address.
- 3-4: speculative audience or strained fit; impact mostly aspirational.
- 1-2: no coherent audience/problem pairing.
Audience size is NOT a multiplier.

## Aggregate & ranking
aggregate = leverage + execution + impact + creativity (4-40). Nothing else enters.
Tie-breaks: Leverage, then Execution, then Impact, then Creativity, then higher
combined evidence confidence, then slug alphabetical. See PROTOCOL.md Ranking section.

Like Comment Story Updates Inspiration Agent usage keeps growing, but the shape of it generally doesn't change; go to a chat window, describe the thing, then wait until it comes back. This means the agent is never working in the room with you. That's what I wanted to experience: where the agent and user are on the same surface at the same time, simultaneously reaching for things. And more specifically, when the agent isn't the app's own. Notion's AI works, but it still doesn't feel like you're working together with it. What it does It's an infinite sticky-note canvas. Open it in multiple windows, and now you have several people each with their own agent, all editing the same board live. There's no server holding state, the tabs sync directly, and the board lives in the browser. Any agent with WebMCP capabilities can join by calling the eleven tools the page puts up. It can read the board, ask what's changed since it last looked, ask what the humans are doing right now, arrange things, draw links between them, annotate, collapse a cluster into a summary, add notes, or propose reorganizing the whole thing. An important part is that it can refuse agent actions, taking into account interactions between humans and other agents. How we built it Some brainstorming and then AI building tools. Stack was React 19, Vite, TypeScript, React Flow for the canvas, Zustand for scene state, and Yjs with awareness for peer sync over BroadcastChannel. Also, agent actions all go through one wrapper that gives them a body on screen, which includes a cursor, a visibly held note, and motion that takes time. Without that, an agent taking actions just looks like notes teleporting. Challenges we ran into The biggest one was realizing that a refusal has to be a return value and not an error. If you throw at a model it just tries again. If you tell it what it ran into and who got there first, it usually does something sensible instead. That changed the shape of every tool. WebMCP also has no way for a tool to ask the user anything, which is awkward when your whole thesis is asking permission. The handler for the destructive tool ends up just sitting there and waiting while a dialog goes up in every tab. Deciding that silence should count as a refusal rather than approval took longer to settle than the code did. Then there was a bug where the two tabs weren't in the same room at all. The room was keyed off the whole URL, so a tab carrying an extra query parameter quietly joined a room of its own. Both windows looked perfect on their own, which is what made it bad. Every cross-tab guarantee was untested and I didn't know it. Accomplishments that I'm proud of I think this is a good exploration into how multi human-agent interactions working together in the same room would look like in the future. It doesn't run with a server, and you can actually see multiple agents working side by side with humans. What I learned Notion's last write wins policy works for now, but what'll happen when dozens of agents are working on the same thing at the same time? I think that's what the future holds and it makes me realize that the current systems are not built at all for that kind of scale. Why WebMCP Fits This is about an agent being outranked; by a hand, by the room, or by the board moving while it thought. None of that exists outside the page: a hand on a note lives 300ms and never reaches a database. A server can't see it, and automation is the user, so there's nobody to yield to. WebMCP is the only shape where the agent is a guest. What's next for Co-Presence Not too sure to be honest, but I can envision this system being used for other applications as well, not just note taking. What it does A person creates a browser-local workspace and describes an objective. The person can request changes, accept a deliverable while keeping the task open, or accept and complete it. Project Desk is local by design. Project Desk exposes eleven typed domain actions against the same live workspace the person is reviewing. The agent can inspect state, read references and approved decisions, add necessary work, submit deliverables, record and reconcile evidence, and request judgment. The tool boundary deliberately withholds workspace selection, deliverable acceptance, unrestricted task editing, and human completion. The linked GitHub repository includes the WebMCP tool reference, implementation details, and testing documentation. Instead of repeatedly copying plans, decisions, and revisions between ChatGPT and a separate project tracker, the person and agent can keep the work in one shared workspace. After the person supplies decisions or references, the agent can inspect the updated project, reconcile outdated evidence, advance multiple tasks, and save reviewable deliverables, while the person retains control over acceptance and completion throughout repeated work-and-review rounds. That creates a safer and more useful human-agent loop: the agent can contribute work to a structured application, while evidence, uncertainty, decisions, and final authority remain visible to the person. How it was built Project Desk is a React and TypeScript application packaged for ChatGPT Sites. Like 1 Comment Story Updates 01-intro 05-compare-tools-vs-baseline 03-blocked-at-gate 02-agent-filling 04-submitted-confirmation 06-webmcp-tools-panel CoAuth - an agent-native prior-authorization cockpit Inspiration Prior authorization is where American healthcare loses time it cannot afford - and it loses it to paperwork, not to medicine. In Medicare Advantage in 2024, 80.7% of appealed prior-authorization denials were fully or partially overturned, yet only 11.5% of denials were ever appealed ( KFF ). Read those two numbers together: most denials that get contested turn out to have been wrong, and almost no one contests them. The clinical decision is usually fine. The filing is where it breaks. That is an unusually clean fit for WebMCP, and not just because forms are tedious. Browser agents are weakest exactly where this work lives - long, write-heavy, multi-step forms full of coded fields (ICD-10, HCPCS J-codes, NPIs) that a pixel-clicking agent has no way to get right. And in this domain a human is not optional: a licensed clinician is legally required to attest. So the task splits along a natural seam. The agent is good at the mechanics; the human is mandatory for the judgment. That is what human-plus-agent collaboration is actually supposed to be - not an agent doing everything while a person watches a progress bar, but two participants each doing the part only they can. What it does CoAuth is a single live page that registers 13 typed WebMCP tools on document.modelContext . An agent loads a patient record, reads a payer's coverage rules as structured data, fills every non-judgment field, attaches clinical evidence, scores denial risk, detects contraindications and unmet step-therapy criteria, and drafts the medical-necessity narrative and an appeal letter. What it cannot do: fill a clinician-judgment field, resolve a critical contraindication, or submit. Those belong to the human, and both halves are enforced in code rather than described in prose. The demo case is Marcus Lee, a psoriatic-arthritis patient requesting Humira through Aetna. The agent fills the form cleanly and then catches what a rushed clinician might miss - a positive TB screen, a contraindication for this drug - flags it, tries to submit, and is stopped. It drafts the medical-necessity language from the chart but cannot adopt its own draft. From there the clinician signs in, makes the call on the TB screen and documents why, accepts the drafted wording as their own, attests, and signs. Only then does the submission go through, with a server-verified audit trail in the clinician's name. Two details make that boundary real rather than cosmetic. The signature is minted only for an authenticated clinician who re-enters their own credential at the moment of signing - each clinician has a distinct secret, so the name and NPI in the audit trail belong to the person who actually signed, not to anyone who knew a shared password. And the filed submission is rendered as an X12 278 - the HIPAA-mandated Health Care Services Review transaction payers actually receive - so every value the agent filled and the clinician signed maps to the real prior-authorization EDI, not just our own JSON. How I built it Vite, React, TypeScript, and Zustand on the front end; Vercel Edge Functions and Upstash Redis on the back end. A 15-rule clinical engine drives conflict detection, denial-risk scoring, and validation. The architectural decision that carried everything else: the WebMCP tool surface is the application's only write path. Every click in the interface calls the same invokeTool an agent calls, tagged with the actor - there is no parallel code path for humans. So the tools cannot rot behind the UI, attribution is a property of the call path rather than something each component must remember, and the built-in "with tools vs. without" comparison drives the real tools rather than a re-implementation. Tool schemas are generated from the seed data, so the patient, payer, drug and field enums cannot drift stale - an early bug had a payer hint still reading "uhc or aetna" after a third payer was added. Every property carries a description; write tools declare destructiveHint / idempotentHint so a runtime can require confirmation before submit ; and a get_submission_state tool lets an agent joining mid-task see what is filled, who filled it, what is unresolved, and whether the clinician has signed. Correctness is proven by 127 checks that run against the deployed system - not unit tests, and nothing mocked. They attempt to submit without a signature, with a forged approval, with a form edited after signing, with a clinical override swapped after signing, and with one approval replayed twelve times concurrently , requiring exactly one acceptance. They drive the registered WebMCP tools through a real model-context surface and try, as an agent would, to set the attending attestation. They drive every clinician control the way a hostile script would and require that none of them takes effect. And a meta-check reads the clinical rules table and fails if any rule is not exercised by a test. Challenges I faced The entire pitch is that an AI agent cannot file a prior auth without a real clinician signing off, and the hard part was making that actually true instead of just looking true. Every time I thought I'd locked it down, I'd poke at it and find another way around my own gate. My first gate was a browser check, which is useless against an agent that lives in the browser. I moved it to the server, then found the sign endpoint would hand a signature to anyone who asked, under any name they typed. I fixed that, then found a script could still sign using the logged-in doctor's own session, no click required. Turning on the database to stop replays silently rejected every submission in production. And the safety rules were reading tea leaves, so a note saying "no adverse events" got written into a letter to the insurer as a treatment failure. The real enemy was never a single bug. Every time I fixed the exact thing in front of me, the same kind of bug was sitting one layer over: I'd guard the button and leave the API open, guard one tool and leave its twin open, protect the signature and forget the reasoning behind it. What finally worked was to stop patching the specific hole and close the whole category at once, with one shared check instead of five copies I'd never keep in sync. What I learned Stop trusting the moment something starts working. Every real hole showed up after the feature looked done, and I only made progress once I started trying to break my own product on purpose. A passing test can also be a lie: I had one proving an approval couldn't be reused, and it passed while the live site happily accepted a burst of the same token, because the test wasn't checking the thing I was actually afraid of. And the big one, about the product itself: the interesting part of a human-plus-AI tool isn't making the AI capable, that part is easy. It's drawing the line the AI cannot cross, building the wall around what belongs to the human, and proving it holds when someone is actively trying to climb it. For something that touches patient care, that wall isn't a feature. It's the whole point. There is no separate demo code path, which means the animation can't lie about what the tools do — if the gating works in the demo, it works for a real agent, because it's the same code. The gating itself lives in the tools, not in prompts: check_policy returns the contradiction and refuses to resolve it, naming the authority holders instead contact_support refuses a more intrusive channel without a recorded failure on a less intrusive one resolve_case treats evidenceIds as lookup keys only, reading authoritative and supports off the stored record and ignoring anything the agent claims alongside pay_seller ignores the agent's approved: true entirely and reads payment.humanConfirmedAt , which only a human UI click writes Thirteen invariant checks run headlessly against all of it. External systems — support center, seller, telephony, payment — are simulated. Tool registration, tool invocation, evidence gating, escalation gating, deadline-dependent routing, and state transitions are real. Challenges we ran into We built a project about deadlines under a deadline, which was funnier in concept than in practice. Three hours, self-imposed. The virtual clock in the demo moves one hour every four seconds. Ours moved about the same. We gated the conclusion and forgot to gate the escalation. This was the real one. The first version locked down resolve_case beautifully — no evidence, no answer — and then let contact_support escalate to a phone call based purely on what time it was. Which meant an agent could skip the text entirely and cold-call a stranger's personal number at 16:01, and the site would happily connect it. The whole thesis is deadline changes the route, not the authority , and the route change had no requirements at all. We caught it with about an hour left and fixed it by requiring a recorded failure of the less intrusive channel. It turned into the second-best scene in the demo. An agent that waits looks identical to an agent that's broken. The wait is the intellectual center of the project and it is, visually, nothing happening. We had to promote it from a passing beat to a persistent badge holding for nine real seconds, anchored to the human support scene on the other screen, before it read as a decision instead of a hang. Restraint is hard to film. Everything interesting here is a refusal, and refusals don't animate. We ended up building the demo around three blocked calls instead of one successful one. Accomplishments that we're proud of The tool table in our README has a column header that reads "what it refuses." That was the moment the project clicked. Thirteen invariants that hold under adversarial input, including the one we're fondest of: hand resolve_case four genuine, real, correctly-formed pieces of evidence that don't happen to support the conclusion, two minutes before the deadline, and it says no. Not because a prompt asked it to be careful. Because the site checked. One action layer, three entry points, zero divergence. The demo cannot show something the tools don't actually do. And we shipped a working, deployed, agent-testable site in three hours without cutting the part that made it worth building. What we learned Guardrails belong to whoever owns the state, and that isn't the model. Every attempt to enforce this in the agent's reasoning is a suggestion. Enforced in a tool's return value, it's a fact. WebMCP turns out to be a surprisingly good place to put policy, because the site already knows what actually happened. Partial gating is worse than no gating , because it looks finished. We locked the front door and left the escalation path wide open, and the demo ran perfectly

WebMCP gives AI agents tools to act inside web applications. We wanted to explore what comes next: how those actions can work together, and how an agent can recover when only part of your request succeeds. The moving scenario is our way of making that problem easy to see. The bigger idea is simple: if we trust AI to take action for us, it also needs a careful way to handle what happens when something goes wrong. What it does CommitWeb brings several service actions into one plan. It checks that they work together, asks for your approval, and helps recover when only part of the plan succeeds. Our demo follows a move from Hyderabad to Bengaluru using four services: electricity, movers, mail forwarding, and internet. You set the rules. Keep setup costs within 5,000 rupees. No appointments before 10 AM. No overlapping visits. Have electricity ready before the internet installation. Before booking anything, CommitWeb checks the whole plan. If two appointments clash, it shows the problem and offers a repair. You review the corrected plan and approve it. Then comes the important part. Three services confirm their changes, but the internet slot expires. CommitWeb keeps those three confirmations and looks for a new internet slot that still fits your original rules. It shows why an unsuitable option is rejected. It checks the replacement against the bookings that already exist. And because the plan has changed, it asks for your approval again. Only the replacement internet booking runs. The other three are not repeated. At the end, you get a record of what happened, including the failure and the recovery. That record survives a page refresh and can be downloaded. How we built it We built CommitWeb with Next.js, React, TypeScript, and native WebMCP. Each demo provider has its own browser document, saved state, and WebMCP tools. Seven checks decide whether a plan meets the user's rules. These checks run in code rather than relying on an AI to guess. For example, the budget rule is: Total setup cost must stay within 5,000 rupees. Approval is tied to a fingerprint of the exact plan. A changed plan cannot reuse the old approval. Retry protection prevents the same approved action from creating duplicate bookings. We used Codex throughout planning, implementation, debugging, and testing.
