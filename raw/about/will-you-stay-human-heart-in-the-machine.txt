A Metropolis-inspired moral decision game where WebMCP lets an AI agent act inside the live experience—while the architecture keeps moral choice and final authority human. Like Comment Story Updates Inspiration AI can already write, sort, recommend, and act for us. The more interesting question is no longer simply what AI can do, but what we still want to do ourselves. Will You Stay Human? — Heart in the Machine is a Metropolis-inspired moral decision game about that boundary. It draws on the tension between mind, hands, and heart, but tells an original story inside Machine City. Its central idea is that meaningful human–AI collaboration requires more than automation. The agent may assist, explain, and act, but the human must remain responsible for deciding how much authority to delegate. I wanted to turn this abstract question into something people could experience through concrete, everyday situations. What it does The player enters Machine City and meets Futura, an AI agent connected directly to the live game through WebMCP. Across one persistent session, the player encounters four playable dilemmas: writing an apology to someone they hurt; completing homework where AI assistance is permitted; creating a shortlist of job candidates; checking a rapidly spreading claim before sharing it. For every dilemma, the human chooses one of three approaches: MIND — The Machine helps; I decide HAND — I leave it to the Machine HEART — I decide and act The choice is initially tentative. Futura presents a direction-specific reflection and asks the player to consider the other side. The player can still change direction. Only after the human explicitly confirms the decision can its consequence be revealed. Each confirmed choice changes the Human Balance across five dimensions: Convenience Control Connection Freedom Responsibility The Human Balance is not a morality score. It shows what the player kept for themselves and what they handed to the Machine. Why WebMCP A fair criticism of many agent demonstrations is that the AI can simply automate the entire experience. If the agent can also make and confirm the decision, the human role becomes theatrical. Here, that boundary is the product. WebMCP turns the agent into a real participant in the game rather than an external chatbot or a scripted narrator. The agent can: enter Machine City; present the next valid dilemma; read authoritative, structured game state; reflect on the player’s actual tentative selection; reveal the consequence of the decision that was truly confirmed. However, the agent cannot select MIND, HAND, or HEART, acknowledge its own reflection, or confirm the final decision. Those actions exist only in the player interface. This creates a collaboration that was previously difficult to demonstrate clearly: the agent can understand and advance a live experience while the application itself enforces where human authority must begin. The boundary does not depend only on a prompt asking the agent to behave. It is enforced by the application architecture. How I built it The project was created during the challenge as a bilingual, production-deployed web experience with five live WebMCP tools: enter_machine_city present_dilemma get_current_game_state present_choice_reflection reveal_confirmed_consequence The game is driven by an explicit domain state machine. Agent commands and player commands use separate ports, so an agent action cannot accidentally execute a human-only decision. Every session returns structured state including: sessionId stateRevision the current phase tentativeSelectionId confirmedDecisionId the next allowed actions Revision checks protect the session from stale tool calls. Opaque selection and decision identifiers ensure that reflections and consequences are attached to the real stored choice rather than something inferred from the conversation. The consequence tool is available only after confirmation and is idempotent, so retrying it cannot apply the Human Balance change twice. The session persists across refreshes, allowing the browser interface and the WebMCP agent to remain synchronized throughout the experience. Codex supported the implementation process from product requirements and state-machine design through contract tests, browser verification, UX iteration, and production deployment. I approached the project primarily from a Business Analyst and Product Owner perspective, which made translating responsibility, state, and permissions into enforceable technical contracts a major part of the learning process. Challenges I ran into The hardest conceptual challenge was deciding what the agent must not be allowed to do. It would have been easier to expose every interaction as a tool and let the agent complete the entire flow. But that would contradict the project’s central idea. The human-only boundaries therefore had to be enforced technically rather than described as optional behavior. A late adversarial UX review with Claude Opus also exposed a critical regression. The flow moved from Futura’s introduction too quickly to the MIND–HAND–HEART cards, while the concrete question — “Should I apologize on your behalf?” — did not receive its own readable moment. The player could see the available directions but could not clearly understand what they were deciding about. I corrected this by introducing a presentation sub-state inside the existing human-selection phase. The dilemma now receives a dedicated screen with its full context and a clear transition into the choices. This improved the dramaturgy and comprehension without changing the domain state machine or any WebMCP contract. Another challenge was keeping three layers synchronized: the cinematic browser experience; the persistent domain state; the agent’s structured WebMCP view. Revision checks, explicit identifiers, allowed-action lists, and automated contract tests became essential for preventing those layers from drifting apart. Accomplishments that I am proud of Built a complete, coherent experience rather than only a technical tool demonstration. Implemented four playable dilemmas in one persistent session. Exposed five working WebMCP tools from the live production origin. Enforced human-only selection and confirmation at the architecture level. Connected every reflection and consequence to authoritative live state. Made stale and duplicate agent calls safely rejectable or idempotent. Created a bilingual, cinematic interface inspired by Metropolis. Turned an abstract AI-responsibility question into a playable experience. Used adversarial AI review to identify and correct a real UX regression before submission. What I learned WebMCP is most interesting when it is used for more than remote control. Its real value is that an agent can participate in a live web experience through structured capabilities while the application retains authority over state, permissions, and human boundaries. I also learned that domain state and presentation state are not always the same. A technically correct transition can still fail as a human experience if the player is not given enough time and context to understand what is happening. Finally, identifiers, revision control, idempotency, and allowed-action contracts are not merely backend details. In an agentic application, they are part of the user experience because they make the collaboration predictable and trustworthy. What’s next The concept can grow into: additional everyday and professional dilemmas; age-specific or topic-specific editions; a facilitator mode for classrooms and AI-literacy workshops; multiplayer discussion before individual confirmation; a physical Machine City board connected to the online agent experience; deeper accessibility testing and support for additional WebMCP clients. The long-term goal is not to tell people whether using AI is good or bad. It is to help them notice the exact moment when assistance becomes delegation — and decide consciously where they want to draw that line.
