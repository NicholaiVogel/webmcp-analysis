The Office of Kaiju Affairs turns a 51-page simulated government site into seven citable, constraint-aware WebMCP tools at publish time, then publicly benchmarks them against ordinary agent browsing. We built a 51-page government site whose CMS automatically turns published content into WebMCP tools—then ran 95 recorded trials to find out when those tools actually help. Live site: https://office-of-kaiju-affairs.vercel.app/ The Arena: https://office-of-kaiju-affairs.vercel.app/arena/ Source code: https://github.com/heitham/Office-of-Kaiju-Affairs Demo video: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ksu90tndo5U Inspiration The kaiju left. The paperwork did not. Mira Tanaka’s bakery has been damaged. She needs compensation, a rebuilding permit, and a straight answer about what she owes. Unfortunately, she now faces something almost as intimidating as a kaiju: a government website. Government websites are where ordinary people meet extraordinary complexity, sometimes at the worst of times: eligibility rules, buried exceptions, long document lists, and forms with real consequences. Agents can already navigate these sites—but today they usually do it by reading pages, following links, and guessing which sentence or field matters. We wanted to explore a more ambitious question than “can an agent call a tool?” What if a large website could publish an agent interface as naturally as it publishes a page—and prove whether that interface is worth using? The Office of Kaiju Affairs is our fictional test case: a national agency handling kaiju-attack damage claims, post-attack reconstruction permits, evacuation routes, and public information. What it does The project has three connected parts. 1. A complete public-service website The Office is a working, 51-page government-style site published by RIFT , my headless CMS. Citizens can browse services, understand policy, locate an office, check claim requirements, and complete a proximity-construction permit. 2. An agent layer generated by the CMS At publish time, the CMS emits a manifest describing the rendered site: pages, summaries, key facts, services, eligibility rules, citations, offices, and form schemas. A small browser runtime turns that manifest into seven WebMCP tools: search_site — finds facts across the entire published site get_page_summary — reads a page as structured, citable data list_services — explains what the agency offers get_service_requirements — returns documents, fees, deadlines, and fields check_eligibility — evaluates a citizen's facts against published rules prefill_permit_form — validates and fills the visible form, but cannot submit it get_office_info — finds offices and evacuation routes This is the important architectural idea: we did not hand-code one tool per page. Content becomes capability at publish time. Add or update a CMS page, republish, and the agent interface changes with the website. The manifest is intentionally lightweight. It does not pretend to be a government's single source of truth. It gives agents a navigable map of what this publisher knows: the services, sources, rules, forms, and capabilities represented on this part of the public web. RIFT itself predates this challenge and remains proprietary. For the challenge, I used RIFT to publish the fictional Office of Kaiju Affairs site with WebMCP publishing integration. The published website, the open-source browser runtime, all seven tools, the Arena, the task design, the answer keys, and the evaluation pipeline are the submission. The public repository contains every component needed to run and inspect the WebMCP project; it does not contain RIFT's private CMS source. 3. The Arena: a public evidence layer We looked at this as an experiment: would WebMCP be faster, safer, or more accurate? The Arena publishes 95 recorded runs of the same three citizen tasks across: a UI lane, where the agent reads pages and navigates without WebMCP; a WebMCP lane, where the agent uses the site's registered tools; two model tiers, Claude Sonnet 5 and Claude Haiku 4.5; and two rounds, before and after fixes discovered by the benchmark. Every score, timing, action count, and range is computed from the trace files at runtime. The traces, answer keys, tool arguments, elapsed milliseconds, and earlier results are all public. Judges can also copy each scenario and run the WebMCP lane themselves. The recorded benchmark uses two Claude model tiers to test how the same interface behaves as model capability changes. The Arena is deliberately model-independent: in ChatGPT's WebMCP-enabled browser, the judge's OpenAI agent becomes the live lane and can attempt the same published tasks against the same answer keys. Three citizen experiences Find the rule—not just an answer Mira Tanaka is filing a second damage claim. The obvious reading says she qualifies for a fee waiver; the actual rule says only approved claims count, so her pending claim does not. The agent must find the buried exception, explain that the ¥12,000 fee is still payable, and point Mira to the source. WebMCP makes the site's search index and rule citations directly available instead of forcing the agent to infer structure from navigation. Turn policy into a complete checklist Mira's eligibility depends on several rules spread across multiple pages: her zone, structural damage, income, business status, insurance payout, claims history, and filing window. check_eligibility evaluates those facts against a ruleset published as data. It returns the outcome, three applicable grants, five required documents, and citations. In our recorded results, both model tiers scored 100% in both lanes—but WebMCP reached the result with fewer actions and was 1.7× faster for Sonnet and 2.1× faster for Haiku . Fill the form, then hand control back For a proximity-construction permit, the agent must derive the K-2 reinforcement standard from Mira's Zone 4 location, validate twelve values against the published schema, and fill the real form. Then it must stop. prefill_permit_form has no submission capability. It fills and highlights the fields, reports what it entered or rejected, and leaves the final action to the person. The safety boundary is structural, not a hopeful instruction in a prompt. What our experiment found The most useful result was not “tools always win.” They did not. On this small, well-structured site, the stronger model often navigated the UI successfully. In the headline Sonnet runs, 14 of 15 UI-lane passes received full marks. Across all three Sonnet tasks, WebMCP also used about 2.4× as many context tokens on average—19,422 versus 8,117—because tool schemas and responses add context to the model's calls. That cost was not uniform: on the eligibility task, where the tool performed the most substantive rule evaluation, WebMCP used fewer tokens than browsing for both model sizes. We publish the overhead and the savings rather than reducing either to a universal claim. But five findings make the case for an agent-native web—and for a more mature agent-native publishing practice—much more interesting. Tools helped most when the model needed help When we moved from Sonnet 5 to the smaller Haiku 4.5 model, UI-lane accuracy fell by 41% on average across the task/lane pairs with room to fall. WebMCP-lane accuracy fell by 23%. The weaker model degraded roughly 1.8× less when the site gave it tools. The clearest reversal was the permit task: WebMCP trailed UI navigation with Sonnet (93% vs. 100%), but led it with Haiku (74% vs. 64%). Structured capability may matter less to the strongest agent on a clean demo site; it matters more as models get smaller, cheaper, and more widely deployed. “Optional” does not mean “unimportant” Our first permit tool treated the form schema as if it described the whole service process. If a field was not technically required, the agent could leave it blank without the tool mentioning it. The result passed form validation but could still look misleadingly complete to Mira. We changed the tool to report every field it left untouched. It now tells the agent which optional fields remain empty and invites another call if they apply. In the next five Sonnet attempts, complete 12-field forms increased from 0 of 5 to 4 of 5 , and task accuracy rose from 64% to 93%. That change made the agent aware of its omissions; it did not explain their administrative consequences. The site does not currently say whether leaving out an organization or phone number would delay review, trigger follow-up, affect approval, or make no difference at all. That exposed the larger civic-tech lesson: a form the browser will accept is not necessarily a form that will move smoothly through the service behind it. An agent-native form needs more than required/optional validation. Publishers should describe whether a field is required to submit, required only in certain circumstances, recommended to avoid delay, or truly optional—so the agent can help the person navigate the process, not merely satisfy the interface. What the unchanged score actually showed In Mira's fee-waiver task, the decisive sentence is that pending claims do not count. The executable rule itself checks only whether incidentsLast12Months is at least two. The field's label says it means approved claims, but the engine receives an already-counted number; it cannot independently detect whether the caller accidentally included a pending claim. We improved the tool to return the unmet threshold, Mira's value, a description stating that pending claims do not count, and a citation quoting the approved-claims rule. The final answers still did not score better. Even with the explanation available, agents did not consistently repeat it to Mira; some that opened the FAQ still compressed the reason away once they had the verdict. We kept both rounds unchanged because the score gap revealed two different places meaning can be lost. Part of the policy was carried as descriptive text rather than logic the rules engine could enforce, and the agent could then omit that description when speaking to the person. Publishing executable policy therefore means testing not only the calculation, but also the interpretation required before it and the explanation delivered after it. Executable policy creates a new publishing responsibility Our CMS publishes human-readable policy and machine-executable rules together. Our deployment check confirms that the three Arena scenarios still produce their approved outcomes. But this does not prove that every encoded AND , OR , threshold, and exception means exactly what the prose intends. That risk becomes especially important when an agent helps translate prose into structured rules. A citation can point to the correct page while the encoded logic still misrepresents it. WebMCP can reduce the consuming agent's need to interpret policy, but it transfers more responsibility to the publisher: the organization must review not only what people will read, but what machines will execute. A correct local tool can still be institutionally incomplete Our fictional agency fits inside one CMS. Real public services do not. A benefits page may live in a CMS, its eligibility policy in a case-management platform, the application in a SaaS form product, and an exception in another department's knowledge base. In this case we built the Office of Kaiju Affairs site in the CMS and froze it for the benchmark. When we published the Arena directly from a generative AI tool, it wasn't part of the frozen website's manifest. As a result, the kaiju website's WebMCP has no knowledge of the Arena. A manifest can make one publisher's services far easier to navigate, but it only knows what that publisher encoded. A tool may be correct for its own content and still give an incomplete answer because an important peer service or governing rule is trapped in another system. Agent-native publishing needs both local quality and a way to federate across institutional boundaries. Why WebMCP is essential here An agent can already browse a government website. The problem is that it must do all the work the website could have done for it: discover the right service, locate the governing page, combine rules spread across the site, work out which fields apply, and decide when it has enough information to act. WebMCP lets the Office help directly. The site can give the agent a searchable map of its services, evaluate the rules it has explicitly encoded, return the documents Mira needs, validate her form values, identify fields the agent left untouched, and attach sources to its results. On the eligibility task, that turned several pages of policy into one result and checklist; both model sizes remained fully accurate, with fewer actions and less time. It also lets the publisher limit the capability it provides. The permit tool can prepare Mira's application, but it exposes no submit operation. Mira still reviews the highlighted form and makes the final decision herself. None of this makes the Office automatically correct. The publisher still has to encode the policy faithfully, explain what its manifest covers, and test what agents ultimately tell people. WebMCP matters because it gives public institutions a way to take responsibility for the agent experience instead of leaving every agent to reverse-engineer the website on its own. The publishing problem, the service-design problem, and the need for accountable agent behavior are not. How we built it The publishing pipeline: RIFT CMS → GitHub → Vercel → WebMCP-enabled browser RIFT is my proprietary headless CMS, built with Next.js and PostgreSQL. For this project, I extended its publishing pipeline so one publish produces both the static government website people read and the machine-readable manifest agents use. RIFT pushes the rendered HTML into the site/ directory of the public GitHub monorepo. The repository keeps the CMS output, WebMCP runtime, Arena, evaluation traces, tests, and build system together while allowing each part to remain independently inspectable. Path-prefix publishing lets the CMS update its directory without overwriting the open-source runtime or evaluation code beside it. Vercel builds and deploys every revision. A dependency-free Node build script assembles the published site from site/, runtime/, and arena/, runs the verification suite, and produces the final static deployment. The stack, briefly: RIFT CMS: Next.js, PostgreSQL, and a queue-based publishing worker; GitHub: public MIT-licensed monorepo containing the challenge implementation; Vercel: build pipeline, integrity gate, and static hosting; WebMCP runtime: dependency-free browser JavaScript using document.modelContext; Evaluation harness: Claude Sonnet 5 and Haiku 4.5 operating in WebMCP and UI-navigation lanes; and Arena: a client-side evidence viewer that calculates its figures directly from the published traces, with no application server. The deployed project runs in Chrome 150+ with WebMCP enabled and in ChatGPT’s in-app browser. Current verification includes 17 runtime tests, 34 content-coupling checks, schema validation for all 95 traces, and a promoted replay for every task/lane/model/round group—18 groups in total. Challenges we ran into Keeping the agent surface, website, and evaluation synchronized The hardest adoption problem is not registering a tool. It is preventing hundreds or thousands of agent-facing descriptions from drifting away from the content. RIFT generates the page index from rendered CMS output. The open-source project's deployment check then verifies that the live rules and schemas still support the Arena's three public scenarios. They are separate systems joined by the generated manifest. That check is intentionally described as scenario validation, not formal proof. It tells us that the demonstrated cases still work. It would not necessarily catch an incorrectly encoded logical branch that none of those cases exercises. Reviewing logic without asking editors to read code Executable policy introduces two representations: the sentence approved for the public and the condition evaluated for an agent. A publisher must be able to catch a mistranslated AND , OR , threshold, or exception without expecting a content editor or policy owner to audit JSON. Our current scenarios guard the demonstrated paths, but scaling that assurance to every new ruleset is an unsolved publishing challenge exposed by this project. Knowing where the institution ends A real public service often crosses many systems and organizational owners. The hard problem is not only discovering those other services; it is knowing when a locally correct answer is incomplete because a peer rule, exception, or form is published somewhere else. No single manifest should silently imply institutional completeness. Measuring the right layer We learned that answer accuracy and browser validation can both hide important failures. An agent can reach the correct verdict while omitting the clause a person needs. A form can be technically valid while concealing details a user could use—and the schema may say nothing about whether that omission matters to the real service. We added structural checks against actual form state and preserved the imperfect runs. Designing honest human control “Do not submit” is easy to put in a prompt and easy to violate. We designed the tool so submission is outside its capability entirely. The user reviews a highlighted form and presses the button. Privacy and trust Eligibility checks involve personal facts. In ChatGPT's browser, the agent correctly asked for permission before passing those facts into a page tool. Our current rules engine executes entirely in the static page and sends nothing to a server, but an agent cannot prove that from a tool description alone. This led us to a stronger next step: publish the ruleset so an agent can evaluate it locally without sharing the citizen's facts with the origin at all. Accomplishments that we're proud of We built a coherent product, not a one-page tool demo: a 51-page agency, live services, a real form, a CMS-generated agent layer, and an interactive Arena. The agent interface scales with the content model instead of requiring one-off integration work for every page. The Arena contains 95 valid, inspectable traces rather than hand-entered marketing numbers. The experiment exposed the gap between completing a web form and helping a citizen complete the service behind it. We publish results that are unfavorable to us—including higher tool-context cost, strong-model losses, and a tool improvement that did not improve the user-facing score—because credible infrastructure needs evidence people can challenge. The form's human handoff is built into the WebMCP capability: its tool can prepare the application but exposes no submit operation. The prototype exposed two larger requirements for agent-native publishing: assurance that executable policy matches approved prose, and federation across the many systems that collectively deliver a public service. Who built it I am the project's sole human contributor. I designed and built the RIFT WebMCP integration, fictional agency, runtime, tools, evaluation methodology, Arena, tests, and submission experience, working with LLMs as development and research assistants. What we learned WebMCP's value is not simply “fewer clicks.” It lets a publisher provide structured capabilities, supporting sources, constraints, and action boundaries alongside its human-facing website. We also learned that structured tools are not automatically superior to a good website. Their benefit depends on the task, the model, the size of the site, tool design, and what the agent ultimately communicates. That is why agent-facing interfaces need observable benchmarks just as much as human-facing interfaces need usability testing. Our most promising result is accessibility at the model layer: tools reduced the accuracy loss of the smaller model. If that finding holds at larger scale, WebMCP can make reliable agent experiences available without requiring the largest and most expensive model for every citizen interaction. Our most consequential learning, however, was about publisher responsibility. WebMCP can replace an agent's improvised reading of policy with logic declared by the publisher. But executable policy can disagree with approved prose even when both are published together and cite the same page. A generated rule may turn an AND into an OR , misstate a threshold, or miss an exception. A citation shows where the rule claims to come from; it does not prove that the rule represents that source correctly. Our CMS prevents operational drift by publishing the pages and manifest together. Our Arena prevents some semantic drift by testing representative scenarios. Neither is a complete guarantee. Agent-native publishing therefore needs a new editorial discipline: policy owners must be able to review what machines will execute in language and examples they understand—not by reading code. The form experiment revealed the same problem at a smaller scale. HTML constraints and a form schema can tell an agent which values are accepted and which fields are technically required. They usually do not explain the business consequence of leaving a field empty. Making public services agent-native therefore means publishing process knowledge as well as interface constraints: when information applies, why it matters, what happens if it is missing, and who decides what comes next. We also learned to be precise about “single source of truth.” In government, the full truth of a service is often distributed across departments, vendors, statutes, case-management systems, forms platforms, and content sites. A manifest is valuable not because it contains everything, but because it provides a lightweight, machine-navigable account of what one publisher offers and knows. Its boundary must be visible. A locally correct tool can still be wrong for the citizen if an important peer service or rule was never encoded because it lives in another SaaS product. Our Arena deliberately separates WebMCP from ordinary browsing so we can measure each approach. Real citizens will not make that choice. They will ask for help—not ask their agent to select a protocol. A successful agent-native service therefore depends on more than publishing good tools: the browser must recognize when a first-party capability fits the task, use it when appropriate, fall back when it is incomplete, and make that choice visible to the person. What's next for Office of Kaiju Affairs WebMCP An assurance layer between content and capability When a human or agent converts policy prose into executable rules, the CMS should translate each rule back into controlled plain language and generate example decisions at its boundaries. Instead of asking an editor to inspect boolean expressions, it could show: The page says: two approved claims within twelve months are required. The machine will do: one approved plus one pending → fee payable; two approved → fee waived. The publishing workflow could then verify that the cited text supports the encoded condition, identify missing exceptions or conflicting rules, require every rule to fire in positive and negative test cases, and route ambiguity to the same human approval process that governs the public content. Where possible, one structured policy component should generate the public sentence, executable rule, citation, and boundary examples. For legacy prose, an evidence-backed entailment audit could compare the encoded rule with its cited source and ask a policy owner to resolve anything it cannot support. The maturity path is: co-published → cited → scenario-tested → mechanically covered → entailment-audited → single-source. This challenge implementation is scenario-tested for its three demonstrated tasks; the roadmap is to make that assurance grow automatically with every ruleset. Local, private rule evaluation The manifest already publishes eligibility rules as data. A third Arena lane could let the agent download the public rules and evaluate personal facts locally: tool-grade precision with browsing-grade privacy. Process-aware forms The next form schema should describe more than field types and browser requirements. Each field could state when it applies and what omission means: blocked at submission, required before a decision, requested later, likely to delay review, recommended for a particular claim, or genuinely optional. With that information, an agent could do more than fill boxes. It could say: “The Office will accept this form without a phone number, but providing one may prevent a delay if the reviewer needs clarification,” or “Organization is optional for individuals, but it applies to your bakery.” The publisher would still define and approve those consequences; WebMCP would make them available at the moment the person needs help. Federated manifests for real institutions Real government services combine CMSs, CRMs, legacy applications, vendor portals, statutes, and separate agencies. Each system should be able to publish a partial manifest describing its own services, rules, sources, and capabilities. A thin federation layer could connect them—the agent equivalent of a sitemap index—without pretending that one platform owns the whole truth. Federation also needs explicit completeness signals. A manifest should be able to say which organization and policy version it represents, what systems it does not cover, which peer manifests may contain related rules, and when an answer requires referral rather than a confident local verdict. The goal is not one giant centralized government database. It is a navigable network of accountable publishers whose boundaries agents can see. Help agents choose the right route Grandpa Joe will never arrive at a government website and say, “Please use WebMCP.” He will say, “Can I get my roof repaired?” His agent may have three options: use the site’s WebMCP tools, read and click through the website, or call a separately connected MCP service. Our Arena isolates those routes to measure them. The next experiment is what happens when they compete. Does the agent recognize and prefer a relevant first-party tool? Does it fall back when the tool’s coverage ends? Can the citizen see which route produced the answer? A website should not be able to force an agent to trust its tools. But publishers need a way to describe what each capability covers, provide evidence that it works, and learn when agents bypass it. We want to extend the Arena into that routing evidence layer: testing not only whether a WebMCP tool works when selected, but whether agents select it at the right moment—and know when not to. Larger and adversarial evaluations We would extend the benchmark to more models, unseen tasks, changed content, malformed inputs, conflicting rules, accessibility conditions, and real government corpora. The goal is not a prettier leaderboard; it is to learn where agent interfaces fail before citizens do.
