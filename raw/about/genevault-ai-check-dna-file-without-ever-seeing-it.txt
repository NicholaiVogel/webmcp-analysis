GeneVault uses zero-knowledge Groth16 zk-SNARKs to prove genetic drug contraindications in-browser. Clinical safety for AI agents, zero exposure for your raw DNA. Like Comment Story Updates Inspiration Imagine an AI medical assistant wants to prescribe you a common blood thinner — Plavix. But for about 30% of people, genetics prevent the drug from working, which can lead to fatal blood clots. So the AI asks: "Just upload your full DNA sequence so I can check." And there's the trap. You should never paste your private genetic code into an AI model or a cloud server — once your DNA leaks, it's leaked forever. You can change a stolen password; you cannot change your genome. We started GeneVault the moment we realized the technology to break this deadlock already existed in the browser. WebMCP lets a website advertise real, callable tools to any AI agent. Zero-knowledge proofs let you prove a statement about secret data without revealing the data. Put them together and the answer to "upload your DNA" becomes: "No — but I'll prove the conclusion to you mathematically, right here on my device." That reframing — the browser as the trust boundary between a patient and any AI model — is the entire project. And here is the part that keeps us up at night (in a good way): this deadlock is not a medical problem. It is the structural problem of the AI era. An AI HR tool that wants your therapy history. A tax agent that wants your bank statements. A hiring agent that wants your face. In every case the model's context window is treated as the place where private data goes to be processed — and leaked. GeneVault is our answer, demonstrated end-to-end on the highest-stakes dataset we could find: the human genome. If the pattern survives DNA, it survives anywhere. What it does GeneVault turns a browser tab into a privacy-hardened pharmacogenomics workstation that any WebMCP-capable AI agent can operate — without ever receiving a single letter of DNA. Parses real raw genetic files fully client-side. Drop in a 23andMe / AncestryDNA / VCF export and GeneVault scans 643,000+ positions in a single pass, entirely in local memory. Nothing is uploaded — there is no server to upload to . Advertises 12 live tools to any AI agent through document.modelContext.registerTool — file parsing, marker listing, drug-safety checks, alternative-therapy recommendations, proof generation/verification, and even 3D protein manipulation. The agent discovers them on its own; we never prompt-inject instructions. Answers the question that matters: "Can I safely take this drug?" — for CPIC Level A pairs like Plavix/CYP2C19, Warfarin/CYP2C9+VKORC1, Simvastatin/SLCO1B1, Codeine/CYP2D6, 5-FU/DPYD, Abacavir/HLA-B and more. Backs every risky verdict with a real Groth16 zero-knowledge proof generated on-device in ~4.4 seconds. The agent receives a verified pass/fail — not your genotype. Keeps the human in charge. Every tool call from an external agent hits a consent gate (Allow once / Allow all session / Deny). Deny returns denied_by_patient and nothing is released. Every call lands in an on-page audit ledger. Visualizes the science. A built-in 3D viewer loads real protein structures (e.g. CYP2C19, PDB 4GQS). Every safety verdict now auto-renders its target enzyme in the viewport — and the agent can dock a drug into the catalytic pocket, zoom, highlight, and pin clinical annotations — live, from a chat prompt. The bigger picture: a pattern, not just a product. Everything above is shipped and working for pharmacogenomics — but the architecture itself is domain-agnostic by construction, and it has exactly three reusable layers: The tool boundary (WebMCP). The website advertises capabilities to any agent via document.modelContext.registerTool ; the agent never gets raw data access, only tool calls with typed schemas. The consent gate (human-in-the-loop). Every tool execution passes through a human decision point that cannot be prompt-injected around, with machine-readable denial ( denied_by_patient ). The zero-knowledge release. Verdicts leave the device only as Groth16 proofs — mathematical conclusions with zero source data. Swap "genome" for credentials, finances, or imaging and the same three layers hold. The genome demo is our proof that the pattern is real, not theoretical. How we built it The WebMCP layer. Each capability is registered in the standard form — document.modelContext.registerTool({ name, description, inputSchema, execute }) — and verified via getTools() with AbortController cleanup. Tool descriptions are written for agents , not humans: the discovery quality of the demo comes entirely from those description strings. For non-WebMCP browsers we ship an honestly-labeled local fallback, so judges on any browser still see the full pipeline. The zero-knowledge layer. The core claim — "this patient's CYP2C19 diplotype belongs to the poor-metabolizer set" — is compiled into a Circom circuit (8,845 constraints on the bn128 curve): Poseidon-hashed Merkle membership with depth $k = 16$, an EdDSA curator signature, and a single-use nullifier, all verified inside the SNARK: $$ \text{Verify}_{vk}(\pi_a, \pi_b, \pi_c;\; \text{root},\, \text{claim},\, \text{nullifier}) \Rightarrow 1 $$ Secrets are derived at parse time from the patient's actual file — never hardcoded: $$ s = \text{sha256}(\text{gene} \,|\, \text{genotypes} \,|\, \text{salt}) $$ $$ \text{nullifier} = \text{Poseidon}(s, \text{nonce}) $$ The nullifier makes every proof single-use, so a leaked proof can't be replayed by an attacker — double-spend protection, but for DNA claims. Groth16 proving runs in-browser via snarkjs (~4.4 s for witness + proof). Because witnesses are generated from the real file, a false phenotype fails witness generation — we ship a "Forgery Audit" button so anyone can watch the circuit refuse a forged claim in real time. The trust UX. A consent wrapper intercepts every execute() . External agent calls block until a human decides; the 3D viewport, HUD, and audit ledger visualize what the agent is doing as it does it. One click runs a built-in "Full Agent Demo" where the AI self-asks six questions and executes the full pipeline — our insurance policy for any demo environment. Challenges we ran into Two parse paths, one engine. Our file-upload and inline-text parsers initially kept separate state, so the ZK engine would report "not ready" after one path but not the other. We only caught it through end-to-end agent testing — the fix was unifying both paths into a single claim-tree build. Lesson: in agent-driven apps, every entry point must reach the same invariants. Making Groth16 feel instant. A ~4.4-second proving pause is an eternity in a demo. We restructured the UI so the wait is part of the show — the HUD narrates witness generation, proving, and verification step by step, turning dead time into credibility. Blocking an agent with a human. Agents expect fast async answers; our consent gate intentionally makes them wait for a human click. Wiring that without deadlocking the tool promise — and returning a machine-readable denied_by_patient the agent can respect — took several iterations. The honesty tax. SNP arrays can't see CYP2D6 copy-number variants, HLA alleles beyond our panel, or UGT1A1. We could have hidden those gaps; instead every limitation is stated in-UI, in tool responses, and in the README. It cost us "it does everything" bravado and bought us something better: judges believing everything else we claim. Accomplishments that we're proud of Nothing is mocked. Real Groth16 proofs, independent on-device verification, replay rejection, forged-claim rejection — all running in a browser tab with zero backend. Our automated suite passes 32/32 end-to-end (parse → phenotype → secrets → Merkle trees → prove → verify → replay-reject → forgery-reject). A real agent really did it. In our final integration test, a WebMCP-connected AI discovered all tools unprompted and executed the full chain — parse a genuine 643,163-position genome, assess Plavix, recommend an alternative, generate and verify a Groth16 proof — without ever receiving the genome. We made refusal a feature. Watching the circuit reject a forged phenotype, or an agent accept denied_by_patient , communicates our values better than any slide. What we learned With WebMCP, descriptions are the API. Agents choose tools based almost entirely on description quality. Writing for the model — not the user — was the highest-leverage engineering we did. Client-side ZK is production-plausible today. Seconds, not minutes, on commodity laptops, with snarkjs and careful circuit design. The "too slow for the browser" excuse expired. Policy belongs at the tool boundary, not the prompt. Prompt-level safety evaporates with a clever prompt; a consent gate inside execute() cannot be talked around. This, we think, is a pattern every WebMCP app should copy. Design for the agent's blind spots. In one real integration session an agent did everything we asked but never touched the 3D tools — because nothing told it they mattered. We now return next hints in every tool response and auto-render the enzyme on every verdict: the wow no longer depends on the agent's improvisation. Trust compounds through admitted limits. The sections where we say "this cannot do X" are the ones that make judges trust the sections where we say "this provably does Y."
