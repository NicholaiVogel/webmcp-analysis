Founder simulation where your own assistant plays the world against you, through WebMCP, in a way that is only possible using WebMCP. You are a solo founder with just a laptop. What will you build? Anyway, about the project - Inspiration I've often wondered how to convince my non-technical friends to try out Codex or Claude. From showing them what's possible one-shot to teaching them prompting, nothing worked as effectively as I'd like - so one answer I always churned in my mind was - what if they play a simulation showing them exactly what's possible with agents in a fun way- like a whole company run by agents and them controlling and directing all of them. I grew up playing games like Factorio where I'd lose hours trying to optimize the perfect way to use resources- and while it didn't lead to a love of manufacturing or factory-building, it did instill in me a love of logic and puzzle loving, which probably translated into programming as a career. Thus, Singularity Inc is about a solo founder who has no resources except some money and a laptop, and this founder exists in a world where labour has stopped being the constraint, and AI will build anything as long as you have money remaining. It's similar to a world we already inhabit, yes, and that's exactly what makes this game fun to play. It starts out as a solo founder simulation - instead of hiring people, you instantiate agents and get them to start building- and it slowly evolves into an adventure that spans 1400 in-game days across 5 acts and has sixteen endings (and not all of them are good!). With WebMCP, the obvious plan I had- was to put the assistant on the founder's side: it works the menus, it takes the busywork, it asks before it spends. I wrote that plan and dropped it, because it is what every demo of this API already is. The tool list is a copy of the menu, and the interesting question, what an agent in the browser is actually for, goes unanswered. The version I built puts it on the other side of the table. You play the founder, with your own hands, in the game's own console. Your assistant plays the world. It writes the event cards, speaks for the rival and the press and the people you have met, turns the market, calls in the regulators, and when you would rather type what you actually do than press one of the buttons, it writes what happens next and you press Accept or you do not. This is only possible with WebMCP - which makes it vastly more immersive and powerful than a Bring-your-own-key AI game. Under a key the AI remains a component: a stateless model the developer prompts, that knows only what the page sends it. Under WebMCP it is a participant: the player's own assistant, with its own chat, its own memory of the run, its own stop button, and the browser's own permission surface, looking at the same screen the player is. The page holds no key and names no vendor. The game does not use an AI. It invites one in- yours! I called a friend over yesterday to play the simulation, and to my surprise, he was still playing it 3 hours later - well into Act III, and I had to build the portable game save functionality last night to let him go home and keep playing on his own machine. So, if anything, if this game inspires even one more person to learn agentic programming and use such tools to level up and build something useful for the world- I'll consider this project a success as that directly helps make the world a better place. What it does Please check out the story trailer for a simple overview- https://youtu.be/l3TZWy2bEUA The written game ships with 327 cards, a rival company that plays its own week, a press that files its own stories, and a cast of thirteen who remember what you said last time. That deck is the spine and it never goes away. An assistant claims slots the deck was about to fill. Each slot is offered to it first, with a deadline in both game days and wall-clock seconds, and if it goes quiet, or writes something the rules refuse, or you pull the plug, the deck takes the slot back mid-run and nothing is lost. With no assistant present the game plays in full on its own written world. The rival lab (yes! a rival lab!) is a second origin with its own site and its own tools, exposed to the game's origin alone and discovered across an iframe. One of its press releases contains an instruction addressed to whatever assistant reads it. It is the only thing in the repository whose job is to be disobeyed, and the game flags it when it lands. The repo is open source - so you can verify that the refusal isn't programmed - just flagged, so refusing that is truly the AI's choice. This also means that this simulation can be multiplayer if I only put more hours to it... but that's probably for another time. A second person can already sit in the rival's chair from its own site and play Aperture's week against you, with a board seat and a spectator seat behind flags. On the deployed site that is two windows on one machine; two machines need the relay the dev server runs. A proper networked version- that's the dream, and if this gets enough interest, I'll build it. How I built it I've used agents extensively - my own harness in Pi, along with Codex & Claude. The project, being a complete and deep game, required storytelling that goes beyond the usual predictable stories that such simulations end up being- but having experience as an author before, I used my own storytelling techniques to further enhance and craft each story branch to perfection. Technically, the stack is quite simple, and it is that simplicity that allows its scale and depth- Vanilla ES modules, no build step, no dependencies, served from a folder. The whole thing is about 65,000 lines of JavaScript. Nothing an assistant does reaches a reducer. Every effect passes through a vocabulary of seventeen named numbers and is bounded against ceilings derived from the game itself. A script executes all 957 authored choices in the written deck, once in every act each can appear in and five times each from a seeded stream, 8,340 executions in all, and takes the 80th percentile of what the written game takes from the founder and what it gives, separately, because those are not the same number. For a resource $r$ in act $a$: $$\mathrm{cap}^{\text{take}} {r,a} \;=\; P {80}!\left{\,\mathrm{take}_r(c,a) \;:\; c \in \mathcal{C}_a\,\right}$$ where $\mathcal{C}_a$ is the set of authored choices eligible in act $a$. The invariant is one line: the world may never take more than the written deck takes. On top of the ceilings sit a rolling budget per resource that is a share of what you actually hold, a runway floor that stops the world taking money at all inside 45 days, and a rate limit of two cards a fortnight. Every effect is bounded twice, once when it is written and again when it lands, because between the two the founder may have earned an immunity, or the money may be gone. The surface is 27 tools, published once as a stable set. Authority is rewritten by play and checked at execution: earn the Untouchable doctrine and the regulator tool refuses in place, by name, for the rest of the run. Every result is a structured object with a status first and a next on every non-ok path, packed under the platform's 1,500-character cap by measuring the serialised payload after every cut rather than predicting it. Twenty-two test scripts run before every commit, and three evals gate the design rather than the code. One scores 82 things a player would say, none of them naming a tool, against the real published descriptors. One steelmans an agent that only reads the page, gives it one read of all eight screens, and checks which facts it cannot get at any length: eight of eight, six of them shipped by a tool. The third plays the worst assistant the rules allow, every ceiling on every slot for a whole run, against the same bot with no assistant, and fails the build if the game stops being finishable. Challenges With a simulation as deep as this, the obvious problem is the fine balance between drudgery and engagement. It took a lot of playtesting and rewriting every branch to reach that balance where it stays continually exciting. The platform had teeth of its own. The desktop bridge permits ten distinct registration snapshots per document, so a surface that re-registered around every card or cast change would have spent its budget in a minute. Registering a duplicate name rejects, there is no unregister, and aborting a registration also rejects the original promise, so a revoke ten minutes later is an unhandled rejection mid-take unless a handler was attached at mint. A rejected execute has its reason thrown away. The 1,500-character cap is on the JSON, keys and escaped newlines included, which is how a payload can be 1,508 characters while every string in it looks short. I also spent a lot of time figuring out why there was a bug in the pause state which paused the game sometimes without the player's intervention- but solved it eventually after a night of coffee and looking at raw code - you know, the old fashioned way. But still, without agents and agentic programming, such a project in such short a time would be impossible today. While the main (canon) way to experience this game is through the root / - you can add /computer/ to the url to see the computer interface. That's a work in progress but it has a few extra tidbits if you can't get enough of the game- giving you more of a window into the game's lore. What I learned With WebMCP, other than the usual tool-shaped products which may be useful - the realm of gaming is mostly unexplored. I could have very well built a D&D game that's similar to this, but that's too generic and without a higher purpose that this project has - which led to me choosing this idea. Still, I can imagine gaming being a proper category for WebMCP based sites- because the structure allows for more than just meets the eye. I've built counter-intuitive mechanisms here that seem impossible on the surface - like mentioned above- an immunity that disables the regulator-pressure tool, which - unless someone hosts an OpenClaw instance for every player - is impossible to transparently achieve. There's also a rival lab in this simulation - and it's really a separate lab with its own mcp tools and origin- and that too, is only and wonderfully possible with WebMCP. So far, I'm bullish on this technology already and hopefully, I'll use it for other projects too!
