Public reporting often begins and ends with the most visible elements of these activities: a post, advert, account, headline, campaign, or a platform takedown. But the less visible enabling relationships remain scattered across datasets, platform disclosures, investigative reporting, archives, registries, and infrastructure records. I had already been imagining a larger online observatory project that could connect these traces, but the WebMCP Challenge supplied the missing interaction model. Rather than placing a chatbot beside a static dashboard, I wanted a researcher and an AI agent to work on the same inspectable terrain. That way the agent could search, organise, follow relationships, and surface current reporting, while the human researcher retained control over the provenance, uncertainty, and interpretation of evidence, and what deserved to enter the research record. This Hyphosphere project grew from that junction of needs and observations. It is a proof of concept for the thesis, as well as an experiment in research methods: a way to make the enabling ecommerce assemblage visible, without treating every single suggestive pattern as a conclusion. It helps to prevent premature closure on a messy and interesting problem. What it does Hyphosphere presents a human–agent research environment for tracing the commercial infrastructure behind online information operations. A visitor can: follow a reproducible investigation across cases, services, datasets, and reporting; distinguish verified, supported, inferred, and disputed relationships; inspect each research artifact’s provenance, original citation, reason for inclusion, and ecommerce-stack position; explore six layers of the enabling commercial stack; search curated records and linked source collections; ask an agent to assemble a compact, visible research brief; receive current, directly linked reporting through an agent-mediated signal feed; pause the feed, inspect citations, and retain selected leads; save discoveries and agent briefs in a device-local notebook; export retained research notes in Markdown; and preview possible outputs such as timelines, cluster summaries, micro case studies, and network visualisations. The project is deliberately not just a dashboard of troll posts. It begins with what is publicly visible, but then it helps the researcher examine the wider field of services, platforms, data, interfaces, and infrastructure that may enable an operation. How I built it The application was built as a responsive React research console and iterated with Codex. Its data model connects research objects through typed relationships, while preserving separate evidence states and six ecommerce-stack levels. A deterministic demonstration corpus supports the principal Atlas Relay investigation, so that judges can reproduce the same path. The page registers twelve WebMCP tools through document.modelContext.registerTool(...) . These tools can: follow a relationship; open supporting evidence; change the evidence threshold; reveal the source terrain; save a discovery; draft a possible research output; list source collections; search curated and linked sources; assemble a research brief; save an agent brief; publish current signals; and save an individual signal to the notebook. Human interface controls and WebMCP tools call on the same underlying React actions. This means an agent does not operate an invisible parallel system: its actions produce visible changes in the interface that the researcher can inspect and guide. The live signal feed is also agent-mediated rather than presented as an autonomous page crawler. A connected agent finds current material relevant to the research topic, as defined by the researcher, and supplies constrained, HTTPS-linked records through publish_signal_feed . The page then validates and displays those records with labels for source, relevance, information-warfare, and ecommerce-stack. The prototype notebook uses browser-local storage, with Markdown export providing a portable research trail. These are materials a researcher may later cite and repurpose in their work. Challenges I ran into The central challenge was balancing agent initiative with evidentiary caution. If automation silently produced a polished conclusion, it would undermine the very research method the application was intended to support. I therefore made agent actions visible, preserved citations, labelled with levels of uncertainty, and left final decisions about retention and interpretation with the researcher. A second challenge was how to use this to communicate a complex research idea to people who might be unfamiliar with information operations or online commercial infrastructure. The interface needed to explain the research problem, but without becoming an extended essay. Stable tabs, progressive disclosure, a guided investigation, interactive stack levels, and recurring visual cues helped make the concept navigable. I also encountered practical interaction problems: controls that changed content outside the viewport, competing menus, inactive-looking buttons, responsive layouts that concealed summoned material, and panels that obstructed the evidence beneath them. Repeated browser walkthroughs helped to consolidate the navigation and make interactions produce visible, comprehensible responses. Finally, WebMCP’s emerging implementation required careful debugging. Tool registration initially produced promise rejections until execution handlers were placed correctly inside each tool definition. The repaired implementation now exposes and executes all twelve tools. Accomplishments that I'm proud of Twelve functioning WebMCP tools operate a meaningful shared page state. Agent actions are visible and inspectable rather than hidden behind a chat response. A reproducible investigation, and an agent-mediated current-research path, co-exist without being confused. External leads retain direct citations, provenance, stack positions, and reasons for inclusion. The notebook brings discoveries, research briefs, and current signals together. The six-level stack model makes largely invisible enabling infrastructure easier to understand. The interface gives newcomers several ways to explore the topic, without forcing any pre-determined conclusions. The project has developed a distinctive visual identity based on branching relationships, layered infrastructure, and colourful research artifacts. What I learned Building the instrument has become part of the research discovery for the wider project. Designing the WebMCP actions revealed where agent assistance is genuinely valuable: searching, structuring, comparing, labelling, and bringing scattered material into a common field. It also clarified where human judgement must absolutely remain explicit: assessing provenance, interpreting relationships, recognising context, and deciding whether a lead actually belongs in the research record. I learned that an agent-native interface needs more than just exposed functions. Tool descriptions, input schemas, visible state changes, reversibility, and clear feedback, all shape whether people and agents can work together successfully. Most importantly, the project demonstrated that the commercial infrastructure surrounding information operations can be made more legible without reducing uncertainty or turning a recurring pattern into an automatic verdict. What's next for Weaponised Ecommerce: The Hyphosphere Observatory This challenge entry is the first glimpse of a much larger online observatory. Future development will include: a production database for durable research records; authenticated and collaborative researcher notebooks; automated ingestion from suitable public datasets and archives; a larger reusable collection of case studies; researcher-contributed records and annotations; richer temporal and geographic analysis across local, regional, and transnational relationships; tools for observing how actors and assemblages change over time; expanded network, timeline, and stack visualisations; stronger WebMCP evaluations and security annotations; and carefully governed access to private or sensitive research collections. The long-term aim is to create an observatory where people and agents can gather heterogeneous evidence, test relationships, compare cases, and produce reusable research notes, all while keeping provenance, uncertainty, and human agency central to the process.
